Historical Context

Work on the First Emperor’s tomb began soon after he became king of Qin in 246 B.C.E. and continued after his death in 210 B.C.E. The burial mound near Mount Li, east of modern Xi’an, has never been opened. In 1974 farmers digging a well found the first of several pits about 1.5 kilometers east of the mound, filled with life-size terracotta soldiers and horses. Archaeologists estimate that the three main pits held about 8,000 figures, along with real bronze weapons. Other pits around the mound held figures of officials, entertainers, and animals, and two half-size bronze chariots.

The figures were made in workshops that combined standard molded parts—bodies, legs, heads, and hands—and then finished each figure by hand, so that faces and hairstyles vary. Stamped or incised marks on many pieces name workshops or foremen. The pits were damaged by fire and collapse soon after the Qin fell, and the figures have been restored from fragments. The site is also known from the account by the Han historian Sima Qian, translated below, which describes the underground palace inside the mound. Parts of his account cannot yet be checked, although soil samples from the mound show high levels of mercury.

Editorial note. The photographs show the site as excavated and displayed today: the hangar-like roof over Pit 1, museum lighting, and visitors are modern. The kneeling archer and the bronze chariot are shown in museum displays at the site. Sima Qian’s description was translated for this reader from the Chinese text of the Shiji, chapter 6 (entry for 210 B.C.E.).

Document Text

Sima Qian, Shiji, chapter 6: The burial of the First Emperor (210 B.C.E.)

In the ninth month, the First Emperor was buried at Mount Li. When the First Emperor first came to the throne, work began on digging and shaping Mount Li. After he united the world, more than 700,000 convict laborers were sent there from all over the realm. They dug down through three layers of groundwater, poured in bronze, and set the outer coffin in place. Replicas of palaces, pavilions, and the hundred offices, together with rare vessels, precious objects, and wonders, were brought in to fill it. Craftsmen were ordered to make crossbows and arrows rigged to shoot anyone who tunneled in. Mercury was used to make the hundred rivers, the Yellow River and the Yangzi, and the great sea, with machinery to make it flow from one to another. Above were set out the patterns of the heavens, and below the shape of the earth. Candles were made of the fat of “man-fish,” calculated to burn for a long time without going out.

The Second Emperor said, “It is not fitting that those of the late emperor’s palace women who had no sons should be sent away.” He ordered them all to follow the emperor in death, and those who died were very many. After the burial was complete, someone pointed out that the craftsmen who had built the mechanisms knew all about the treasure, and that knowledge of the treasure would leak out. When the great ceremony was over and the treasure had been sealed away, the inner passage was closed and the outer gate of the passage was lowered, shutting in all the craftsmen and workers who had hidden the treasure, so that none came out again. Trees and grass were planted over the tomb so that it looked like a hill.
