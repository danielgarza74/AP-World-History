Selection Overview

Stark, a sociologist, uses quantitative measures for the 22 largest Greco-Roman cities around 100 C.E. to ask where Christianity spread first. He finds that cities closer to Jerusalem, larger cities, and cities with Jewish diaspora communities gained churches earliest, arguing that travel, trade, and "networks of interpersonal relationships based on kinship, friendship, or commerce" prepared the way for Christian missionaries; he also argues that Gnosticism arose as a Christian rather than a Jewish heresy.

78 SOCIOLOGICAL ANALYSIS SELECTING CITIES BY SIZE Despite libraries stuffed with books on many Greco-Roman cities, the sad fact is that, as Lewis Mumford (1974) has pointed out, "the city itself remains a shadow." 1 Indeed, it has required Herculean efforts even to estimate such an elementary and essential fact as the population of these cities. Fortunately, Tertius Chandler made it his life's work to try to discover the populations of ancient cities and, assisted by Gerald Fox (with Kinglsey Davis performing the vital role of midwife), his extraordinary work was finally published. In 3000 Years of Urban Growth, Chandler and Fox offer a plausible and well-documented basis for estimating the populations of the largest cities in the world in 100 C.E. Among these are 20 Greco-Roman cities. However, because Chandler and Fox chose to list only those cities having a population of 40,000 or more, they provided no population estimates for Athens or Salamis, although these are usually included in lists of important Greco-Roman cities. I added these two cities, bringing the total to 22. However, I was unable to muster any Chandler-like scholarship to determine their populations at this time. But, after a good deal of poking around, I settled on 35,000 for Salamis and 30,000 for Athens.2 If these prove faulty, removing them from the analysis has no effect on the results I report below. For example, Numa Denis Fustel de Coulanges's classic work The Ancient City, published in 1864 (and suggested to me as an exception to this claim), discusses nothing but the culture and customs of Greco-Roman times, and his title simply means that the setting was urban. The city per se could have well have been a figment of imagination, for there is nary a word about streets, sewers, plumbing, water supplies, buildings, industry, markets, ethnic enclaves, crime, garbage, beggars, or any of the realities of urban life. In fact, the word "house" appears only in several passing references, and no houses are described. Or, consider a modern work with nearly the same title, The City in the Ancient World, by Mason Hammond (1972), and published as part of the Harvard Studies in Urban History. This volume has an excellent index. Under the topic of Roman citizenship, the index lists 24 page references. Entries also appear for Romulus, Trojan war, Borneo, and even Pleistocene period. But not one of the following words occurs in this index: aqueduct, bath, baths, bathing, build, building, crime, disease, death, environment, epidemic, ethnic, food, fuel, garbage, house, housing, homes, manure, pipes, plague, plumbing, privy, sewer, street, waste, or water. The omission of these index listings reflects the fact that this book, too, is about culture and about political and military history and is not about cities at all.
