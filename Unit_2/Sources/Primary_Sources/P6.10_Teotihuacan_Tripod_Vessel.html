A Teotihuacan Tripod Vessel
350–450 C.E.
 
TOC FIT Civilizations of Mesoamerica: Teotihuacan and the wider region (textbook pp. 237–241).
WHY HERE One kind of object, made at Teotihuacan and imitated elsewhere, turns up from central Mexico to the Maya lowlands: evidence of exchange and influence within Mesoamerica.
TREATMENT Open photographs of the same object (The Met Open Access, CC0); context paraphrased from the museum’s object description.

Historical Context

This cylindrical ceramic vessel on three short feet is a Teotihuacan tripod vessel, a form made in many sizes and finishes in the city and widely traded or copied outside it. The Metropolitan Museum of Art dates this example to 350–450 C.E. According to the museum’s description, the same complex design is repeated four times around the outside: a feathered medallion framed by a large feathered border that resembles a headdress shown on high-ranking figures in Teotihuacan art, with tassels and a symbol often called the “reptile-eye.” The potter cut the design into a polished dark surface so that it stands out against a background rubbed with red ochre, a slow technique that was later replaced by faster stuccoed and painted decoration.

The museum notes that tripod vessels of this kind are found elsewhere in Mexico and even in the Maya lowlands, where cities claimed political or commercial connections to the distant metropolis, and that local potters imitated them. A single object does not tell us how a vessel reached a distant place: it could have been carried by traders or officials, been a gift, or been copied from a model. Its value here is as one concrete example of a pattern that other evidence, such as the Maya texts that mention Teotihuacan-linked power, helps interpret.

The vessel is reported to come from a burial site in the Basin of Mexico, Santiago Ahuizotla, that was excavated in the 1940s by local inhabitants rather than by archaeologists, so its findspot is a report, not a documented excavation context.
