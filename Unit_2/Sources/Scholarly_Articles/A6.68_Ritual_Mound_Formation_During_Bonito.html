Selection Overview

Wills questions the now-common view that the great earthen mounds beside Chaco Canyon's great houses were sacred platforms built for pilgrim gatherings and feasts. Re-examining the Pueblo Alto and Pueblo Bonito mound evidence, he argues that they grew mainly from construction debris, site location, and length of occupation, and that they "do not offer clear evidence for either periodic ceremonies or intentional design," though he grants that people used them for small-scale ritual.

Much of the scholarly work on Chaco dur- ing the past decade has emphasized a metaphorical or symbolic role for Bonito-phase architecture, in which buildings and other constructions are inter- preted as materialization of ideological or cosmo- logical frameworks (Kantner 1997; Sebastian 1992; Stein and Lekson 1992; Stein et al. 1996). In this explicitly functional perspective, Bonito-phase archi- tecture was created to promote the political strate- gies of ritual leaders by demonstrating their ability to attract followers, or to provide a sanctified place that facilitated pan-regional social integration, or both. I believe these assumptions are unjustified as an explanation for mound origins. In the following dis- cussion I argue that the evidence for ritual convoca- tions and intentional destruction of ceramic vessels in the Alto mound is extremely circumstantial, and that stratigraphic data point more convincingly to construction activity as the most important of sev- eral formation processes accounting for mound growth. The same argument can be made for the Pueblo Bonito mounds. However, I do not propose that the mounds were unrelated to ritual, but that their relationship was mostly an indirect one through the construction of great houses. Some aspects of Bonito-phase ritual can be found in the archaeology of Chaco mounds, but it is unlikely that these fea- tures represent intentionally designed sacred archi- tecture, or that they contain material evidence for political strategies. Background Pueblo Alto is a great house of 133 rooms located on the mesa top north of Chaco Canyon, approxi- mately one kilometer from Pueblo Bonito and other great houses in the canyon 50 m below (Figure 1). Built and occupied between ca. A.D. 1020 and 1120, Pueblo Alto was part of a canyon-wide building boom in the late eleventh century that involved mas- sive construction projects designed and executed according to prepared ground plans (Dean and War- ren 1983; Lekson 1981, 1984; Vivian 1990; Windes 1987a). The architectural portion of Pueblo Alto cov- ers eight hectares; adjoining walled plazas encom- pass a similar area. Fourteen rooms and a variety of sampling units were excavated from 1976 to 1978 as part of the Chaco Project, a joint National Park Service-University of New Mexico investigation.
