The Satapatha Brahmana: The Sacred Fire Carried East, and the Burial Mounds of the “Easterns”
composed in Sanskrit by Brahmin ritual schools over a long period, often placed between the 8th and 6th centuries B.C.E. (date debated; transmitted orally)
 
TOC FIT Cultural Traditions of Classical India: From Ritual Sacrifice to Philosophical Speculation; The Buddhist Challenge (textbook pp. 149–154).
WHY HERE A Brahmin ritual text’s own story of how the sacred fire, and the Brahmins who tend it, reached the eastern Ganges lands where the Buddha later taught, and its rule about how “the Easterns” bury their dead.
TREATMENT Public-domain translation (Eggeling, Sacred Books of the East 12, 1882, and 44, 1900), two passages: Satapatha Brahmana 1.4.1.10–17 in full and 13.8.1.5 in full, with the opening words of 13.8.1.1. Later written record of oral ritual teaching.

Historical Context

The Brahmanas are prose manuals attached to the Vedas that explain the meaning of each verse and act of the Vedic sacrifice. The Satapatha Brahmana (“Brahmana of a Hundred Paths”), the longest of them, belongs to the White Yajurveda. It was composed and memorized in Brahmin schools over a long period; scholars often place it between the 8th and 6th centuries B.C.E., but its date is debated, and its later books may be younger than its first. The first passage interrupts an explanation of the kindling verses with a legend: Mâthava, king of Videgha (Videha, north of the Ganges in what is now Bihar), carries the fire god Agni Vaisvânara in his mouth; the fire escapes at the Sarasvatî River in the west and burns its way east until it stops at the Sadânîrâ, which Eggeling thought was most probably the Gandak River, the boundary between the kingdoms of Kosala and Videha. The second passage comes from the funeral rites in Book 13 and gives the required shape of a burial mound.

Read the legend as the Brahmins’ memory of their own spread, not as a record of events. Eggeling’s note, following the scholar Albrecht Weber, takes the fire as a figure for Brahmin worship and settlement moving east. What the text itself says is that Brahmins once would not cross the Sadânîrâ, that the land beyond it was “very uncultivated, very marshy” until Brahmins brought it into the sacrifice, and that “now-a-days” many Brahmins live there. In the funeral passage, “the Easterns and others,” called people of “the Asura nature” (the Asuras are the enemies of the gods), build round mounds instead of four-cornered ones. These are a Brahmin author’s judgments about outsiders. They show that Brahmins saw the eastern Ganges lands, beside the kingdom of Magadha where the Buddha later taught, as late to enter their ritual world and as home to people with other customs. The text never mentions the Buddha, and it does not say what the easterners themselves believed; some historians link the round mounds to the round Buddhist stupa, but the text makes no such link.

Editorial note. The text is Julius Eggeling’s translation in The Satapatha-Brâhmana, part 1 (Sacred Books of the East, vol. 12, Oxford, 1882) and part 5 (Sacred Books of the East, vol. 44, Oxford, 1900), as transcribed by the Internet Sacred Text Archive. Section numbers (1.4.1.10 is book 1, chapter 4, brahmana 1, section 10) are given in bold; the online transcription runs each number together with a repeat of the section number (“1:4:1:1010”), which is corrected. Eggeling’s printed page numbers are marked [p. 105] and so on where each page begins. His footnote numbers and notes are removed; his parentheses, which mark words supplied for sense, his double hyphens, and his spellings and accents (Mâthava, Brâhmans, Sadânîrâ, Pragâpati) are kept. Omitted: the rest of 1.4.1, which explains the kindling verses (sections 18–19, before the explanation resumes, have the priest ask the king why he did not answer); the rest of 13.8.1.1, a play on the word smasâna (burial-place), marked [. . .]; and sections 2–4 and 6–20 of 13.8.1, on the timing, site, and building of the mound.
Document Text

Book 1, chapter 4, brahmana 1, sections 10–17 (Mâthava the Videgha carries Agni east)

1.4.1.10 [p. 104] ‘With buttered (spoon)--’ he adds. Now Mâthava, the (king of) Videgha, carried Agni Vaisvânara in his mouth. The Rishi Gotama Râhûgana was his family priest. When addressed (by the latter), he made no answer to him, fearing lest Agni might fall from his mouth.

1.4.1.11 He (the priest) began to invoke the latter with [p. 105] verses of the Rig-veda, ‘We kindle thee at the sacrifice, O wise Agni, thee the radiant, the mighty caller to the sacrificial feast (Rig-veda V, 26, 3)!--O Videgha!’

1.4.1.12 He (the king) did not answer. (The priest went on), ‘Upwards, O Agni, dart thy brilliant, shining rays, thy flames, thy beams (Rig-veda VIII, 44, 16)!--O Videgha-a-a!’

1.4.1.13 Still he did not answer. (The priest continued), ‘Thee, O butter-sprinkled one, we invoke! (Rig-veda V, 26, 2);’ so much he uttered, when at the very mentioning of butter, Agni Vaisvânara flashed forth from the (king’s) mouth: he was unable to hold him back; he issued from his mouth, and fell down on this earth.

1.4.1.14 Mâthava, the Videgha, was at that time on the (river) Sarasvatî. He (Agni) thence went burning along this earth towards the east; and Gotama Râhûgana and the Videgha Mâthava followed after him as he was burning along. He burnt over (dried up) all these rivers. Now that (river), which is called ‘Sadânîrâ,’ flows from the northern (Himâlaya) mountain: that one he did not burn over. That one the Brâhmans did not cross in former times, thinking, ‘it has not been burnt over by Agni Vaisvânara.’

1.4.1.15 Now-a-days, however, there are many Brâhmans to the east of it. At that time it (the land east of the Sadânîrâ) was very uncultivated, very marshy, because it had not been tasted by Agni Vaisvânara.

1.4.1.16 Now-a-days, however, it is very cultivated, for the Brâhmans have caused (Agni) to taste it through [p. 106] sacrifices. Even in late summer that (river), as it were, rages along: so cold is it, not having been burnt over by Agni Vaisvânara.

1.4.1.17 Mâthava, the Videgha, then said (to Agni), ‘Where am I to abide?’ ‘To the east of this (river) be thy abode!’ said he. Even now this (river) forms the boundary of the Kosalas and Videhas; for these are the Mâthavas (or descendants of Mâthava).

Book 13, chapter 8, brahmana 1, sections 1 and 5 (funeral ceremonies: the shape of the burial mound)

13.8.1.1 [p. 421] They now do what is auspicious for him. They now prepare a burial-place (smasâna) for him, [p. 422] (to serve him) either as a house or as a monument;

[. . .]

13.8.1.5 [p. 423] Four-cornered (is the sepulchral mound). Now the gods and the Asuras, both of them sprung from Pragâpati, were contending in the (four) regions (quarters). The gods drove out the Asuras, their rivals and enemies, from the regions, and, being regionless, they were overcome. Wherefore the people who are godly make their burial-places four-cornered, whilst those who are of the Asura nature, the Easterns and others, (make them) [p. 424] round, for they (the gods) drove them out from the regions. He arranges it so as to lie between the two regions, the eastern and the southern, for in that region assuredly is the door to the world of the Fathers: through the above he thus causes him to enter the world of the Fathers; and by means of the (four) corners he (the deceased) establishes himself in the regions, and by means of the other body (of the tomb) in the intermediate regions: he thus establishes him in all the regions.
