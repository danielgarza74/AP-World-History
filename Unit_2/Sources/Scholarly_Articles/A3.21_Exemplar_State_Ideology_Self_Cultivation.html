Selection Overview

Holcombe argues that in Eastern Jin south China (317–420), after the fall of the north, a refugee court and aristocracy that "lacked the material basis for power" relied on ideology, so that aristocrats won influence by ostentatiously withdrawing from office and cultivating a transcendent reputation. He shows that even a military strongman like Liu Yu had to present his rise as ideologically correct and reluctant before taking the throne, because "fourth-century ideology would only permit leaders who claimed to be uninterested in leadership."

94 CHARLES HOLCOMBE were striving for something far more noble than mere temporal power.2 It is true that conventional Chinese scholarship is apt to charac- terize reclusion, whenever it occurs, as a form of Confucian political protest. Donald Holzman, for example, observes that Chinese litera- ry critics almost unanimously insist that the ballads of transcendence written by Ts'ao Chih (192-232) should be read as laments to the failure of his political career, rather than the exultation of empyreal triumph and freedom from worldly cares they appear to be on the surface.3 This interpretation, moreover, cannot lightly be dismissed, even though it sometimes makes heroic strains on credulity, because of the very real influence of the "Li-sao" poetic tradition in China. In the case of the Six Dynasties period, however, this approach may often simply be misleading. The medieval Chinese vogue for reclusion did spring out of political protest, at the end of the Han, but it quickly evolved into something more compelling-something valued for its own sake. The many evocations of reclusion and immortality in Six Dynasties China should not usually be construed as mere conventional political protest: they represent the highest ideals of the time, and avenues to power in their own right. After the fall of the Han, the highest social aspiration ceased to be participation in the formal government. Instead, a transcendent posture itself vested men, in contemporary eyes, with powers of another kind, beyond those available to the state. In their imagina- tion the self-realized cognoscenti of fourth-century China believed themselves capable of superhuman feats, or at least spoke metaphor- ically of such powers; and, if this was clearly delusion, their posture really did hold the power to mobilize society through the psycholog- ical levers of opinion.
