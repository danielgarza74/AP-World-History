Selection Overview

Toll traces how pottery was made and moved in the Chaco world of the tenth through twelfth centuries, showing that outlying areas such as the Chuska Valley sent large quantities of everyday vessels more than 60 km to Chaco Canyon and that small sites and great houses shared the same assemblages. He reads the layered trash mounds at great houses as residue of periodic gatherings, possibly devotional, and argues that a region this large "had to have operated on a primarily voluntary rather than coercive basis."

By the time g houses were being built in the Chaco sphere, four wares were in use and some areas were supplying large quantities of pots to the sites in Chaco Canyon. Widespread stylistic trends indicate mechanisms of communication and shared ideals over a huge area. Ceramics are an index of how large quantities of everyday items were transported more than 60 km to Chaco. The simi- larity of source andform assemblages at small sites and great houses demonstrates the close link between the two kinds of sites. The scale and layout of great houses and layering in great mounds associated with them are suggestive of periodic events that are components of Renfrew's model of central Chaco as the location of devotional events. Scrutiny of layer contents indicates that such events were variable, and that lithics and ceramics from a single geographic source seem to be independently trans- ported. Different areas may have been represented differently at different events, but layer-s always retain some of their com- plex composition of sources. Starting from a sin- gle ware, pottery became differentiated in paste, fin- ish, and decoration. Because of our interest in events in and around Chaco, the focal period here is the tenth through twelfth centuries A.D. By the 900s four wares were being made. The production of these wares over- laps spatially, but not equally. That is, there are areas where more than one of these wares was produced, but few or none where all were produced. Gray and white wares are by far the most abundant in the Chaco area, and are products of similar raw materials, prepa- ration, and firing. The spatial and temporal overlap in production between these two wares is quite com- plete, although there were probably areas where one or the other was made in greater quantity. In ascend- ing sheer numbers of sherds around Chaco Canyon, pottery comprises polished smudged brown wares, red wares, white wares (Color Photo 6, p. 67), and gray wares. Color photos of examples of these wares are available in Peckham (1990) and in Hays-Gilpin and van Hartesveldt (1998). Smudged Bivown War-e These vessels were produced south of the Chaco area in the Mogollon Rim country (Cameron and Toll, this issue:Figure 1). They are technically brown ware, although most vessels found in Chaco are not brown. Most examples of this ware found in the San Juan Basin are striking-looking bowls with lustrous black, smudged interiors, and highly polished red to orange to tan exteriors. These wares were also made with corrugated exteriors, but this variety occurs much less often in Chaco than bowls with polished exteriors. The volcanic clays used to produce these pots are often self-tempered and oxidized red. While their status as brown ware perhaps has less cultural significance than geological significance (Wilson et al. 1996), brown wares have traditionally been asso- ciated with the Mogollon "culture." The main sig- nificance of these vessels in the San Juan Basin is as a trade ware rather than as a production element. They are, however, a constant presence in the ceramic assemblage, and the presence of highly fragmented bowls at the base of the Pueblo Alto mound suggest they had special significance and were intentionally destroyed (Toll and McKenna 1987:54, 178-182). Red Ware While smudged wares are attributable to one region, the production history of red pottery found in and near Chaco Canyon was more complicated. In suc- cessive periods red wares were produced in south- east Utah (the San Juan Red wares, 700s through 1000s), northeast Arizona (the Tsegi Orange wares, 1000s through 1200s, mostly late 1000s in Chaco Canyon), and in central western New Mexico and eastern Arizona (the White Mountain Red wares, 1000s through 1400s). As with smudged brown wares, red wares are always in the assemblages of both small and large San Juan Basin sites and always in small quantities. Although produced over a wide area over a long period, red wares follow a common pattern of red to orange field with black painted designs. They are usually well polished, and toward A.D. 1100 are increasingly decorated with white in addition to black or with black and red on orange, producing polychromes. Decorative motifs are sim- ilar to those on contemporary white wares, and fomis are the same as white ware forms as well. In Chaco most red ware is in the bowl form, although closed forms also occur. Whlite Ware White ware is the well-known decorated pottery of the prehistoric pueblos. These wares have fairly coarse pastes similar to utility wares until around the 800s. Pastes tend to become increasingly fine into the 1200s, although there is considerable variability. Surface treatments are also variable, but by the 900s most vessels are polished at least on the decorated face and are slipped. White ware vessels were used for food service (bowls and ladles), water storage (ollas and canteens), and combinations of storage and service (seed jars, jars, pitchers). Bowls are by far the most abundant white ware form. White wares and gray wares were made in most of the pueblo area through the 1300s, though pro- portions produced of each varied by area. In areas where red wares were made they replace some white wares in vessel assemblages, red and white ware forms being the same. After the Chaco period red and yellow wares increasingly replaced white wares. Although white wares were made in most areas, assemblages from almost any area will have at least a few vessels from other areas in almost any period. Decoration of white wares conformed to styles over a very large area so that it is usually possible to rec- ognize a vessel's period of manufacture across the Pueblo area.
