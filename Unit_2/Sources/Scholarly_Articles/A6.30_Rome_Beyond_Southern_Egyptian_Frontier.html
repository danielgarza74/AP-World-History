Selection Overview

Kirwan surveys Roman penetration south of Egypt's frontier from Augustus to Diocletian: Petronius's punitive campaign against Meroe in the 20s B.C.E., Nero's expedition that reached the Sudd marshes, and the three centuries of peace, diplomacy, and barter trade that followed, when Roman glass, bronze, and pottery filled the richer Meroitic tombs in exchange for ivory, skins, and slaves. He describes Meroe as "an African civilization strongly influenced by the traditions and religious beliefs of ancient Egypt" and suggests that Roman-era devices such as the saqya water-wheel had "a salutary effect on the meagre economy" of Nubia.

The story of Petronius' campaign is well known and is described by both Strabo (d. a.d. 24) and Pliny (d. a.d. 79). It need not concern us here except perhaps to lament that a possible Meroitic version of the story is still largely untranslatable7. The Meroites, ill-armed, ill-disciplined but far more numerous, were defeated in a series of battles, and by land and by river were driven southwards across the Roman frontier at Maharraqah. Petronius then advanced over the desert and captured Premnis or Primis (Qasr Ibrim), a natural hill fortress controlling the river and desert approaches, dating probably from Ptolemaic times. This the Romans later temporarily occupied as an advance post to the frontier. After the capture of Qasr Ibrim, Strabo and Pliny give differing versions of the route taken by the Romans, though both agree that they penetrated as far south as Napata (Jebel Barkal), an ancient religious centre near the Fourth Cataract. According to Strabo, Petronius, from Qasr Ibrim, struck out across the desert directly to Napata, joining probably the caravan route which runs from Korosko to Abu Hamed. Pliny, on the other hand, states that Petronius, after the capture of Ibrim, con? tinued further up the Nile capturing five other Meroitic cities, the southernmost of which stood "where the River Nile, as it thunders down the precipices, has quite deprived the inhabitants of the power of hearing." No one who has listened to the tumble and rush of the waters in the Second Cataract and southwards in the so- called Batn el-Hagar region (the "Belly of Rocks") can fail to recognize Pliny's description. Of the two versions, Pliny's in this context is to be preferred for south of Qasr Ibrim, but on the west bank of the river, below the Second Cataract, there are at Faras large cemeteries marking the site of perhaps the most important Meroitic city of Lower Nubia. This could scarcely have been allowed to escape the vengeance of the Roman arms.8 By the end of the first century b.c, Rome (in a military sense) had thus already penetrated far beyond the southern Egyptian frontier. By the middle of the first century a.d., she had advanced not only more deeply into "the dusky realms of Cephean Meroe" of which Propertius had sung, but even beyond the southern limits of the Meroitic Kingdom, into darkest Africa. Both Pliny and Seneca 9 have told the story of Nero's remarkable expedition, and Seneca?who may well have had a hand in the plans?interviewed the explorers on their return to Rome. The expedition was sent out by Nero in the autumn of the year 61 and consisted of a party of Praetorian soldiers under the command of a tribune and two centurions. Travelling, like Petronius, first up the Nile and then across the eastern desert, they reached Meroe nearly a thousand miles from the frontier. They saw parakeets, and monkeys, and the tracks of rhinoceros and elephant in the neighbourhood. They noted that here the grass was of a "greener and fresher colour" (probably after the rains). They observed too the small, thin trees of the incipient African forest.
