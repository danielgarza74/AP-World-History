The Great Stupa at Sanchi
brick core, 3rd century B.C.E. (tradition: Ashoka); enlarged in the 2nd–1st centuries B.C.E.; carved gateways, ca. 1st century B.C.E.–1st century C.E.
 
TOC FIT Cultural Traditions of Classical India: The Buddhist Challenge—stupas, monasteries, and Ashoka’s patronage (textbook pp. 152–153).
WHY HERE One of the best-preserved early Buddhist monuments: evidence of royal patronage and of gifts from donors of many kinds, of the cult of relics, and of how the Buddha is shown by symbols rather than as a human figure.
TREATMENT Open photographs of the monument and of two gateway reliefs (CC BY-SA 4.0 and CC BY 2.0); modern photographs of an ancient monument that was repaired in the 19th and 20th centuries.

Historical Context

A stupa is a solid dome of earth, brick, and stone built over relics of the Buddha or of a revered teacher; Buddhists honor it by walking around it clockwise. The Great Stupa (“Stupa 1”) stands on a hill about ten kilometers from the town of Vidisha in central India. Tradition credits the emperor Ashoka with starting Sanchi as a sacred place in the 3rd century B.C.E., and UNESCO’s description says that a brick stupa and an Ashokan pillar date from his time. The dome was later enlarged and faced with stone (during the Shunga dynasty, in the 2nd century B.C.E.), surrounded by a railing, and entered through four carved stone gateways (toranas) added around the first century B.C.E. (UNESCO dates their carving to the first century C.E.). Britannica reports that the monument is about 37 meters wide and 17 meters high.

Two things make the monument useful evidence. First, the carvings include many short inscriptions that name donors, among them the ivory workers of Vidisha, so the building shows who paid for early Buddhism in addition to the king. Second, the reliefs tell stories of the Buddha’s life and earlier lives without showing him as a human figure: his presence is shown by a tree, a wheel, footprints, or an empty seat. Remember that Sanchi was abandoned after the 12th century, rediscovered in 1818, and repaired and partly reconstructed between 1881 and 1919, so what we see is an ancient monument as it was restored. The photographs are modern, and the carvings are sandstone reliefs that tell a story selected by their patrons.

Image 2 is a carved lintel panel from the eastern gateway; the photographer’s caption identifies it as the departure of Prince Siddhartha from Kapilavastu.
