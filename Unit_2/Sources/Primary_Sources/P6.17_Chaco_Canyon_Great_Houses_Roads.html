Historical Context

Chaco Canyon, in northwestern New Mexico, was the center of an ancestral Puebloan culture that flourished from the mid-800s to the 1100s C.E. Its great houses, multistory stone buildings with hundreds of rooms, were planned and built over generations. The largest, Pueblo Bonito, had some 650 rooms. Roads linked Chaco to many other great houses across the region. Yet the canyon is dry and its growing season short, and scholars still disagree about what Chaco was: a ceremonial center that drew people at certain times, a political capital, a hub of trade in turquoise and other goods, or a combination.

The first National Park Service text reproduced here reports the view of Pueblo descendants that Chaco was a special gathering place for many peoples and clans, and sets out the questions that researchers still ask. The second text reports research on cylinder jars, tall painted vessels found almost only at Pueblo Bonito. Chemical analysis of fragments found a chemical marker of cacao, a plant that does not grow in the Southwest, which suggests contact with distant societies and, perhaps, a drink restricted to a small group.

These are modern descriptions based on archaeology and the views of descendant communities; neither records what Chacoans said. Keep separate what archaeology shows (buildings, roads, objects, residues) and what is interpretation (public architecture, gatherings, elite consumption). The Navajo narrative recorded in 1889, elsewhere in this reader, is a different kind of memory of the same canyon, told by a different people about a great house at Chaco.

Editorial note. Both texts are U.S. government works (National Park Service) reproduced verbatim from the pages “History & Culture” (updated January 15, 2026) and “Pre-Columbian Chocolate Discovered at Chaco” (updated May 2, 2024), with omissions marked [. . .]: from the first, a paragraph on the sequence of great-house construction (beginning “During the middle and late 800s”) and the later paragraphs on tribal affiliations; from the second, passages on the excavation, Maya cacao use, and the sherd analysis. Document Text

“The Center of Chacoan Culture” (NPS, History & Culture)

For all the wild beauty of Chaco Canyon’s high-desert landscape, its long winters, short growing seasons, and marginal rainfall create an unlikely place for a major center of ancestral Puebloan culture to take root and flourish. Yet this valley was the center of a thriving culture a thousand years ago. The monumental scale of its architecture, the complexity of its community life, the high level of its community social organization, and its far-reaching commerce created a cultural vision unlike any other seen before or since.

The cultural flowering of the Chacoan people began in the mid 800s and lasted more than 300 years. We can see it clearly in the grand scale of the architecture. Using masonry techniques unique for their time, they constructed massive stone buildings (Great Houses) of multiple stories containing hundreds of rooms much larger than any they had previously built. The buildings were planned from the start, in contrast to the usual practiced of adding rooms to existing structures as needed. Constructions on some of these buildings spanned decades and even centuries. Although each is unique, all great houses share architectural features that make them recognizable as Chacoan.

[. . .]

By 1050, Chaco had become the ceremonial, administrative, and economic center of the San Juan Basin. Its sphere of influence was extensive. Dozens of great houses in Chaco Canyon were connected by roads to more than 150 great houses throughout the region. It is thought that the great houses were not traditional farming villages occupied by large populations. They may instead have been impressive examples of “public architecture” that were used periodically during times of ceremony, commerce, and trading when temporary populations came to the canyon for these events.

What was at the heart of this great social experiment? Pueblo descendants say that Chaco was a special gathering place where many peoples and clans converged to share their ceremonies, traditions, and knowledge. Chaco is central to the origins of several Navajo clans and ceremonies. Chaco is also an enduring enigma for researchers. Was Chaco the hub of a turquoise-trading network established to acquire macaws, copper bells, shells, and other commodities from distant lands? Did Chaco distribute food and resources to growing populations when the climate failed them? Was Chaco “the center place,” binding a region together by a shared vision? We may never fully understand Chaco.

In the 1100s and 1200s, change came to Chaco as new construction slowed and Chaco’s role as a regional center shifted. [. . .]

“Pre-Columbian Chocolate Discovered at Chaco” (NPS)

[. . .] In the late 1800s Richard Wetherill and his assistant George Pepper, the first archaeologists to work in Chaco, excavated 111 cylinder jars from one particular room at Pueblo Bonito. [. . .] Over the course of subsequent excavations, archaeologists have gained a deeper understanding of just how unique these vessels are. Fewer than 200 have been found in the entire American Southwest, including those in Room 28 at Pueblo Bonito. [. . .]

[. . .] Only the three sherds most likely from cylinder jars exhibited trace theobromine, a conclusive indicator of cacao or chocolate. The implications of this find are extraordinary. The cacao plant grows only in certain tropical climates, and the nearest possibility for Chaco is Central Mexico. [. . .] Further, the limited distribution of the cylinder jars could be evidence that only an elite or small segment of the population consumed the beverage.
