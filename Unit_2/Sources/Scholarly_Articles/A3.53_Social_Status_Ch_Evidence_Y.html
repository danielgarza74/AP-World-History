Selection Overview

Yates uses legal documents buried with a local Qin official around 217 B.C.E. to reconstruct the status of ordinary commoners (shih-wu) in the Qin state, noting that "Han apologists heaped abuse on the Ch'in at the same time as they apparently continued certain Ch'in practices." He shows commoners were registered by height, owed military and labor service and taxes, and were grouped in mutually responsible five-family units, and he rejects the claim that the Qin state kept them uniformly poor, finding "a wide range between the richest and the poorest."

The recent discovery of an enor- mous army of pottery figures, apparently guarding the First Emperor's tomb to the east of Xi'an in Shensi province, has understandably captured the imagination of many throughout the world, and has increased interest in the origins and history of the Ch'in state and the circumstances surrounding the founding of the world's longest lasting empire. But the precise nature of the administrative techniques the Ch'in applied, the military organization it adopted, and the social base of its extraordinary achievement have remained largely obscure because so much of the primary source material was lost in the savage wars accompanying the Ch'in's destruction and the Han's victory. In addition, Han apologists heaped abuse on the Ch'in at the same time as they apparently continued certain Ch'in practices. What was original with the Han, and what did they inherit from their predecessor? Many questions of cardinal importance have yet to be answered definitively. Yet we are fortunate that in 1975 a discovery was made in Hupei province perhaps even more remarkable for the historian than those spectacular finds at the first Emperor's gravesite. For it was then that the coffin of a local Ch'in official revealed a treasury of legal documents and almanac texts written on slips of bamboo which had apparently been deposited in or shortly after 217 B.C, a bare four years after the unification of China.'
