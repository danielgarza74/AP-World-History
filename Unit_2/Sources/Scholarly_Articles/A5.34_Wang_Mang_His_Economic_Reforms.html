Selection Overview

Dubs argues that Wang Mang's reforms (nationalizing land under the well-field system, limiting large holdings, banning the sale of slaves, new monopolies and price controls) came from Confucian literati rather than from any concern of Wang's for the people, and that "these reforms did not actually improve the economic position of any class." He grants that the land and slave limits "would have benefited society" but shows they were rescinded within two years under pressure from the powerful, while the monopolies burdened the poor and helped provoke the rebellions that killed Wang.

WANG MANG AND HIS ECONOMIC REFORMS BY HOMER H. DUBS Duke University Wang Mang was one of the cleverest moulders of educated public opinion that China has ever had. By his manipulations of that opinion he was able to take advantage of the fact that three successive emperors had no living sons to succeed them. He thus made himself emperor (9 A.D.). Thereupon he inaugurated certain economic reforms, which, combined with a series of natural cal- amities and misgovernment, brought about banditry, rebellion, and his own death. He has been praised as "one of the greatest statesmen China has ever produiced", a socialist who was ahead of his time and who failed merely because he lacked the modern facilities for government checks and controls 1). Marxists have lauded him as the world's first revolutionist. My own study of the documents, however, has convinced me that Wang really cared little or nothing for the interests of the people; he ruthlessly sacrificed them in furthering his own ends. Before taking up this theme, it is well to consider the source for our information about Wang Mang, since it has been said that this account is prejudiced. The only extensive source for the history of his time is Pan Ku's Han-shu 1). In the reign of Emperor Ch'eng (ca. 32-18 B.c.), Pan Ku's clan had for a time been very close to the throne, enjoying an eminence that was said to have shaken the empire"). About 1 B.C., however, Pan Ku's grandfather had been compelled by Wang Mang's associates to retire from official life 3). Hence the Pan clan was not in sympathy with Wang Mang, although it took no part in the rebellions against him. Pan Ku was, moreover, a loyal adherent of the Later Han dynasty; he was highly honored by and intimate with the second and third emperors of that dynasty4). He thus had ample motives to be prejudiced against Wang Mang. There is, however, little or no evidence that he actually dis- torted his history because of any such prejudice. In the first place, the method of writing history that he inherited from Szu-Ma Ch'ien was that of extensive quotation from the sources.
