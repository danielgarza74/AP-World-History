Excavating Jenne-jeno: Reports from the Middle Niger
Site occupied from about the 3rd century B.C.E.; excavators’ reports of 1983 and 2001
 
TOC FIT Civilizations of Africa: Along the Niger River: Cities without States (textbook pp. 235–237).
WHY HERE Two reports by the archaeologists who dug Jenne-jeno, an early African city cluster with iron and long-distance trade but little sign of palaces or great differences in wealth.
TREATMENT Short quotations (about 320 words in all) from two copyrighted modern excavation reports, with full citations; modern scholarship, not an ancient text. No openly licensed photograph of the site was found.
ESSAY PROMPTS 6.1, 6.2
ARGUMENT ROLES 6.1.1 Supports; 6.2.2 Supports; 6.2.3 Qualifies
Historical Context

Jenne-jeno (“ancient Jenne”) is a mound of settlement debris in the Inland Niger Delta of Mali, on a floodplain where the river deposits rich silt each year. Roderick and Susan McIntosh excavated it in 1977 and 1981 and returned to the region in the 1990s. Their finds, including iron-working, pottery, imported stone, and a mud-brick wall, show a large settlement growing in this region by the last centuries B.C.E., long before camel caravans carried Saharan trade across the desert to the north.

The two passages below are the excavators’ own words, written for different audiences about eighteen years apart. Each mixes data (dates, areas, kinds of objects found) with interpretation (what the data mean for how people lived and were governed). The authors describe a “cluster” of closely spaced mounds and argue that its communities specialized in different crafts. Notice the limits of what excavation can show: “low social differentiation” is measured here only by the quantity of imported objects, and a city wall is evidence of organized labor but does not by itself show who directed it.

The famous terracotta statues from the Inland Niger Delta, often shown in textbooks, mostly belong to later centuries and are not part of this evidence.

Editorial note. Both works are in copyright. They are quoted briefly for teaching, and nothing else from them (including their maps and figures, apart from one figure caption quoted below) is reproduced. Quotation marks identify the authors’ words. Each passage is continuous; the passages are given in the order of the source and are separated by omitted text, except that the last two passages from 1983 are consecutive sentences. EXCERPTS FROM THE EXCAVATORS’ REPORTS

Roderick J. McIntosh and Susan Keech McIntosh, “Forgotten Tells of Mali” (1983)

“Founded by the 3rd century B.C., Jenne-jeno expanded to at minimum twelve hectares by about A.D. 50 and was a settlement of thirty-three hectares by sometime before A.D. 600-800. By this last date Jenne-jeno was enclosed by a 3.3 meter wide mud-brick wall of about 2.0 kilometer circumference—the settlement was of true urban dimensions.”

“Traffic was in iron ore (a medium-carbon steel was smelted and refined, apparently on industrial scale, as early as 200 B.C. at the site of ancient Jenne; R. F. Tylecote 1982:personal communication), sandstone grinders (which were imported in a number of standardized shapes and sizes), and exotic volcanic stone required for production of beads and other ornaments. The nearest source for any of these materials was at least 50-75 kilometers away.”

“Specialist ‘communities’ gravitated together but were initially unwilling to surrender completely their identities to a monolithic urban presence. The resolution to the paradox was made through clustering. The whole functioned as a city of many heterogeneous communities, each nevertheless able to maintain

a separate physical identity.”

“By the end of the 1st millennium A.D., some urban clusters had become very large indeed. The ancient Jenne-jeno cluster of twelve sites could boast a combined area of 51.2 hectares.”

Susan Keech McIntosh and Roderick J. McIntosh, “Results of Recent Excavations at Jenné-jeno and Djenné, Mali” (2001)

“Among the most intriguing and unexpected patterns, given the size of the settlement at Jenné-jeno and its intensity of occupation, is the low social differentiation across the site, as measured by quantity of imports (including all copper, iron, stone, and glass artifacts).”

“Clark’s preliminary findings to date support claims of inter-site specialization in pyrotechnological activities, as evidenced by spatially segregated concentrations of furnaces on three of the mound sites. However, radiocarbon results suggest that these activity areas were not used simultaneously.”

From p. 6, on evidence for different phases of the city wall: “This hypothesis of different episodes of city wall construction requires further investigation.”

From the caption to Figure 2 (p. 12): “The concentration of population on a few very large and many,

smaller, closely-clustered sites is a highly original aspect of urban development in this region.”
