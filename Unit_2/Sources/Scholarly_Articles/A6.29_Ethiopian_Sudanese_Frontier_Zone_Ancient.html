Selection Overview

Kirwan asks why the kingdoms of Aksum and Meroe, separated only by the easily crossed Butana plain, had so little peaceful contact, and he answers that each looked outward in a different direction: Aksum to South Arabia and the Red Sea, Meroe down the Nile to Graeco-Roman Egypt, until Meroe "virtually became a Roman satellite." Reading Ezana's fourth-century inscriptions, he argues that Aksumite invasions dealt "a mortal blow" to an already decaying Meroe, weakened first by the loss of its Nile trade route and the collapse of its Roman market, and may have helped shift power north to Lower Nubia.

AN ETHIOPIAN-SUDANESE FRONTIER ZONE IN ANCIENT HISTORY L. P. KIRWAN Favourable geographical conditions in the dry season across the Ethiopian- Sudanese frontier zone, between the kingdom of Aksum and the Nilotic kingdom of Meroe, while they apparently failed to encourage peaceful ex changes, favoured warfare between two kingdoms and peoples very differen environmentally and in cultural and political orientation. Evidence for thi warfare across the Butana and in the 'Island of Meroe' (between the Atbara and the Nile) is contained in Aksumite inscriptions of the first half of the fourth century ad. Two of these and probably a third, a recent chance discovery commemorate Aksumite invasions of the Meroitic heartland; invasions largel instrumental, it would seem, in bringing about the end of a long line of Meroitic rulers and the disintegration of an already decaying kingdom. These invasion and subsequent Aksumite pressures may have been a principal factor in th economic decline of the Nile valley upstream of the Third Cataract from t mid-fourth century onwards. These pressures may also have been a factor in northward shift of power-centre (accompanied by a revival of Meroitic roya traditions) at about that time, from the Island of Meroe to the First-Second Cataract region adjoining Egypt. Travelling FROM khartoum across the Butana and the Atbara river towards northern Ethiopia, one might wonder why there seems to have been so little commercial or cultural contact between the two ancient kingdoms on either side of this frontier zone: the kingdom of Aksum (broadly, Tigrai, Eritrea and northern Begamedir) and the kingdom of Meroe on the Nile between Sennar and Egypt. Certainly, in the dry season (October-May) there are no obstacles to travel. The Butana, bounded by the Atbara and the Nile (see map), is a gently undulating plain, grassy savannah in the centre, semi-desert in the north. Except in the south, where there is a thin spread of acacia, tree growth is mostly limited to a few long drainage systems (wadis). Nor are the Atbara or its Ethiopian tributary, the Tak- kaze, formidable obstacles at this time of year. The Atbara dwindles to a series of pools from the end of August onwards, while the Takkaze, a stream in winter, descends from the Aksum region {c. 2000m) between broad banks of white shingle, thus providing a relatively easy avenue of advance down to the Sudanese plain; or so it seemed when I last crossed the Takkaze three years ago on my way from Aksum to Gondar. The country here, moreover, and farther north, falls away gradually from the level of the Ethiopian plateau, in sharp contrast to the precipi- tous mountain wall over to the east that towers above the Red Sea coast. Despite this relative ease of access, evidence of trade?indeed of peaceful con? tacts of any kind?is negligible and virtually limited to an Aksumite coin of the fourth century ad from the site of Meroe (some 200km north of Khartoum on the east bank of the Nile) and to some Meroitic bronze bowls of first-second century date from Aksum (Shinnie, 1970, 1967). Incompatibility between an essentially highland and an essentially lowland and mainly riverain people was, no doubt, a factor. More significant were the cultural differences dividing the two kingdoms. These were profound and rooted in history. At about the turn of the third century this Arabian impact was renewed?this time through conquest?as I have described in an earlier article in this journal (Kirwan, 1972). This may have led to a new Aksumite dynasty, Arabian in origin (Drewes, 1962). By contrast, in the Meroitic kingdom?basically African though it was in so many respects, including the first use of a purely African written language? Egyptian influence was inevitably predominant. It had, after all, emerged in the * Island of Meroe* (between the Atbara and the Nile) c. 600 bc out of the strongly Egyptianized kingdom of Napata, near the Fourth Cataract; a kingdom which had itself arisen from the ashes of pharaonic colonial settlement in the Nubian Nile valley. Just as Aksum looked east across the Red Sea to the Yemenite kingdom of Himyar as a powerful source of cultural inspiration, so Meroe looked north down the Nile and across the linking desert expanses to Graeco-Roman Egypt with its legacy of pharaonic tradition. Graeco-Roman (Hellenistic) influence is of course evident enough at Aksum also, for Aksum traded with Egypt through its Red Sea port of Adulis, which was already large and flourishing by the late first century (Kirwan, 1972). Zoscales, king of Aksum then or somewhat later, was, according to the Periplus, * acquainted with Greek literature', while Greek was used in Aksu? mite royal inscriptions down to the fourth century, though probably more for the benefit of foreign than native readers (Schoff, 1912). However, compared with Aksum, Graeco-Roman influence is far more widely evident in the Meroitic kingdom, not only in the use of Greek?probably taught in at least one Meroitic school (Shinnie, 1967), and even of some Latin for official purposes (Hintze, 1964)?but in art and architecture and even in some ofthe tastes and social habits of Meroe's aristocracy. These cultural influences were diffused not only through trade but also through Meroe's intimate political relationship with the Roman Empire. After the Augustan victories on the Nubian Nile in 24 and 22 bc, witnessed by Strabo, Meroe in fact virtually became a Roman satellite (Jameson, 1968), profiting greatly from her geographical position on the Nile as an entrepot for Rome's rich trade with Central Africa. Juvenal (c. 100 ad) refers to this trade which reached Egypt through the port of Aswan (Syene); trade in ivory destined to embellish the dining-tables of Rome's affluent society. 'Nowadays a rich man takes no pleasure in his dinner?his turbot and his venison have no taste, his unguents and his roses no perfume?unless the broad slabs of his dinner- table rest upon a ramping, gaping leopard of solid ivory, made of the tusks sent to us by the swift-footed Moor from the portal of Syene' (Ramsay, 1918). In return, luxury manufactures from Alexandria and other Mediterranean centres flowed south and have been found in abundance in Meroitic tombs. 'Hot Meroe* also achieved a certain fame in Rome in another direction, as a very ancient and revered centre of Isis worship, particularly after the public worship of Isis was permitted by the emperor Gaius Caligula (Kirwan, 1957). This pax romana endured along the Nile south of Egypt until the fourth century. Aksum by contrast was by no means wholly at peace with the Empire, and the inclusion of Aksumite captives in Aurelian's triumph through Rome, recorded in the Historia Augusta (33, 4), indicates that Aksum had a part in the great uprising of the Orient against the Empire in the second half of the third century. It was only after the advent of Constantine and the introduction of Christianity to northern Ethiopia early in the fourth century that Aksum was to be found firmly within the Roman orbit.
