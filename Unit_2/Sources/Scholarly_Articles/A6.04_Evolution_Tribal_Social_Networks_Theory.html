Selection Overview

Braun and Plog argue that nonhierarchical, or "tribal," regional networks, in which decisions were made "primarily through consensus," grew more integrated as a response to rising environmental uncertainty, not out of competition and conflict as older models held. Using pottery style and exchange evidence from the Western Anasazi Southwest (about 700–1300 C.E.) and western Illinois in the Hopewell and later Woodland periods (about 1–700 C.E.), they find that communities answered greater local risk with closer, more formal regional cooperation.

EVOLUTION OF "TRIBAL" SOCIAL NETWORKS: THEORY AND PREHISTORIC NORTH AMERICAN EVIDENCE David P. Braun and Stephen Plog This paper addresses two topics central to the study of nonhierarchical, regional social networks, sometimes termed "tribal" social networks: (1) alternative models of the evolution of regional integration; and (2) the archaeological determination of characteristics of such regional networks. Problems in previous ethno- logical and archaeological studies are identified, and an alternative model is proposed. This is based on a more general theory of organizational processes in nonhierarchical social systems. Data from the prehistoric North American Southwest and Midwest are shown to support the more general model, which treats such networks as organizational responses to increasing environmental uncertainty occasioned by either cultural or physical ecological factors, or both. EVIDENCE OF AN intensification of regional integration within relatively undifferentiated or nonhierarchical social networks appears in several areas of prehistoric North America. Ar- chaeological data from these regions therefore can be used to evaluate models of early societal evolution prior to the formation of complex, hierarchically differentiated systems. In this paper we briefly review previous explanations for the development of regional integra- tion within so-called "tribal" or nonhierarchical social networks and note their limitations. We then suggest that such limitations can be avoided by subsuming the previous explanations within a general model of the organizational processes of such networks. This general model explains in- creasing regional integration as an organizational response to, or effect of, changes in the degree of local environmental unpredictability. Such unpredictability may be triggered by a variety of cultural or ecological factors. Archaeological evidence from the Western Anasazi area of the American Southwest, ca. A.D. 700 to 1300, and from the prairie regions of western Illinois, ca. A.D. 1 to 600 or 700, will be used to illustrate the greater utility of the general model. Archaeological methods for studying nonhierarchical social systems require numerous assump- tions about the meaning of particular material measurements. Therefore, before considering the archaeological evidence, we shall discuss the bridging assumptions involved, emphasizing the analysis of two frequently preserved media of communication: decorated domestic implements and goods exchanged as socially obligating tokens. SOCIAL THEORY Our concern here is with the evolution of formal panresidential integration within so-called "tribal" social systems. Our definition of such systems follows those of Middleton and Tait (1958), Gluckman (1965), Sahlins (1968:14-27), and Service (1971:109-132): social networks integrated by cross-cutting panresidential institutions, but lacking class structure or full-time segmental specialization. Such systems also may be termed "nonhierarchical," in the sense that decision- making occurs primarily through consensus rather than through the full-time exercise of power by formally sanctified authorities. We realize that the terms "tribal" and "nonhierarchical" are awkward, but our profession lacks a more adequate set of terms for discussing such social phenomena. David P. Braun and Plog] EVOLUTION OF "TRIBAL" SOCIAL NETWORKS 505 Ethnological Approaches The literature generally depicts "tribal" regional social institutions as institutions for mutual nonagression, noninterference, or defense (e.g., Childe 1951:105-106; Radcliffe-Brown 1952:178-211; Keesing and Keesing 1971:148-176). Such institutions are often held to have evolved in response to conditions of competition or conflict among initially autonomous residential units, or between such units and larger, more complexly organized populations (e.g., Fried 1966, 1975; Carneiro 1970; Dumond 1972; Sahlins 1968:4-13; Service 1971:100-109; Adams 1975:222-228.) Such explanations rely on two key assumptions, neither of which we accept. First, they require assuming that local autonomy is a natural or universally sought condition in human social rela- tions. Such autonomy is held to be possible among hunter-gatherers only in unusual cir- cumstances. The development of food production, however, is thought to have allowed every local population to become self-sufficient, and hence to be autonomous-at least initially (e.g., Childe 1951:71-74; Carneiro 1972; Cohen 1977:27-40). Second, the standard explanations require the assumption that, if an institution, e.g., a set of clans or sodalities, generally performed certain in- tegrative functions in its known ethnographic setting, then it probably originated in order to per- form those functions. The image of local autonomy that emerges from ethnographic studies, however, is misleading. As Wobst (1978) most recently has noted, this image is more a product of the methods and condi- tions of ethnographic fieldwork than it is a verified behavioral condition. Furthermore, many of the ethnological studies, whose findings have been used to support various explanations of "tribal" regional evolution, were conducted by scholars operating within a functionalist paradigm (see, for example, the explanatory arguments in Fried [1967] or Service [1971, 1975]). The interpretations provided by these studies mirror the scholars' preconceptions of "societies" as relatively bounded, discrete phenomena analogous to organisms. Many standard explanations for the evolution of "tribal" social institutions, in fact, portray the development of regional integration simply as an increase in the demographic scale at which autonomy is defined. Such portrayals have tended to confuse the debate over origins, by combin- ing two distinct questions: first, the question of the degree to which a region may be considered socially integrated; and second, the question of the extent of boundary formation accompanying such integration (e.g., Fried 1975; Sanders and Webster 1978). As we shall discuss below, the two questions are necessarily analytically separate. Finally, explanations of an institution's evolution based solely on ethnographic generalizations about that institution's "function" involve teleological or, at best, tautological reasoning (e.g., see Turner and Maryanski [1979] for a recent review of this problem). Different institutions, for ex- ample, can have similar organizational effects under differing circumstances. Conversely, similar institutions can have quite different organizational effects, again depending upon circumstances. Our concern therefore should not be with fitting archaeological evidence to mental images of ethnographically known institutions. Rather, we should be concerned simply with using the ar- chaeological record to try to observe the organizational effects of interest, i.e., effects on com- munication, delegation of decision-making responsibilities or differentiation of productive tasks (F. Plog 1974b:49-54, 1977b; Hill 1977; Lewontin 1978; Synenki and Braun 1980). An Ecological Approach An alternative to the traditional approach to the study of organizational processes in "tribal" social networks (e.g., Braun 1977:80-102) is suggested by recent research concerning adaptive processes in living systems (e.g., Miller 1978:9-87; Lewontin 1978; Rappaport 1979:145-160; Winterhalder 1980). Numerous studies have shown (see recent summary in Winterhalder [1980] ) that survival in living systems requires effective response not to average environmental condi- tions but to risks arising from environmental unpredictability (Slobodkin and Rapoport 1974). Uncertainty and risks follow from the unpredictability of all environments over time and space.
