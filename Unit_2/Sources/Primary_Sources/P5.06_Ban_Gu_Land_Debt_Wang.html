Ban Gu on Land, Debt, and Wang Mang’s Edict (Han shu 24 and 99)
memorials and edicts of ca. 168 B.C.E. to 12 C.E.; recorded in the Han shu in the 1st century C.E.

WHY HERE Han officials describe small farmers in debt, great families gathering land, a failed attempt to limit holdings, and Wang Mang’s sweeping land and slave reform, all in one historian’s record.

Historical Context

The Han shu (Book of Han) is the dynastic history of the Western Han (206 B.C.E.–9 C.E.) and of Wang Mang’s reign (to 23 C.E.). Ban Biao began it, and his son Ban Gu (32–92 C.E.) wrote most of it; it was unfinished at Ban Gu’s death, and his sister Ban Zhao, whose Lessons for Women appears in this reader, helped complete it. Its chapter 24 is a treatise on food and money. Chapter 99 is the biography of Wang Mang, the Han official who took the throne in 9 C.E. and founded the short-lived Xin dynasty. Ban Gu wrote under the restored Han and was no friend to Wang Mang, so the account of his reforms and their failure comes from a hostile pen.

The passages are arguments and decrees, so each is a source about what its author wanted the court to believe. Chao Cuo, advising Emperor Wen, argues that farmers carry the state and lose their land to merchants. The Han shu does not date his memorial; the emperor’s answer, a remission of half the land tax, is recorded for 168 B.C.E. Dong Zhongshu, the Confucian adviser of Emperor Wu, blames the loss of land on Qin law and proposes limits. The debate over limits in 7 B.C.E. shows that the court could discuss restraining great households and could also drop the plan when powerful families objected. Wang Mang’s edict of 9 C.E. describes land concentration and slave markets in strong terms before trying to end both. Ask who gains from each description, and what the passages show about land, debt, and the power of great households.

1. Chao Cuo on the farmer and the merchant (ca. 168 B.C.E.)

Han shu 24A Chao Cuo then also urged the emperor: [ . . . ] “Now a farming household of five has no fewer than two members who owe labor service; the land they are able to plow is no more than a hundred mu, and the harvest from a hundred mu is no more than a hundred shi. In spring they plow, in summer they weed, in autumn they reap, in winter they store; they cut firewood and brush, repair government offices, and supply labor service. In spring they cannot escape the wind and dust, in summer the heat, in autumn the rain, in winter the cold and frost; through the four seasons there is not one day of rest. And in addition they privately see off those departing and welcome those arriving, mourn the dead and visit the sick, and raise orphans and rear the young, all within this. With such toil and hardship they are still struck by flood and drought, by urgent and cruel government, by taxes levied out of season, by orders issued in the morning and changed by evening. When [the levies] fall due, those who have goods must sell them at half price; those who have none must borrow at interest that doubles the debt. And so there are those who sell their fields and houses, and even sell their sons and grandsons, to pay their debts. “But the great merchants hoard goods and make double profit, and the lesser ones sit in rows at their stalls selling, holding the scarce and surplus goods and wandering the markets of the capital every day. Taking advantage of the government’s urgent needs, whatever they sell fetches double. So their men do not plow or weed, their women do not raise silkworms or weave, yet their clothes must be patterned and embroidered and their food must be fine grain and meat. They have none of the farmers’ bitterness but gain a thousand or a hundred fold. Because of their wealth they mingle with kings and marquises, and their power exceeds that of officials; they bring one another down for profit. They roam for a thousand li, hats and carriage canopies in sight of one another, riding stout carriages and whipping fat horses, wearing silk shoes and trailing white silk. This is how the merchants annex the farmers, and why the farmers drift away and flee.

“Now the law lowers the merchant, but the merchants have already become rich and honored; it raises the farmer, but the farmers have already become poor and humble. So what custom honors is what the ruler despises, and what the officials look down on is what the law honors. High and low are reversed, likes and dislikes clash, and to hope in this way for a wealthy state and a law that stands is impossible.” [ . . . ]

2. Dong Zhongshu on the legacy of Qin (reign of Emperor Wu, r. 141–87 B.C.E.)

Han shu 24A Dong Zhongshu said to the emperor: [ . . . ] “In antiquity the people were taxed no more than a tenth, so what was asked of them was easy to supply; the people were put to service no more than three days [a year], so their strength was easily sufficient. The people’s wealth was enough at home to support the aged and fulfill filial duty, enough outside to serve their superiors and supply the tax, and enough below to raise wives and children with full affection; and so the people gladly followed their superiors. With Qin it was not so. It used the laws of Shang Yang and changed the institutions of the emperors and kings. It abolished the well-field system and allowed the people to buy and sell [land], so that the rich had fields linked by the thousand and the hundred [mu; the two Chinese characters may also be read as “field paths”], and the poor had no land to stand an awl on. [The rich] also monopolized the profit of rivers and lakes and controlled the wealth of mountains and forests; dissolute, they broke every limit and competed in extravagance. A town had the dignity of a lord, a village the wealth of dukes and marquises. How could the common people not be impoverished? Added to this, the people were made to serve a month as substitute conscripts, then again as regular conscripts, then a year of frontier garrison and a year of labor service, thirty times what was done in antiquity. The land tax, the poll tax, and the profits of salt and iron were twenty times what they were in antiquity. Some tilled the fields of powerful households and had to pay half. So the poor commonly wore the clothes of oxen and horses and ate the food of dogs and pigs. Piled on this were greedy and cruel officials, who imposed punishments and executions at random. The people, miserable and with nothing to live on, fled into the mountains and forests and turned to banditry; men in the reddish-brown clothes of convicts filled half the roads, and the cases judged each year numbered in the tens of millions.

“The Han rose and followed this without change. Although the old well-field method is hard to carry out at once, it would be fitting to come somewhat closer to antiquity: limit the land that people may register in their names, so as to make up what the needy lack and block the road to annexation. Give salt and iron back to the people. Do away with slaves [or: slavery], and abolish [the owners’] power to kill them on their own authority. Lighten taxes and reduce labor service, so as to ease the people’s strength. Only then can there be good government.”

3. A proposal to limit land and slaves, and its fate (early in the reign of Emperor Ai, 7 B.C.E.)

Han shu 24A When Emperor Ai took the throne, Shi Dan, assisting in government, proposed: “Of the sage kings of antiquity, none failed to set up the well-field system, and only then could government be made even. Emperor Wen, following the wars of the fallen Zhou and the chaotic Qin, found the empire empty, and so he worked to encourage farming and silk-raising and led by frugality. The people were just beginning to be prosperous and the harm of annexation had not yet arisen, so he set no limit on the people’s fields or on slaves. Now, after generations of peace, powerful and wealthy officials and commoners have assessed property running to enormous sums, while the poor and weak grow ever more distressed. A gentleman in government values following established ways and is slow to make changes, but the reason for changing is to relieve urgent need. It may not yet be possible to work out every detail; it would be fitting to set limits in broad terms.”

The emperor sent the proposal down for discussion. Chancellor Kong Guang and Minister of Works He Wu memorialized and requested: “Let kings and marquises be allowed to register fields in their own states. Marquises resident at Chang’an, princesses who register fields in the counties and dao [districts administered like counties], and the Marquises Within the Passes, officials, and commoners who register fields, shall none register more than thirty qing. Let kings have 200 slaves, marquises and princesses 100, and marquises within the passes, officials, and commoners 30. The time limit is three years; violators shall have [the excess] confiscated by the government.” At that time the prices of fields, houses, and slaves fell [or: would have fallen under the limits]. The Ding and Fu clans were in power and Dong Xian was highly honored, and all found it inconvenient. The edict said it should wait, and so the matter was shelved and not carried out.

4. Wang Mang’s edict on land and slaves (9 C.E.)

Han shu 99B Mang said: “In antiquity, huts were set up in well-field units of eight households, each husband and wife having a hundred mu of land, taxed at one-tenth, so that the state was supplied, the people were rich, and songs of praise arose. This was the way of Tang [Yao] and Yu [Shun], which the Three Dynasties followed. Qin was without the Way. It laid heavy taxes to provide for itself, exhausted the people’s strength to satisfy its desires, destroyed the sages’ institutions, and abolished the well-field system. Because of this annexation arose and greed and meanness were born: the strong measured off fields by the thousands, while the weak had not so much as a place to stand an awl. They also set up markets for slaves, [penned] in the same enclosures as oxen and horses, controlled by commoners and ministers who arbitrarily decided over their lives. Wicked and cruel men found profit in this, even to the point of kidnapping and selling other people’s wives and children, which goes against the will of Heaven, violates human relations, and contradicts the saying that ‘of the nature of Heaven and Earth, humans are the most precious.’ The Documents say ‘I will enslave and put you to death’ only of those who disobey orders, and only then is such a penalty applied.

“The House of Han reduced the land tax to one-thirtieth, but there were constant service levies and poll taxes, and even the infirm and sick were all made to pay, while powerful households encroached and oppressed, parceling out fields and forcing others to rent. In name it was a thirtieth; in fact it was five-tenths. Fathers and sons, husbands and wives toiled the whole year and what they gained was not enough to keep themselves alive. So the rich had grain to spare for their dogs and horses, grew proud, and did wrong; the poor could not get their fill even of dregs and chaff, grew desperate, and committed crimes. Both fell into guilt, and punishments were never set aside. Earlier, when I was at the Great Foothills [that is, as regent], I began to order that the land of the empire be made public land, apportioned by head in well units; at that time there was the omen of the fine grain, but the rebels and traitors brought it to a halt. Now I rename the land of the empire ‘king’s fields’ and slaves ‘private retainers,’ and neither may be bought or sold. A household with fewer than eight male persons but more than one well of land shall divide the surplus land among its nine degrees of kin, neighbors, and villagers. Those who formerly had no land and are now due to receive land shall receive it according to the regulations. Anyone who dares to oppose the sacred well-field system and, lawless, confuses the people shall be cast out to the four borders to ward off demons, following the precedent of the Emperor of Yu [Shun], my imperial first ancestor.”

5. What followed

Han shu 99B [ . . . ] [Mang had put new large and small coins into circulation.] At that time the people found the Han five-shu coin convenient; because Mang’s coins came in two kinds, large and small, that were hard to tell apart, and were changed again and again so that no one trusted them, everyone privately used five-shu coins in buying and selling. False rumor said the large coins were to be withdrawn, and no one was willing to hold them. Mang, troubled by this, issued another decree: “All who hold five-shu coins, or who say that the large coins ought to be withdrawn, shall be treated as opposing the well-field system and cast out to the four borders.” Thereupon farmers and merchants lost their livelihoods, food and goods alike were thrown into ruin, and the people wept in the markets and streets. Those convicted for buying and selling fields, houses, and slaves, or for casting coin, from the nobles, ministers, and grandees down to the common people, could not be counted. [ . . . ]

Han shu 24A Three years later Mang, knowing the people were distressed, issued an edict that all who held “king’s fields” and “private retainers” might buy and sell them, and that they were not to be bound by the law. But punishments were harsh and his other policies were perverse and confused. [ . . . ] In his last years robbers and bandits rose in swarms. Armies were sent out against them, but generals and officers ran wild in the field. In the northern frontier and in the lands of Qing and Xu people ate one another; east of Luoyang rice cost 2,000 [cash] a shi. Mang sent generals of ducal rank [the Chinese is not certain here] to open the granaries of the east and lend to the destitute, and sent grandees and internuncios [palace ushers] to teach the people to boil wood into “curd”; the curd could not be eaten, and the effort only added to the disturbance. Several hundred thousand refugees entered the passes; officials were set up to feed them, but the officials stole their rations, and seven or eight of every ten of the refugees died of hunger.
