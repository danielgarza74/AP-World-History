Primary Source P5.8
The Yellow Turban Rising in the Hou Han shu
events of 184 C.E.; recorded by Fan Ye in the first half of the 5th century C.E.
 
TOC FIT Society and the State in China: Peasants, including the Yellow Turban Rebellion (textbook pp. 192– 193).
WHY HERE A historian’s narrative of the mass rising of 184 C.E., its suppression, the scale of the killing, and the tax relief and popular song that followed.
TREATMENT Fresh translation by the editor from the Chinese text of Hou Han shu 71, selected passages; made for this reader because the modern English translation of a related account could not be reproduced.
ESSAY PROMPTS 5.2, 5.5
ARGUMENT ROLES 5.2.3 Supports; 5.5.2 Supports
Historical Context

In 184 C.E. a religious movement led by Zhang Jue rose against the Later Han government across much of the empire. Its followers tied yellow cloths around their heads and became known as the Yellow Turbans. The court defeated the main rebel armies within months, though bands of rebels lasted for decades, and the generals and local strongmen who had raised armies against the rising kept them, so that the dynasty fell apart over the next decades.

The text is from the Hou Han shu (Book of the Later Han), compiled by Fan Ye in the 430s and 440s C.E. from earlier Han records. These passages come from the biography of Huangfu Song, the general who played the largest part in defeating the Yellow Turbans; the account of Zhang Jue’s movement stands near the start of that biography, and the battles and the tax relief come later. The rebels are seen from the side of the court. The historian blames Zhang Jue’s teaching for the rising and does not give the followers’ reasons in their own words; he does show how many people joined and how they were punished. Numbers of dead are the historian’s and may be exaggerated. Read the passage for what it reports about the movement’s healing practices, its organization, and its numbers, and about what the state did in reply.

Editorial note. This is a fresh translation prepared for this reader by the editor from the Chinese text as transcribed on Chinese Wikisource. It was made directly from the Chinese and has not been checked by a specialist. Square brackets mark words added for clarity; some glosses in brackets draw on the traditional commentary. Omissions are marked [ . . . ]. The historian dates the rising to Zhongping 1 (184 C.E.), using that era name for the whole year; the reign title was in fact changed only at the end of 184, after the main rebel forces had been defeated.
Document Text

Zhang Jue and the rising of 184

Hou Han shu 71 Earlier, Zhang Jue of Julu had styled himself the “Great Worthy and Good Teacher.” He served the Way of Huang[di] and Lao[zi]. He kept disciples; [the sick, or the disciples,] knelt, bowed, and confessed their faults, and with talisman water and spoken charms he treated illness; many of the sick recovered, and the common people believed in him and turned toward him. Jue then sent eight disciples out in the four directions to teach and convert the realm by the “good way,” and they passed it on from one to another, [in the historian’s words] deceiving and deluding [people]. In more than ten years his followers numbered in the hundreds of thousands, linked through commanderies and kingdoms; among the people of eight provinces, Qing, Xu, You, Ji, Jing, Yang, Yan, and Yu, there were none who did not respond. He then set up thirty-six fang [districts, like a general’s command]. The large fang had over ten thousand people, the small six or seven thousand, each with a chief appointed. They spread the rumor: “The Azure Heaven is dead; the Yellow Heaven shall stand. The year is jiazi; the realm will have great good fortune.” With white clay they wrote the characters “jiazi” on the gates of offices in the capital and on the offices of provinces and commanderies.

In the first year of Zhongping [184], Ma Yuanyi, [head] of a large fang, and others first gathered tens of thousands of people from Jing and Yang, planning to rise together at Ye. Yuanyi made many trips to the capital, with the palace attendants Feng Xu and Xu Feng and others as insiders, and it was agreed that on the fifth day of the third month those inside and outside would rise together. Before they could act, Tang Zhou of Jinan, a disciple of Zhang Jue, submitted a memorial informing on them, and Yuanyi was torn apart by chariots at Luoyang. Emperor Ling sent Zhou’s memorial down to the Three Excellencies and the Director of Retainers, and had Zhou Bin, Prefect of the Imperial Gardens, lead the staff of the three offices to investigate the guards in the palace and the common people who served Jue’s Way; more than a thousand were executed, an investigation was pressed in Jizhou, and Jue and others were pursued. Jue and his followers, knowing the plan had been exposed, sent orders by day and night in haste to all the fang, and all rose at once. All wore yellow cloths as their insignia, and people at the time called them the “Yellow Turbans”; they were also called the “moth bandits” [the commentary reads “moth” as “ant,” for their great numbers]. They killed people to sacrifice to Heaven. Jue styled himself “Lord General of Heaven,” his brother Bao “Lord General of Earth,” and Bao’s brother Liang “Lord General of Men.” Wherever they went they burned government offices and plundered towns; provinces and commanderies lost their footing, and many senior officials fled. Within ten days the realm had answered them, and the capital was shaken. [ . . . ]

The suppression

Hou Han shu 71 [Huangfu Song] fought Liang, Zhang Jue’s younger brother, at Guangzong. Liang’s followers were picked and fierce, and Song could not overcome them. The next day he closed his camp and rested his men, to watch for a change. Knowing that the rebels’ resolve had slackened a little, he secretly marshalled his troops by night and at cockcrow galloped to their lines; the fighting went on until late afternoon, and he defeated them completely and killed Liang. He took 30,000 heads; about 50,000 who plunged into the river died; more than 30,000 carts of baggage were burned, and all their wives and children were captured, and a great many others were bound and seized. Jue had already died of illness, so [Song] split open his coffin and cut up his corpse, and sent his head to the capital. Song again joined with Guo Dian of Fengyi, Grand Administrator of Julu, to attack Jue’s brother Bao at Xiaquyang, and killed him too. Heads and captives taken numbered more than a hundred thousand, and he built a “viewing mound” [a mound of the dead] south of the city. [ . . . ]

After the rising

Hou Han shu 71 Because the Yellow Turbans had been put down, the reign-period was changed to Zhongping [“Central Peace”]. Song memorialized, asking that Jizhou’s land tax for one year [be remitted], to relieve the starving people, and the emperor agreed. The common people sang:

“The empire in great disorder—the markets became ruins;
Mothers could not protect their sons, wives lost their husbands;
Thanks to Huangfu we can live in peace again.”
