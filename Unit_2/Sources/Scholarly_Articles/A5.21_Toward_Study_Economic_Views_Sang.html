Selection Overview

Kroll argues that the economic views of the Han statesman Sang Hung-yang cannot be separated from his wider worldview: trade, the "equalization of transportation," price stabilization, unified coinage, and the salt and iron monopolies all expressed a royal universalism modeled on Heaven. In Sang's thinking the sovereign redistributes wealth "to crush economically 'powerful' commoners" and make the people depend on the monarch, so his celebrated "realism" is "a relative one."

TOWARD A STUDY OF THE ECONOMIC VIEWS OF SANG HUNG-YANG J. L. KROLL (Leningrad Department of the Institute of Oriental Studies) The study of the economic views of the eminent Han statesman and thinker Sang Hung-yang ^ % (152-80 B.C.) permits various approaches. One ten dency has been to examine his ideas within the con text of the general history of economic thought. T'ang Ch'ing-tseng, for instance, has attempted to find modern European parallels in his analysis of the Yen t'ieh lun (especially those theo ries attributed to Sang). Later attempts, however, which examine Sang's economic ideas in the context of the history of Chinese economic thought itself seem more fruitful. For example, V. M. Stein has convincingly established the connection between the Kuan-tzu and Sang's views in the Yen t'ieh lun. Stein concluded first that Sang was "a Legalist of a new formation" who "approved of any source of in come," even of the kind of "profiteering" which was later endemic to the society of Peter the Great of Russia; and, second, that he was a practical finan cier, who borrowed the theoretical basis for his measures to regulate the economy from the Kuan-tzu, but disregarded the "deep-laid ethical basis" of this work. Another serious attempt has been made by Hu Chi-ch'uang. Tracing Sang's political views back to Legalism, Hu identifies several sources of his eco nomic thought. Besides the Kuan-tzu, Hu mentions the merchant ideologists Fan Li and Po Kuei, the Le galists Shang Yang and Han Fei, as well as Hsll Hsing, the opponent of Mencius. (One could add the Legalist Ch'ao Ts'o and the Taoist eclectic Ssu-ma Ch'ien to his list.) In contrast to Stein, Hu does not consid er Sang's contribution to the field of Chinese eco nomic thought insignificant, declaring instead that his emphasis on trade was the most outstanding part of his Contribution.3 Though much can be said in favour of such an approach, nevertheless it suffers from one serious limitation. After all, the ancient Chinese had no such science as political economy, in spite of the fact that scholars like Stein and Hu tend to treat their economic ideas as if they had such a science, as if their economic ideas were largely self-suffi cient and divorced from Chinese cultural concepts. These scholars unconsciously detach Sang's economic views from his political, legal, and other notions. It seems advisable, therefore, to investigate Sang's economic views in connection with such notions, i.e. as a realization of his general cultural concepts. Previously I have concluded that, according to Sang, the characteristic feature of the sovereign's attitude toward the alternative ways of exercising influence upon the world, as well as his attitude toward the heterogeneous extensions of space and ^ time, was a universal ism modelled on that of Heaven. Sang's emphasis on universal ism is in full accord with his Legalism.5 The question arises whether Sang's conception of royal universalism has also in fluenced his economic views. In order to answer this I must examine two interconnected theories, those of trade and the redistribution of wealth. Sang attributed the rise of trade not only to the division of labour among farmers, merchants, and artisans, but also to the unevenness of the geo graphical distribution of natural resources. In Hu's words, he "explained the importance of the cir culation of goods, proceeding from the division of labour between regions with different natural re sources."6 It would be more exact to say, however, that, according to Sang, trade fulfills a certain cosmic function. Two general cultural concepts con stitute the background for this theory — a differ entiating role played by Earth and an equalizing role played by Heaven.7 The differentiating role of Earth results from the heterogeneity of exten sions of space of the quarters, each quarter being considered a region dominated by one of "the five movers (or elements)."8 However, as Sang remarks, a region dominated by one "mover" proves also to be a source of supply for some other "mover." The un even distribution of natural resources is the pre requisite for their spatial redistribution through "transportation," "spread," and "circulation" (t'ung ilL) " a process performed by merchants.9 The aim of this circulation was believed to equalize the un even distribution of concrete natural resources in different regions. In other words, "circulation" was believed to realize a universalistic attitude toward the inhabitants of various extensions of space in their "equalization." Sang attributes this universalistic attitude to Heaven. Thus it is that Heaven and Earth appear in his discussion as cosmic agents of the circulation of goods. He says: 11

Of the five movers, the eastern quarter per tains to Wood, but in Tan-[yang and YU-] chang [commanderies] there are mountains containing gold and copper; the southern quarter pertains to Fire, but in Chiao-chih [commandery] there are rivers as big as a great sea; the western quarter pertains to Metal, but in Lung and Shu there are forests of famous timber; the northern quarter pertains to Water, but in Yu-tu there are lands of heaped up sand. This is how Heaven and Earth circulate myriad goods, equalizing those who have and those who have not. Pointing out that in some places there is a super abundance of wood or fish, whereas in the other re gions people have not enough timber even to make coffins or must satisfy themselves with poor vege table food, he proceeds: All the wealth of Heaven and Earth is indeed abundant, all the resources of mountains and seas are indeed rich, and yet the Hundred Clans suffer from want and shortage. Wealth and the means of expenditure are not sufficient because abundance and scarcity [of products] have not been equalized, and the wealth of All-under Heaven has not been scattered [among the people], (YTL ch. 3, p. 20)10 He also points out: As to those who live in the mountains or in the marshes, in places overgrown with wormwood or on poor, sterile lands, there is a possibility to equalize them by means of the circulation of wealth and goods. Thus it would not be that only those who have much [because of rich natu ral resources] suffer from scarcity. If every body lives in his own dwelling-place and eats his own food, then oranges and pummeloes would not be sold and the timber of [the states of] Wu and T'ang would not be used. (YTL ch. 3, P- 21) Since the circulation of goods is considered a means of realizing the universal ism of Heaven, it would be only natural that "circulation" would require the special attention of the sovereign imitating this universal ism. Indeed, for Sang, trade was first in troduced by the sovereigns of antiquity. This be lief is based on a passage from the Hsi-tz'u chuan praising the civilizing efforts of the mythical cul tural heroes, who "deduced" their innovations from certain hexagrams of the I Ching.H In several speeches in the Yen t'ieh lun Sang retells this pas sage, not identifying the cultural heroes by name nor the specific hexagrams in question. He says: Those in antiquity who have established a state and a ruling house, opened the way for the fun damental and secondary occupations, circulated goods tha< one had and others had not by means of markets, unified [prices], gathered scholars and other commoners, and collected myriad goods. The farmer, merchant, and chief of the artisans "each obtained what he desired, and returned home, having exchanged [his goods]." The Book of Changes says, "Holding to the thread of un alteration in their changes, they [i.e. the sage rulers] achieved that the people did not grow tired."12 (YTL, ch. 1, pp. 2-3) He also declares: "People depend upon the merchants for the circu lation [of goods] and upon the artisans for the manufacture [of articles]."13 This is the way sages have invented and "made" such [transport] facilities as "boats" and "oars" in oder to move unobstructedly through rivers and gulleys, "yoked oxen and harnessed horses" in order to travel over hills and plateaux; thus they "reached the most distant regions" and penetrated the re motest places [with their loads]; this is how they exchanged goods "for the benefit of the Hundred Clans."™ (YTL, ch. 1, p. 3) Later, Sang supports his view of the necessity for advance defence measures with a quotation from the Hsi-tz'u passage in question.15 That Sang repeatedly cites this passage with approval shows that he shared its ideas. According to Tflkei, the Hsi-tz'u text, which tries to deduce every great "change" from divine afflatus and attri butes the introduction of state-organized exchange to Shen-nung, imbues the ideal of such an exchange with divine status.1® For Sang, the icea of the sovereign's interference in economics (i.e. in the sphere of trade) is in accord with the principles of the world-order and is enhanced by the authority of the cultural heroes of antiquity. He therefore jus tifies the adoption of economic measures (i.e. salt and iron monopolies and his method for the "equali zation of transportation" of tax returns) by ancient precedents established by these heroes. After re telling the Hsi-tz'u passage, he continues: Therefore, [offices in charge of] salt and iron and of equalization of transportation are means of circulating accumulated wealth and helping the distressed. They are [the source] to which the myriad people^ look and from which they draw supplies. It is not proper to abolish them. (YTL, ch. 1, p. 3) It seems that both economic institutions estab lished by Sang to permit the sovereign interference in trade can be regarded as realizations of royal universal ism. The gradual introduction of the institution for the "equalization of transportation" (chUn shu j fft) in 115-110 B.C. absolved "Commandery Admini strators," "vassal kings" and "marquises" of the burdensome obligation of transporting tax returns in kind to the capital. As for the workings of this new system, suffice it to say that "offices in charge of the equalization of transportation" were estab lished in the corananderies and kingdoms. They took the responsibility for taxes in kind. Henceforth these taxes were paid only in local products which were abundant and fit for export. The "offices" themselves received and transported the produce to the regions where they were in demand.17 Thus the establishment of the chtln shu institution meant that control over part of the domain of transportation and circulation went to the state. This accounts for the Confucian attacks on the institution. In the words of Chu Hui (A.D. 6-89?), "The methods for the equalization of transportation do not differ from the speculation of merchants."18 Han histori ans saw the task of this institution as "circulating goods.
