Sima Qian’s Records on the Qin: Memorials, an Inscription, and a Revolt
events 221–209 B.C.E.; recorded by Sima Qian ca. 109–91 B.C.E.

TREATMENT Fresh translation for this reader from the public-domain classical Chinese text; five related selections from the same work.

Historical Context

Sima Qian (ca. 145–86 B.C.E.) was the court historian (Grand Scribe) of the Han dynasty, which replaced the Qin in 202 B.C.E. His Shiji (Records of the Grand Historian) is the main narrative source for the Qin empire. Chapter 6 is the “Basic Annals of the First Emperor of Qin”; chapter 48 is the “Hereditary House of Chen She,” the leader of the first great revolt against the Qin.

Sima Qian had access to Qin documents, and he copied into his history several texts that the Qin themselves produced, such as memorials (formal proposals to the throne) and the words of inscriptions the First Emperor set up on mountains. But he wrote about a century after the events, for a dynasty that justified its own rule by pointing to Qin’s failures. Some stories, especially the burying of scholars in 212 B.C.E., are doubted by modern historians. As you read, separate what Qin officials said, what Qin set in stone, and what the Han historian tells us happened.

Editorial note. Translated for this reader by the editor from the Chinese text of the Shiji on Chinese Wikisource. The five selections are complete passages unless [. . .] marks an omission; headings and dates are added. The Mount Langya inscription is rhymed verse in four-character lines in the original; it is translated here as prose lines.
Document Text

1. Kings or commanderies? The debate of 221 B.C.E. (Shiji 6)

Chancellor Wang Wan and others said: “The feudal lords have just been destroyed, and the lands of Yan, Qi, and Chu are far away. If no kings are set up there, there will be no one to keep them in order. We ask that you establish your sons as kings. We hope Your Majesty will agree.” The First Emperor passed the proposal down to his ministers for discussion, and all of them thought it was a good idea.

The Commandant of Justice, Li Si, argued: “Kings Wen and Wu of Zhou enfeoffed a great many sons, younger brothers, and members of their own clan. But later their descendants grew distant from one another and attacked each other like enemies. The feudal lords made war on one another in turn, and the Son of Heaven of Zhou could not stop them. Now, thanks to Your Majesty’s divine power, all within the seas has been unified and made into commanderies and counties. If your sons and meritorious officials are rewarded generously from the public taxes, that is quite enough, and they will be easy to control. When the realm has no thought of rebellion, that is the way to peace. Setting up feudal lords would not be beneficial.”

The First Emperor said: “The whole world has suffered from endless war because there were lords and kings. Thanks to the ancestors, the world has just been settled. To set up states again would be to plant the seeds of war. Seeking peace and rest that way would be difficult indeed. The Commandant of Justice is right.”

He divided the realm into thirty-six commanderies, and in each commandery he placed a governor, a military commander, and an inspector. He renamed the common people the “black-headed people.” There was a great feast. He collected the weapons of the whole realm, brought them to Xianyang, and melted them down to make bells, bell-stands, and twelve metal statues, each weighing a thousand dan, which were set up in the palace. He unified the laws and standards, the weights and measures, and the units of length. Carts were given axles of the same width. Writing was made uniform in its characters.

2. The Mount Langya inscription, 219 B.C.E. (Shiji 6)

[The First Emperor climbed Mount Langya on the coast of Shandong, moved thirty thousand households there, built a terrace, and set up a stone inscribed to praise the virtue of Qin. Sima Qian copies the inscription:]

In the twenty-eighth year, the August Emperor made a new beginning.
He set right the laws and standards, the guiding threads of all things.
He made clear the affairs of men, so that fathers and sons live in harmony. Sage, wise, humane, and just, he made plain the principles of the Way.
He went east to calm the eastern lands and to inspect the soldiers.
His great work complete, he came down to the sea.
The merit of the August Emperor is to encourage the basic work [of farming]. He honors agriculture and does away with the secondary [trades], and the black-headed people grow rich.
All under the whole of Heaven share one mind and one purpose.
Implements are made to a single measure; writing is done in the same characters. Wherever the sun and moon shine, wherever boats and carts can carry,
All complete their allotted lives, and none fails to get what he desires. [. . .] He removes doubts and fixes the laws, so that all know what to avoid.
Regional lords are given separate duties, and every government task runs easily. [. . .] Noble and humble, high and low, do not step out of their proper order. [. . .] The virtue of the August Emperor keeps the four corners of the earth secure. He punishes disorder and removes harm, brings benefit and good fortune. [. . .] The black-headed people live in peace and do not need to take up arms. The six degrees of kin protect one another, and there are no robbers or bandits. [. . .] Within the six directions, all is the August Emperor’s land.
West it reaches the Flowing Sands; south to the lands where doors face north; East it holds the Eastern Sea; north it goes beyond Daxia.
Wherever human footprints reach, there is no one who is not his subject. His achievements surpass the Five Emperors; his kindness reaches even oxen and horses. None fails to receive his virtue; each lives at peace in his own home.

3. The burning of the books, 213 B.C.E. (Shiji 6)

[At a banquet in 213 B.C.E., the chief court scholar praised the First Emperor for turning the feudal states into commanderies. . . .]

Chunyu Yue, a court scholar from Qi, came forward and said: “I have heard that the kings of Shang and Zhou ruled for more than a thousand years because they enfeoffed their sons, younger brothers, and meritorious officials as supports and props. Now Your Majesty possesses all within the seas, but your sons and younger brothers are mere commoners. If suddenly there should appear ministers like Tian Chang or the Six Ministers of Jin, with no one to help, how could you be saved? I have never heard of anything that lasted long without taking antiquity as its teacher. Now Qingchen flatters you to your face and makes Your Majesty’s errors worse. He is not a loyal minister.” The First Emperor passed the matter down for discussion.

Chancellor Li Si said: “The Five Emperors did not copy one another, and the Three Dynasties did not imitate one another; each governed in its own way. This was not because they rejected one another, but because the times had changed. Now Your Majesty has created a great enterprise and established a merit that will last ten thousand generations. This is certainly not something that foolish scholars can understand. And what Yue speaks of are the affairs of the Three Dynasties—why should they be taken as a model? In earlier times the feudal lords competed with one another and generously welcomed wandering scholars. Now the realm is settled, and the laws and orders come from a single source. Common households should devote their strength to farming and crafts; men of learning should study the laws and prohibitions. Yet these scholars do not learn from the present but study the past in order to criticize the present age, and they confuse and stir up the black-headed people.

“Your servant the Chancellor, Li Si, at the risk of death, says: In ancient times the world was scattered and in disorder, and no one could unify it. That is why the feudal lords rose up together. In their speeches they all praised the past in order to harm the present and dressed up empty words in order to confuse the facts. Each man valued what he had learned privately and used it to criticize what his superiors had established. Now the August Emperor has united the world, distinguished black from white, and fixed a single authority. Yet those with private learning join together to criticize the teaching of the law. When they hear that an order has been issued, each judges it by his own learning. At court they disapprove in their hearts; outside they debate it in the streets. They win fame by boasting before the ruler, gain distinction by holding different views, and lead the people below to spread slander. If this is not forbidden, the ruler’s power will decline above and factions will form below. It is best to forbid it.

“I request that all records of the historians other than the records of Qin be burned. Except for those held by the court scholars in their official duties, anyone in the realm who dares to keep the Classic of Poetry, the Classic of Documents, or the writings of the Hundred Schools must hand them over to the governor or commander to be burned together. Anyone who dares to discuss the Poetry or the Documents with another shall be executed in the marketplace. Anyone who uses the past to criticize the present shall be executed with his whole family. Officials who see or know of such cases and do not report them shall share the same guilt. Anyone who has not burned his books thirty days after the order is issued shall be tattooed and sent to build walls. Books not to be destroyed are those on medicine, divination, and the planting of trees. Those who wish to study the laws and orders should take officials as their teachers.” The imperial decision was: “Approved.”

4. The scholars of Xianyang, 212 B.C.E. (Shiji 6)

Master Hou and Master Lu [two men who had been seeking elixirs of immortality for the emperor] plotted together, saying: “The First Emperor is by nature stubborn and headstrong, and he trusts only himself. Having risen from a feudal lord to unite the world, he has everything he wants and thinks no one since antiquity has equaled him. He relies only on officials in charge of prisons, and they are the ones who enjoy his favor. Though there are seventy court scholars, they only fill the posts and are not used. The chancellor and the great ministers just receive decisions that have already been made and depend on the emperor for everything. The emperor delights in using punishment and execution to show his power, and so everyone in the realm fears guilt, clings to his salary, and no one dares to be fully loyal. The emperor never hears about his own faults and grows more arrogant every day, while those below are cowed and deceive him to keep his favor. [. . .] All affairs of the realm, large and small, are decided by the emperor. He even weighs the documents on a scale, setting a quota to read day and night, and he does not rest until he has met it. Someone this greedy for power cannot be sought out for elixirs of immortality.” Then they ran away.

When the First Emperor heard that they had fled, he was furious. [. . .] He had the censors investigate all the scholars. The scholars informed on one another, and the emperor personally chose more than 460 who had broken the prohibitions and had them all buried in pits at Xianyang, making it known to the whole realm as a warning for later times. [. . .] His eldest son, Fusu, protested: “The realm has only just been settled, and the black-headed people in distant places have not yet come to terms with it. The scholars all recite and follow Confucius, and now Your Majesty binds them with heavy laws. I fear the realm will not be at peace.” The First Emperor was angry and sent Fusu north to supervise [the general] Meng Tian in Shang commandery.

5. The rising of Chen She, 209 B.C.E. (Shiji 48)

In the seventh month of the first year of the Second Emperor [209 B.C.E.], nine hundred men from the poor quarters were conscripted to guard the frontier at Yuyang, and they camped at Dazexiang. Chen Sheng and Wu Guang were both in the group and served as squad leaders. It happened that there was a heavy rain, the roads became impassable, and they judged that they would be late. Under the law, being late meant beheading for all. Chen Sheng and Wu Guang plotted together, saying: “If we run away now, we die; if we start a great undertaking, we also die. Since we die either way, why not die for a kingdom?”

Chen Sheng said: “The world has suffered under Qin for a long time. I have heard that the Second Emperor is a younger son and should not have come to the throne; the one who should have succeeded is Prince Fusu. Because Fusu criticized his father several times, the emperor sent him away to command troops. Now some say that the Second Emperor has killed him although he did nothing wrong. Many common people have heard of his worth but do not know he is dead. Xiang Yan was a general of Chu who won many victories and loved his soldiers, and the people of Chu remember him fondly. Some think he is dead, others that he is in hiding. If we now use our followers and falsely claim to be Prince Fusu and Xiang Yan, and call on the world to follow us, many should answer.” [. . .]

[After the two men staged omens and killed their escorting officers, Chen Sheng addressed the conscripts:] “All of you have been caught by the rain and are already late. Being late means beheading. Even if we were not beheaded, six or seven out of every ten men sent to guard the frontier die there anyway. A brave man either does not die, or if he dies, he dies making a great name. Kings, lords, generals, and ministers—are they a special breed?” The conscripts all said, “We will obey your command.” [. . .] Chen She then made himself king, with the title “Expanding Chu.”

At this time, in the commanderies and counties that had suffered under Qin officials, people everywhere punished their local officials and killed them in response to Chen She.
