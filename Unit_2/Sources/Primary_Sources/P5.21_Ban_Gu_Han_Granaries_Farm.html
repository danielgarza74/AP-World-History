Ban Gu on Han Granaries, Farm Policy, and Famine (Han shu 24A)
events of 155 B.C.E. to the reign of Emperor Ai (7–1 B.C.E.); written by Ban Gu in the Han shu, late 1st century C.E.

Historical Context

The Han shu (Book of Han), the history of the Western Han dynasty (206 B.C.E.–9 C.E.), was written mainly by Ban Gu (32–92 C.E.) and finished after his death with help from his sister Ban Zhao. Its chapter 24, the treatise on “Food and Money,” traces how Han rulers taxed, stored, and moved grain and how land and wealth were divided. These passages run from the light taxes and full granaries of the mid-second century B.C.E., through Emperor Wu’s costly wars (141–87 B.C.E.) and his late turn back to farming under the official Zhao Guo, to the “ever-normal granaries” proposed by Geng Shouchang under Emperor Xuan (usually dated 54 B.C.E.), which bought grain when it was cheap and sold it when it was dear. Under Emperor Yuan, Confucian ministers had the granaries abolished. The chapter’s two best-known proposals for limiting what great families could own, Dong Zhongshu’s under Emperor Wu and the plan of 7 B.C.E. that the court shelved, are printed in P5.6; this entry gives the surrounding record of what the state actually did.

Ban Gu was a court historian of the restored Eastern Han, writing decades to centuries after these events, and he judges as he narrates: he praises frugal rulers, blames Emperor Wu’s wars for driving people off the land, and ends his first passage with a moral rule, “When things reach their fullness they decline.” The record shows the state helping farmers in specific ways: tools, teaching, grain bought to hold prices up, parkland given to the poor, relief grain. It also shows limits: under the loose laws of the early Han “gangs of the powerful” came to rule the villages, officials ended the granaries “so as not to compete with the people for profit,” and famine returned under several emperors. Ask what the state was protecting farmers from (low prices, bad harvests, war costs, or great families), and who at court argued for and against each measure.

Editorial note. This is a new translation prepared for this reader by the editor from the Chinese text of the Han shu, chapter 24A (“Shihuo zhi,” Treatise on Food and Money, part 1), as transcribed on Chinese Wikisource (the link opens the page 漢書/卷024, which carries both parts of the treatise), without commentary. It has not been checked by a specialist; read fine points of wording with caution. Square brackets mark words added for sense, and two bracketed notes in the document stand in for passages already printed in P5.6 (Dong Zhongshu’s memorial and the proposal of 7 B.C.E.), so that this entry does not repeat them. A mu is a small unit of land, a fraction of an acre; the shi and the hu are grain measures of roughly 20 liters each. Omitted and marked [. . .]: in the first passage, the sale of ranks and the redemption of crimes for grain after a drought, the building of horse parks and palaces, the coins and grain rotting in the capital, horses in the lanes, and a sentence on the people’s respect for law; Dong Zhongshu’s memorial; the description of how alternating fields were laid out, the yield figures, the first places where the method was taught, and the list of regions that took it up; Geng Shouchang’s proposal to triple the sea tax and Xiao Wangzhi’s story that the fish would not come out when the government fished; a note on Cai Gui, who rose to high office for encouraging farming; Shi Dan, Kong Guang, and He Wu’s proposal; and everything after the reign of Emperor Ai (Wang Mang’s reforms, partly printed in P5.6). Uncertain readings: 為吏者長子孫 is taken in its usual sense, that officials stayed in office long enough to raise sons and grandsons; 居官者以為姓號, that office-holders took the title of their office as a family name; 役財驕溢, that people used their wealth arrogantly and without restraint; 內興功利 is rendered “schemes of merit and profit”; 亡以趨澤, “had no means to plow in time while the soil was moist”; 庸輓犁, that people hired one another (庸 read as 傭) to pull plows; 二千石 (officials of two thousand shi) is rendered “commandery governors”; and 齊三服官 is rendered “the Three Offices of Robes in Qi,” imperial workshops for garments. “People ate one another” renders 人相食 literally.
Document Text

Han shu 24A: plenty, and the rise of local strongmen (155 to ca. 140 B.C.E.)

Thirteen years later, in the second year of Emperor Jing [155 B.C.E.], the people were ordered to pay half the land tax, [so that the tax was] one-thirtieth. [. . .] Yet [the emperor] repeatedly ordered the officials to make farming their concern, and the people came to take pleasure in their work. In the seventy years down to the beginning of Emperor Wu’s reign, the state had no [great] undertakings. Unless there was flood or drought, the people had enough and their households were well supplied; the granaries and storehouses of the cities and the countryside were all full, and the treasuries had wealth to spare. [. . .] Those who kept the village gates ate fine grain and meat; those who served as officials [stayed in office long enough to] raise sons and grandsons; those who held office took [the title of the office] as their family name. [. . .] At this time the net [of the law] was loose and the people were rich. [People] used their wealth arrogantly and without restraint, and some even became annexers [of land] and gangs of the powerful who ruled by force in the villages. Members of the imperial clan held land, and from the high ministers and grandees down, [men] competed in extravagance; in their houses, carriages, and clothing they encroached on [the privileges of] their superiors without limit. When things reach their fullness they decline; that is certainly how they change.

Han shu 24A: the cost of Emperor Wu’s wars (141–87 B.C.E.)

After this, [the state] made war on the peoples of the four quarters abroad and pursued schemes of merit and profit at home; labor service and expenses rose together, and the people left the fundamental occupation [farming]. [. . .]

[Dong Zhongshu’s two proposals to the emperor, on planting wheat and on limiting landholding and doing away with slaves, are omitted here; the second is printed in P5.6.]

After [Dong] Zhongshu died, the costs of [military] exploits grew ever greater, the empire was emptied and drained, and people again ate one another.

Han shu 24A: Zhao Guo and the “alternating fields” (ca. 89 B.C.E.)

In his last years Emperor Wu regretted his wars of conquest, and so he enfeoffed the chancellor as Marquis Who Enriches the People. He issued an edict saying: “The task of the present lies in working hard at farming.” He made Zhao Guo Commandant for Grain Collection. [Zhao] Guo was able to practice “alternating fields,” with three furrows to each mu; [furrow and ridge] changed places each year, so it was called “alternating fields.” It was an ancient method. [. . .] The Grand Minister of Agriculture assigned skilled slave artisans and assistants to make farm tools for it. The commandery governors sent county magistrates, the Thrice Venerable, the Diligent Farmers, and village elders who were good at farming to receive the farm tools and learn how to plow, sow, and tend the young plants. Some of the people suffered from having few oxen and had no means to plow in time while the soil was moist, so Guang, magistrate of Pingdu, taught Guo [a way] to pull plows by human strength. Guo memorialized that Guang be made his assistant, and [they] taught the people to hire one another to pull plows together. In general, [a team] with many people could plow thirty mu a day, and [one] with few, thirteen mu; because of this much land was opened for farming. [. . .]

Han shu 24A: Geng Shouchang and the ever-normal granaries (57–54 B.C.E.)

By the time of Emperor Zhao, the refugees had gradually returned, the fields were more and more opened up, and there were considerable stores. When Emperor Xuan took the throne, [. . .] the common people were settled on their land, there were abundant harvests for several years, and grain fell to five cash a shi, so that farmers made little profit. At that time Geng Shouchang, Assistant to the Grand Minister of Agriculture, who was good at calculation and able to reckon costs and gains, won the emperor’s favor. In the Wufeng era [57–54 B.C.E.] he memorialized: “By precedent, four million hu of grain from east of the passes are shipped each year to supply the capital, using sixty thousand soldiers. It would be fitting to buy grain in the commanderies of the Three Adjuncts, Hongnong, Hedong, Shangdang, and Taiyuan, enough to supply the capital; this could save more than half of the transport soldiers from east of the passes.” [. . .] The Son of Heaven followed all his plans. The Imperial Counselor Xiao Wangzhi memorialized: “[. . .] The stirrings of yin and yang and the responses of things of the same kind are so in all affairs. Now Shouchang wants to buy and ship grain from nearby, within the passes, building granaries and fitting out boats at a cost of more than two hundred million [cash]. It is a work that sets the multitude in motion, and I fear it will produce the vapor of drought, and the people will suffer the disaster. Shouchang is practiced in reckoning costs down to the smallest fraction, but his deep plans and long views are truly not to be trusted. It would be fitting to keep things as before for now.” The emperor did not listen. The shipping did prove convenient, and Shouchang then proposed that the frontier commanderies all build granaries, buying [grain] at a raised price when grain was cheap, to benefit the farmers, and selling it at a lowered price when grain was dear. They were called “ever-normal granaries.” The people found them convenient. The emperor then issued an edict granting Shouchang the rank of Marquis Within the Passes. [. . .]

Han shu 24A: the granaries abolished (Emperor Yuan, r. 48–33 B.C.E.)

When Emperor Yuan took the throne, there were great floods across the empire, worst in eleven commanderies east of the passes. In the second year [47 B.C.E.], the land of Qi had famine; grain cost more than three hundred [cash] a shi, many people starved to death, and in Langye commandery people ate one another. Many of the Confucian scholars in office said that the salt and iron offices, the agricultural offices of Beijia, and the ever-normal granaries could be abolished, so as not to compete with the people for profit. The emperor followed their advice and abolished them all. He also abolished the guards of the Jianzhang and Ganquan palaces, the wrestling shows, and the Three Offices of Robes in Qi, reduced the forbidden parks to give [land] to the poor, and cut by half the soldiers guarding the temples of the kings. He also cut five hundred soldiers in Guanzhong, and shipped grain to relieve the destitute and lend to them. Later, when expenses were not enough, he restored the salt and iron offices alone.

Han shu 24A: Emperors Cheng and Ai (33–1 B.C.E.)

In the time of Emperor Cheng, the empire had no wars and was said to be at peace and happy, but custom was extravagant and no one cared about laying up stores. In the second year of Yongshi [15 B.C.E.], the kingdom of Liang and Pingyuan commandery suffered floods year after year, and people ate one another; the regional inspectors, governors, and chancellors were dismissed for it.

[Shi Dan’s proposal of 7 B.C.E. to limit land and slaves, the limits that Kong Guang and He Wu drew up, and the shelving of the plan are omitted here; they are printed in P5.6.]

The stores of the palaces, parks, and treasuries were already extravagant; and although the wealth of the common people did not match [that under Emperors] Wen and Jing, the households and population of the empire were at their greatest. [. . .]
