Emperor Xiaowen of the Northern Wei: The Move to Luoyang and the Reform of Xianbei Customs
events of 493–499 C.E.; recorded in the Wei shu (completed 554 C.E.) and the Zizhi tongjian (1084 C.E.)
 
TOC FIT Comparing Empires: The Collapse of Empires, on China’s division and reunification (textbook pp. 122–125).
WHY HERE An emperor from a Xianbei dynasty of steppe origin moves his capital to the old Chinese heartland and orders his own people to give up their dress, their speech at court, and their surnames, while some of his nobles and his own son resist.
TREATMENT Translated for this reader by the editor from the Chinese texts of the Wei shu (chapter 7B) and the Zizhi tongjian (chapters 139–140) on Chinese Wikisource, selected passages.
ESSAY PROMPTS 3.6
ARGUMENT ROLES 3.6.1 Supports; 3.6.2 Qualifies
Historical Context

The Northern Wei (386–534 C.E.) was founded by the Tuoba clan of the Xianbei, a people of the steppe frontier, and by 439 it ruled all of north China. For a century its capital was Pingcheng (modern Datong), near the steppe in the region the texts call Dai, and its court kept Xianbei language, dress, and rites, such as a sacrifice to Heaven in the western suburb, alongside Chinese-style offices. Emperor Xiaowen (r. 471–499) ruled under the regency of his grandmother, Empress Dowager Feng (Wenming), until her death in 490. In 493 he marched south with a huge army, as if against the southern dynasty (the Qi), and at Luoyang, the ruined capital of the Han and Jin, his officials begged him to stop; later historians, including Sima Guang, present the campaign as a way to force the move. Over the next three years he banned Hu clothing (Hu was the Chinese word for northern and western “barbarian” peoples), banned northern speech at court for officials under thirty, changed the royal surname from Tuoba to Yuan, and ranked Xianbei clans with the great Chinese lineages. Not everyone agreed: the crown prince tried to flee north and was deposed and later forced to die, and a plot by the Xianbei noble Mu Tai at the old capital was put down. The Northern Wei never conquered the south. A rising of its northern frontier garrisons in the 520s led to its split in 534, and the Sui, who reunified China in 589, came out of the mixed Xianbei and Chinese elite of its successor states.

Two histories are used here. The Wei shu (Book of Wei), compiled by Wei Shou and completed in 554 for the Northern Qi, a successor state, gives the emperor’s acts by date, mostly without speeches. The Zizhi tongjian (Comprehensive Mirror for Aid in Government), a chronicle finished by Sima Guang in 1084, draws on the Wei shu and other early records and supplies the speeches; it dates events by the calendar of the southern dynasties, so its months can differ by one from the Wei shu. Both authors were Chinese scholar-officials who admired the emperor’s turn toward Chinese ways, and the dialogues are their versions of what was said, written down decades or centuries later. Separate what the texts record as acts (the march, the move, the edicts, the new surname) from what they say about motives, and notice the signs that the rules were resisted or ignored: “many of the people of the state were displeased,” women in the new capital still wore northern dress, and the crown prince wore Hu clothing in secret.

Editorial note. This is a new translation prepared for this reader by the editor from the Chinese texts of the Wei shu, chapter 7B (“Gaozu ji,” Annals of Gaozu, the temple name of Emperor Xiaowen), and the Zizhi tongjian, chapters 139 (Jianwu 1, 494 C.E.) and 140 (Jianwu 2–3, 495–496 C.E.), as transcribed on Chinese Wikisource, without commentary. It has not been checked by a specialist; read fine points of wording with caution. Square brackets mark words added for sense. Days are named by the sixty-day cycle (for example, gengwu) as in the original, and months are lunar months. The selections are, in order: Wei shu 7B, Taihe 17 (493), from the eighth and ninth months; Zizhi tongjian 139, the debate at Pingcheng, the end of the western-suburb sacrifice, and the dress edict (which the Wei shu records as “reforming the regulations for dress” in the twelfth month of Taihe 18, the first days of 495); Zizhi tongjian 140, the conversation on speech and the speech edict, the burial edict, the surname edict, and the opposition of 496; and Wei shu 7B, Taihe 23 (499), from the emperor’s last charge. The Wei shu records the speech edict (sixth month, jihai), the burial edict, and the change of surname (Taihe 20, first month) in nearly the same terms. Omitted and marked [. . .]: the Wei shu’s notices of grants, envoys, and appointments between these entries; in the Pingcheng debate, Tuoba Pi’s call for divination and the emperor’s answer, the list of earlier Tuoba capitals, a note on the speakers’ families, and the assignment of who would move and who would stay; in the conversation on speech, further exchanges with the princes, the emperor’s rebuke of Li Chong (who had said that whatever the emperor speaks becomes correct), and his complaint that officials agreed at court and disobeyed afterward; a ruling on burials before the burial edict; most of the list of changed surnames, and the passages that follow on the emperor’s marriages to daughters of the four great Chinese lineages and on ranking Xianbei clans with them; the murder of the prince’s tutor and the end of the prince’s flight; Mu Tai’s career and plot; and most of the emperor’s last charge. Wikisource slips read silently: 徇 for 恂 (Xun) and 於果 for 于果 (Yu Guo). Uncertain readings: 勝殘之運 is rendered “a time when cruelty is being overcome,” an echo of Analects 13.11; 國人 (“people of the state”) probably means the Xianbei and other people of Dai, not all subjects; 夾領小袖 (“tight collars and small sleeves”) names northern dress, but the exact cut is uncertain; 黃口之色 on Wikisource is read as 黃中之色, “the yellow color of the center,” the reading this edict is usually given in, without checking a printed edition; 姓或重複 is taken to mean compound surnames; 正音 (“correct pronunciation”) means the Chinese of the court; and 甌吳 (Ou and Wu), old names for the southeast, stand for the southern dynasty. The saying “If names are not correct . . .” abridges Analects 13.3.
Document Text

Wei shu 7B: the southern campaign halts at Luoyang (493 C.E.)

[Eighth month.] [. . .] On the jichou day the imperial carriage set out from the capital [Pingcheng] on a southern campaign, with more than a million foot soldiers and horsemen. [. . .]

[Ninth month.] [. . .] On the gengwu day [the emperor] went to Luoyang and toured all around the foundations of the old palaces. Turning to the officials attending him, the emperor said: “The Jin did not cultivate virtue, and their ancestral sacrifices soon fell. That [this city] has been laid waste to this degree grieves my heart.” Then he recited the poem “Shu li” [an ode of the Classic of Poetry lamenting a ruined capital] and wept over it. On the renshen day he viewed the Luo bridge, went to the Imperial Academy, and viewed the Stone Classics. [. . .] On the dingchou day, in military dress and holding a whip, he rode out on horseback. The assembled officials knocked their heads on the ground before his horse and begged him to halt the southern campaign, and the emperor then stopped. He then settled the plan to move the capital.

[. . .]

Zizhi tongjian 139: the debate at Pingcheng (494 C.E.)

[. . .] [At Pingcheng] he had the assembled officials debate in turn the gains and harms of moving the capital, each stating his own view. Mu Pi, the regional inspector of Yanzhou, said: “Now the four quarters are not yet settled, and it is not yet fitting to move the capital. Besides, if we go on campaign without horses, how shall we win?” The emperor said: “The stables and pastures are in Dai; why worry about having no horses? Now Dai lies north of Mount Heng, outside the Nine Provinces; it is no capital for an emperor or king.” The imperial secretary Yu Guo said: “Your servant does not hold that the land of Dai is better than the beauty of the Yi and the Luo [the rivers of Luoyang]. But since the former emperors [we] have lived here a long time, and the common people are content here; if all at once we move south, the feelings of the multitude will be unhappy.” [. . .] The emperor said: “[. . .] A true king makes all within the four seas his household, now in the south, now in the north; what fixed place is there? My distant ancestors lived for generations in the northern wilds. [. . .] I have the good fortune to live in a time when cruelty is being overcome; why should I alone not be allowed to move?” The officials did not dare speak again. [. . .]

Summer, fourth month, gengchen day: Wei abolished the sacrifice to Heaven in the western suburb.

[. . .]

Zizhi tongjian 139: the ban on northern dress (winter 494–495 C.E.)

The ruler of Wei wished to change the old customs. On the renyin day he issued an edict forbidding officials and commoners to wear Hu [northern] clothing. Many of the people of the state were displeased.

[. . .]

Zizhi tongjian 140: the ban on northern speech at court (495 C.E.)

The ruler of Wei wished to change northern customs. He summoned the assembled officials and said to them: “Do you wish me to follow the Shang and Zhou far back, or do you wish [me] not even to equal the Han and Jin?” Xi, Prince of Xianyang, replied: “The assembled officials wish Your Majesty to surpass the former kings.” [. . .] The emperor said: “‘If names are not correct, speech does not follow, and rites and music cannot flourish.’ Now I wish to cut off all northern speech and follow only the correct pronunciation. Those aged thirty and above have long been set in their habits, and it may be allowed that they cannot change all at once. Those under thirty who are now at court shall not be permitted to keep their old speech; if any do so on purpose, they shall be demoted or dismissed. [. . .]” [. . .] [The emperor] also rebuked the officials who had stayed behind in charge, saying: “Yesterday I saw from a distance women still wearing tight collars and small sleeves. Why have you not obeyed the earlier edict?” They all apologized for their offense. [. . .] In the sixth month, on the jihai day, he issued an edict: “No one may use northern speech at court. Those who violate this shall be removed from the offices they hold!”

[. . .] On the bingchen day an edict said: “When people who have moved to Luo die, they are to be buried south of the [Yellow] River and may not be returned to the north.” Thereupon the people of Dai who had moved south all became people of Luoyang in Henan.

[. . .]

Zizhi tongjian 140: the change of surname (496 C.E.)

The ruler of Wei issued an edict, holding: “Northerners call earth tuo and a sovereign ba. The ancestors of Wei came from the Yellow Emperor and ruled by the virtue of Earth; therefore they were the Tuoba clan. Earth is the yellow color of the center and the origin of the myriad things; it is fitting to change the surname to Yuan [‘origin’]. Among the meritorious officials and old clans who came from Dai, whose surnames are sometimes compound [that is, of two or more characters], all shall change them.” Thereupon for the first time the Baba clan was changed to Zhangsun, the Daxi clan to Xi, [. . .] the Buliugu clan to Lu, [. . .] the Dugu clan to Liu, [. . .]; the other changes are too many to record.

[. . .]

Zizhi tongjian 140: opposition (496 C.E.)

The Wei crown prince Xun did not like study. He was by nature stout and heavy and suffered from the heat of Henan, and he constantly longed to return north. The ruler of Wei gave him caps and robes [of court dress], but Xun often secretly wore Hu clothing. [. . .] In the eighth month, on the wuxu day, the emperor went to Mount Song. Xun plotted secretly with his attendants to call out light cavalry from the horse herds and flee to Pingcheng. [. . .]

[. . .] When the emperor moved south to Luoyang, those he trusted and relied on were mostly Confucian scholars of the Central Provinces, and the imperial clan and the people of Dai were often displeased. [. . .]

[. . .]

Wei shu 7B: the emperor’s last charge (499 C.E.)

[. . .] He gave his last charge to the chief ministers, saying: “[. . .] I moved the capital to lofty Song and set up the tripods by the He and the Chan, hoping to sweep clear Ou and Wu in the south [that is, the southern dynasty] and restore ritual to the myriad states [. . .]. Worn down and in distress, I perish early and cannot carry my purpose through. [. . .]”
