One example of this phenomenon would be the system of state land allocation and redistribution seen in the “Statutes on Households” (sec. 3.18) from Zhangjiashan tomb no. 247. This elaborate system, the precursor of the Tang ‘equal field’ ( jūntián 均田) system, was designed to allocate a certain amount of land to every commoner and rank holder in the empire on a minutely graded sliding scale and to limit total landholdings based on rank. Generated in response to demands for land from rank-holding veterans of the civil war at the founding of Han, this system may have been partially functional in the early Han, when so much land still lay abandoned, but would have been nearly impossible to implement as soon as the land supply became tight, especially in congested areas, such as near the capital. Tellingly, historical sources no longer mention regular land grants or limitations on holdings after the first few reigns of the Han dynasty. In her dissertation, Moonsil Lee Kim (2014) demonstrates that the generous monthly bestowal of food for the elderly, laid out in the “Statutes on Enrollment” (sec. 3.20) and later expanded by Emperor Wen, not only was impeded by official malfeasance but was nearly impossible to implement because feeding such a significant proportion of the population would have nearly bankrupted provinces and localities. She also shows that local officials negotiated and streamlined the implementation of other statutory requirements, such as those providing rations to officials at conveyance stations (see “Statutes on Food Rations at Conveyance Stations,” [sec. 3.12] no. 4). Though the statutes required granting different amounts of food to men of different rank or salary grade, contemporaneous records from conveyance stations demonstrate that all these men received the same amount of food when passing through.

The Place of the Zhangjiashan Legal Texts in the Development of Traditional Chinese Law

From Han times onward there are many cases where Qin law has been in use right up to the present (mid-seventeenth century CE). When Confucian scholars of this time and age mention the laws of Qin, they believe these to be the laws of a vanished dynasty, because they have never deeply investigated the point. —Gu Yanwu (1613–1682), Notes on Daily Accumulated Knowledge9

The Zhangjiashan legal texts do not represent an isolated early-Han expression of legal principles or legislation but reside in a continuous stream in the development of traditional Chinese law. They demonstrate a nearly comprehensive continuation of Qin legal norms and procedures into the early Han, with only minor modifications and innovations. They also reveal that Tang law relied significantly on its Han ancestor for both its basic principles and their concrete application, despite the intervening centuries of dynastic change and legal revision. Modern Chinese scholars frequently quote the phrase “the Han continued the institutions of the Qin” ( Hàn chéng Qín zhì 漢承秦制) when discussing how the Han inherited and carried forward many of the institutions of the Qin state and empire, and this statement is true of nearly every institution of the early Han, including taxation and labor policies, official titles, territorial administration, state sacrifices, and, of course, the position of Emperor. Nowhere is this phrase more valid than with respect to the laws and legal institutions of the Han Empire,10 and one of its earliest concrete expressions is found in the “Treatise on Punishments and Legal Principles” in the History of the Jin, in a specific reference to the actions of the first Han Chancellor of State, Xiao He, when he revised and reissued the Qin legal statutes. According to the accounts of Sima Qian and Ban Gu, when Liu Bang entered the Qin Capital Area in late December 207 BCE, he made a pact with the Qin elders to abolish all the laws of Qin and replace them with three simple laws: “(1) ‘Those who kill persons will be executed; for those who wound (2) or rob (3) persons, their crime will be matched [by a proportionate punishment].’ He rejected and removed the vexations and cruelties, and the host of the people greatly rejoiced.”11 Soon after, as Ban Gu relates, such simple laws were inadequate for holding back the waves of violence that ensued, as the war against the rival faction of Xiang Yu was only just beginning. Therefore, Liu Bang’s

Chancellor of State, Xiao He, “gathered together the laws of the Qin and chose those that were suitable for the times, and produced nine fascicles of statutes.”12 Most scholars view the so-called pact of three laws as an empty gesture, later Imperial propaganda, or pure historical fiction, for there is no evidence that Liu Bang’s agreement with the Qin elders was ever implemented, or that the event even occurred.13 After all, he soon lost control of the capital upon Xiang Yu’s arrival and was then sent to his fief in the southwest. When he returned soon after to reconquer the capital, a full civil war was raging. The laws may in fact have been simplified by the Han out of pure expediency, since the Qin legal system had stopped functioning and the Han had no power to implement anything nearly so complex. The more telling phrase in this account mentions that Xiao He “gathered together the laws of the Qin and chose those that were suitable for the times,” for it was Xiao He who in late 207 BCE supposedly gathered up as many Qin statutes, ordinances, maps, and registers as he could find in the offices of the Qin Chief Minister and Chief Prosecutor while other Han soldiers were looking for valuable booty.14 It is now quite clear from an examination of the Statutes and Ordinances of the Second Year and Book of Submitted Doubtful Cases texts that the early Han did not just selectively borrow some of the legal procedures and laws of the Qin but adopted them almost in their entirety, with only minor revisions to update the language or slightly modify some of the punishment levels. Other Qin rules and procedures were reintroduced over the next decade, as the Han gradually acquired the power and personnel needed to reinvigorate the moribund Qin legal system. In the procedural area, one record in the Book of Submitted Doubtful Cases text thoroughly demonstrates that the Han procedure for personal appeal of convictions was inherited from the Qin, with only minor modifications, mostly having to do with the timing of applying the mutilating punishments specified in the original sentence (see sec. 4.17). The procedure for the mature early-Han process is laid out clearly in “Statutes on the Composition of Judgments” (sec. 3.3) no. 20 (slip nos. 114–17). [. . .]
