Selection Overview

Earle proposes that Chaco Canyon was a chiefdom that followed a "corporate" political strategy, with relatively faceless leaders whose institutions were materialized in great houses and roads and financed by staple surpluses mobilized from a broad supporting population. He treats the regional flows of turquoise, shell, and copper bells as a decentralized reciprocal exchange that was not the basis of Chacoan power, while admitting that how leaders controlled food production "has not yet been defined."

ECONOMIC SUPPORT OF CHACO CANYON SOCIETY 27 Chaco Canyon Chiefdoms Colin Renfrew (1974) recognized that different insti- tutional forms of chiefdoms (a.k.a. "intermediate level societies") exist. He contrasted chiefdoms that accent individual leaders (and social stratification) vs. group identity. Peter Peregrine (this issue; Blan- ton et al. 1996) elaborates on Renfrew's insight in the distinction between corporate and networked strategies, noting that these are not alternatives but options that can be combined creatively to empha- size different institutional matrices structured by these organizing principles. Kinship, for example, both defines relationships among individuals, extending outward as a social web, and defines inward looking, landholding kin groups. Chiefdoms represent a wide variety of historically specific regional institutions using network and a corporate principle of organization. Networks define the relationships between indi- viduals. Individuals are important and their interre- lationships extend spatially as a web across the landscape. Drawing attention to individuals and their linkages, networks are materialized in burial monu- ments and in the possessions obtained through inter- personal reciprocity. The outward-reaching pattern of connections creates the peer-polity interaction spheres described by Renfrew (1982). Network- based institutions are extensive, and they are mate- rialized in gift exchanges and ceremonial displays, as in burial ritual. As discussed in a moment, wealth finance, which involves channeling objects symbol- izing esoteric knowledge and connections, are found in chiefdoms using a network strategy. Inasmuch as individuals are the network's nodes, individual dis- tinctiveness should be emphasized in competitive displays, housing, and burial ritual. Corporate groups define the relationships ofpeo- ple to resources, especially as the owners of pro- ductive land. Groups are the salient sociopolitical form, and they are materialized by such things as public ceremonies and monuments (DeMarrais et al. 1996). In chiefdoms, it is usual to establish ahier- archy of overlapping ownership/use rights in land such that a group's land is vested with its leader or chief. The chief maintains rights to allocate use rights within the group, but not to alienate resources outside of the group (Earle 1998). The ceremonies and monuments represent, or more precisely insti- tute, organizational forms in ways analogous to legal charters; they require the mobilization of labor for construction that defines the institution in a highly visible (public) and enduring form. Memory of the social construction and continued use of monuments probably provide one of the most important ele- ments of personal identity to groups. Corporate chiefdoms are characterized by constructed land- scapes of monuments, walls, and paths that map groups onto land. Often the mapping has a strong religious character; the land may be the place where the group emerged from the earth, and it may be the home for the group's ancestors. Burials often emphasize the group by placing members together in a common grave or cemetery. Counter to stereotypes of complex societies, rel- atively faceless leaders often characterize corporately organized societies. What this means is that the lead- ers are not represented in the arts and that they are not distinguished monumentally in burial practice. In eastern Polynesian chiefdoms that were based on corporate ownership of productive lands by chiefly lineages, chiefs were not given monumental burials, and lavish burial furniture was not present. The bur- ial and artistic representations could not be used to recognize the strong political hierarchies that we know existed historically. In Hawaii, for example, although chiefs wore special clothes identifying them with the gods-feather cloaks, headdresses, and spe- cial "fly swatters," in death their bodies were hidden away and not accompanied by special goods. Ma- terialization emphasized the corporate institution of the chiefdom and not its individualized leaders. Archaeological cases of what appear to be complex societies, like 'Ubaid, often have institution-defin- ing monuments without clear burial evidence for hierarchies. The monuments of Chaco Canyon fit reasonably well to the corporate model. The great houses and the road system suggest a complex institutional form materialized in the constructed landscape that struc- tured and focused the San Juan Basin on the Canyon (Figure 1; Lekson et. al 1988). The great houses of the Canyon are carefully planned built spaces that divided inside from out, visible (plaza) from con- cealed (kiva), and ceremonial from domestic. Most residential architecture seems to be found in the "small houses." In the great houses, residential and storage spaces were repetitive rooms; ceremonial construction included both plazas and many kivas.
