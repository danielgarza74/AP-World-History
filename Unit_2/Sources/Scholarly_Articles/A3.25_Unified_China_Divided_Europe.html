Selection Overview

Ko, Koyama, and Sng, economists, build a model in which a severe threat from one direction (the steppe) pushed China toward political unification, while the many smaller threats Europe faced from several directions kept it fragmented, and they support it with data on nomadic attacks and Chinese unification. They argue that "China's perennial steppe problem was an important driver of its recurring unification," while Europe's many threats "doomed the Roman empire" and blocked later unification, and that institutions such as the Catholic Church and city-states grew out of this early fragmentation and then reinforced it.

INTRODUCTION Since Montesquieu, scholars have attributed Europe's success to its political fragmentation (Montesquieu, 1989; Mokyr, 1990; Diamond, 1997; Jones, 2003). Nevertheless, throughout much of history, the most economically developed region of the world was China, which was typically a unified empire. This contrast poses a puzzle that has important implications for our understanding of the origins of modern economic growth: Why was Europe perennially fragmented after the collapse of Rome? Why was political centralization an equilibrium for most of Chinese history? Can this fundamental difference in political institutions account for important disparities in Chinese and European growth patterns? This article proposes a unified framework based on Eurasian geography to (a) help explain the different political equilibria in China and Europe and (b) explore the economic consequences of political centralization and fragmentation. Historically, Europe faced periodic invasions from Scandinavia, Central Asia, the Middle East, and North Africa. By contrast, although China was relatively isolated from the rest of Eurasia, it had to confront a severe recurring threat on its northern frontier due to its relative proximity to the Eurasian steppe. We develop a Hoteling- style model to show that a severe unidirectional external threat undermines the fiscal viability of small states and thus provides an impetus toward political centralization.
