Selection Overview

Trubitt argues that the changes at Cahokia around 1200 C.E., usually read as decline, mark a shift in how elites held power: from a "corporate" strategy that celebrated communal groups through mound building and collective ritual to a "network" strategy in which individual elites displayed status through prestige goods. Household evidence shows the greatest status differences and the most centralized shell-bead working after 1200, so she concludes that elite control of craft production, "if present, was a late phenomenon."

A rchaeologists researching complex societies look for indications of complexity along sev- eral dimensions. One line of investigation is to look for evidence of social differentiation as an indicator of a heterogeneous society, made up of many parts. Archaeologists have examined mortu- ary treatment, theorizing that the status of individu- als and groups during life may be reflected in their treatment at death (e.g., Binford 1971; Brown 1971, 1981; Peebles and Kus 1977; see also Hodder 1982). Archaeologists have also looked for evidence of dif- ferences in household activities and residential archi- tecture to address questions of differences in social status and wealth within societies (e.g., Costin and Earle 1989; Haviland 1981; Scarborough and Robertson 1986). A second line of investigation is to use evidence for monumental constructions and craft specializa- tion as evidence of the control of labor and resources, an indicator of centralization and hierarchical org- anization. Pyramids and earthworks are large and vis- ible monuments that have been used by archaeologists as an indication of the ability of one sector of soci- ety to control the labor of others (e.g., O'Brien 1989; Peebles and Kus 1977; Trigger 1990). Archaeologists have begun to recognize more variation in complex soci- eties and to reevaluate the role of centralization, hier- archy, and differentiation (e.g., Crumley 1995; White and Pigott 1996). There has been questioning, then, of what our indications of complexity really mean. In recent years I have examined the archaeolog- ical record of the Mississippian-period Cahokia polity with a particular focus on the development of this complex society, examining the evidence for sta- tus differentiation and craft production in the Amer- ican Bottom region of the Central Mississippi River Valley. The results of my initial analysis (Trubitt 1996) were surprising to me. I found a pattern of maximal social and economic differentiation among households in the archaeological record from late in the sequence, the time usually thought to be Cahokia's decline. Since then I have been looking more closely at the later part of the Cahokia sequence, in particular the Moorehead phase (AD. 1200-1275, Figure 1), and I find that the changes cannot simply be characterized as decline. Using Blanton et al.'s (1996) "dual-processual" model (see also Feinman 1995), I suggest that the earlier Cahokian society is characterized by a "corporate" leadership strategy, and that there is a transition to a "network" leader- ship strategy in the later part of the Cahokian sequence (Trubitt 1997a; see also Peregrine 1998). "Corporate" versus "Network" Strategies Recent discussion by Feinman (1995) and Blanton and colleagues (1996) of "corporate" versus "net- work" or "exclusionary" strategies of leadership or modes of political economy has focused attention on the different ways that leaders build their power bases in complex societies. Blanton et al.'s (1996) "dual- processual theory" focuses on the same kinds of dis- tinctions that have previously been identified as "staple" vs. "wealth finance," and "group-oriented" vs. "individualizing chiefdoms" (D'Altroy and Earle 1985; Renfrew 1974). Their focus is on power and its control: whether elites monopolize sources of power or whether power is shared across different groups, what the sources of power are, and whether political action is oriented to external exchange net- works or to relations within the group (see also Earle 1997; Hayden 1995; Mann 1986). In the corporate strategy, power is shared across different groups or segments of society, and there are corporate political structures, often kin-based (e.g., clans or lineages). The interdependence of these groups is emphasized through collective ritual events, often based on fertility, that may involve collection of surplus food and monumental public construction. Economic and social differences within the group are downplayed. In contrast, in the network strategy, indi- vidual leaders try to monopolize power and control the sources of power (such as prestige goods produc- tion). They build networks of supporters not only through kin relations but through patron-client rela- tionships and long-distance exchange networks. Dif- ferences in prestige and wealth between individuals are accentuated and marked. Feinman (1995) con- trasts these "corporate" versus "network" strategies of leadership as alternative paths towards complexity: The corporate mode (encompassing what Renfrew called group-oriented forms) empha- sizes collective ritual (and its potential manipu- lation), public construction, integrated social segments, the importance of kinship affiliation, and relatively suppressed economic differentia- tion (more egalitarian access patterns). In con- trast, the network mode (encompassing Renfrew's individualizing polities) places greatest significance on individual prestige and wealth accumulation, personal networks, long- distance exchange, exotic wealth exchange, and the specialized manufacture of status related craft goods [1995:268].
