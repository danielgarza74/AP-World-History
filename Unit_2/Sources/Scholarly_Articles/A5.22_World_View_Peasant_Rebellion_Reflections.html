Selection Overview

Liu surveys how historians in the People's Republic of China after 1977 reversed the Maoist praise of peasant rebels, now stressing that rebel leaders shared the "monarchism, fatalism, and the hierarchical social arrangement" of the old order (historiography). Writing from a non-Marxist view, he welcomes the more realistic appraisal but asks whether it has "gone too far the other way," neglecting peasant culture and the concrete gains that revolts may have won; his examples come mainly from the Ming and Qing.

XL, No. 2 JOURNAL OF ASIAN STUDIES FEBRUARY 1981 World View and Peasant Rebellion: Reflections on Post-Mao Historiography KWANG-CHING Liu E ven a casual reading of the historical articles that have appeared in the scholarly journals of the People's Republic of China (PRC) since 1977 yields an impression of vitality and deep stirrings in the historical profession there. With the passing of the fear generated by the Cultural Revolution and the Gang of Four, a new era of comparatively uninhibited inquiry arrived. The great emphasis the authorities laid on science, technology, and economic development encouraged empiricism in scholar- ship as well as in policy. Authoritatively ordained ideas, such as "Practice is the sole criterion for testing truth" (Shijian shi jianyan zhenli di weiyi biao zhun) and "Seeking truth from facts" (Shishi qiushi), found a ready response among scholars. Within limits, the PRC historical writings after 1977 may even be characterized as iconoclas- tic. This is most evident in the historical writings that question the importance of class struggle itself and in the debunking of the "great man" theory in the interpreta- tion of rebellions and revolutions. There is a new interest in foreign influences in China, but even more remarkable is a new interest in the role of intellectual movements in history-from the Renaissance and the Enlightenment in European history to China's own May Fourth Movement. Not only Chen Duxiu but also Hu Shi have received qualified praise, and their roles in history are being reevaluated.2 In this generally liberating historiography, a fascinating development is a new appraisal of peasant rebellions in history--movements that have been variously described as peasant revolutions (nongmin geming) or, more commonly nowadays, as peasant wars (nongmin zhanzheng). Long a focus of historical scholarship in the PRC, the peasant rebellion has since 1949 been dealt with in more than a thousand scholarly articles and numerous books published there, making it more popular than any other historical topic.3 This fact should not be surprising when we remember the role played by the peasantry in the Chinese Communist revolution itself. It was, after all, thanks to the support of rural people that Mao's revolution triumphed during and after the Sino-Japanese War. The peasants' contribution to the Chinese revolution was so obvious that it was only natural that historical research should have empha- sized this particular component in Chinese history. A new assessment of the peasants' political role in history has commenced. Within this general inquiry, two separate issues may be identified: first, the nature and limitations of the peasant wars, and, second, the contributions of the peasant wars, taken as a whole, to China's historical development. The reexamination of these issues, one hopes, has only just begun. But enough writings have been published to identify a new trend. New facts, new interpretations of facts, and new theoretical formulations are being advanced in terms of Marxism and Maoism. Moreover, the new interpretations concern not just peasants in rebellion but the peasant world view itself-a subject that should interest scholars in a number of disciplines. The Nature and Limitations of Peasant Rebellion Until the death of Mao, the view of peasant rebelfions among PRC historians was overwhelmingly positive. Most historians in the 1950s and especially the 1960s affirmed the revolutionary nature of the peasant rebels-short of the historical significance of the proletariat, but nonetheless aiming at the overthrow of China's "feudal system."5 An eminent minority had some basic disagreement. This minority, which included senior historians who had been converted to Marxism long before 1949, sympathetically hailed the peasant wars as the "highest form in which the contradiction in China's feudal society expressed itself." But they would insist, as did Jian Bozan in 1961, that peasant rebels even in late imperial China opposed landlords but not landlordism, the emperor and his officials but not the imperial system. A small number of younger scholars accepted and elaborated on this view.6 However, by the early 1960s the majority of the PRC's historical profession were prepared to go much further. As James P. Harrison has written, this vast majority projected into history Mao's enthusiastic portrayal of the contemporary Chinese peasantry in a way which accords with current Chinese pronouncements but very little with traditional Marxist views of the subject. The Chinese peasant rebels may not have been able to carry out advanced political programs, they argue, but they made this attempt.
