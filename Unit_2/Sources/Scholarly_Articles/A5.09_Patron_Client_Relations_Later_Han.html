Selection Overview

Ebrey argues that in the second century C.E. much of the social and political life of upper-class Han men ran through private ties between patrons and clients (teachers and students, officials and the men they appointed or recommended), and that these networks "provided a means of organization outside the formal government system." She suggests they gave the upper class a surer sense that "the status of their class was independent of the government," before such ties lost appeal after the Han fell.

PATRON-CLIENT RELATIONS IN THE LATER HAN PATRICIA EBREY UNIVERSITY OF ILLINOIS AT URBANA-CHAMPAIGN During the second Century A.D., much of the social and political activity of upper class men was organized on the basis of private ties between superiors and inferiors. These patron-client ties were created every time a man took on a teacher, accepted an appointment as a political subordinate, or received a recommendation for office by an official. Clients were expected to show loyalty to their patrons, mourning for them if they died and supporting them in political disputes. Patrons had reciprocal duties and could loose their clients if they did not show them proper respect and aid. Although ties of locality underlay most patron-client relations, high court officials sometimes attracted "students" from all over the country. Patron-client ties could be extended beyond the two individuals involved to form networks of men linked to common patrons, and to the patrons of their patrons. These networks provided a means of organization outside the formal government system. The first to exploit these networks were the various consort family regents who periodically rose to power after A.D. 89. After 140 officials who opposed the power of the consort families and eunuchs relied on their own patron-client networks; confidence that their client would aid and protect them enabled provincial officials to attack the clients of their opponents. In the 170s and 180s, after the failure of the partisan movement, patron-client networks remained strong, incorporating many men excluded from office. With the end of the Han, however, the personal subordination involved in patron-client relations seems to have lost much of its appeal to upper class men, and links between individuals came more often to be made on grounds of common status and kinship. MUCH OF CHINESE HISTORICAL WRITING for the early imperial period portrays political power and political decision-making as emanating from the emperor, his surrogates, or a handful of the highest officials. Men who held office are regularly portrayed in their biographies as obeying their superiors or their perception of the interests of their ruler and dynasty. To scholars today the flaws in this picture are readily apparent. Important decisions must often have been made lower in the bureaucracy, even by local officials, and never debated at court. Officials involved in making decisions would have been subject to pressure of influence from concerned groups. Many apparent decisions probably were not decisions at all but concessions to the demands of some group who resisted imperial authority. Often officials must have been acting consciously or unconsciously to protect the status quo and their own prerogatives. Recognizing the inevitability of these sorts of polit- ical processes, instead of attributing all power to the throne, modern scholars have generally stressed the importance of a ruling class composed of officials, retired officials, expectant officials, their close rela- tives, and social equals. The power of the ruling class is seen as neither solely bureaucratic (derived from the power of their offices), nor solely economic (through their control over tenants, debtors, and workers), but rather as the result of their general social position.' Because of the limitations of the sources, however, the nature of the power of the ruling class has never been closely analyzed. Until we can answer these questions discussions of the power of this stratum must remain vague. One way to approach this problem is to look at the private interrelations between upper class men. Not only were upper class men connected to each other on the basis of kinship, marriage, and locality, but throughout their lives they might acquire further ties to their teachers and fellow students, to their col- leagues, superiors, and subordinates in office, to those who shared their cultural values and interests, and to those who shared with them some exclusive status or privilege. Some of these bonds joined men as equals, others into hierarchies of superiors and inferiors. Some were inherited, others entered into with varying degrees of voluntary choice. Faced with certain problems or goals, men would rely on one kind of connection more than another. Moreover, as historical conditions changed, the ruling class as a whole, and distinct sub- groups within it, reoriented themselves. In one period they relied more upon kinship ties, in another period connections established through official service, and so on. These changes form an important strand in the social history of imperial China. In a previous study I examined some of the ties based on kinship and common status that gave cohe- sion to the aristocratic families in the Northern Dynasties and T'ang.2 Here I want to characterize certain non-kinship ties falling into the category of patron-client relations. I am defining patron-client relations as those between men who were essentially of the same social class, but who through mutual agreement had entered into a relationship of superior to inferior. This relationship was not restricted nar- rowly to any specific function, but covered aid and support in a wide variety of situations.3 I am not including the ties between an upper class man and his tenants or villagers, which are also often called patron-client ties but are of a different sort. In terms of chronology I will focus on the second century A.D., a period when patron-client ties were held in the highest esteem and were frequently employed as a basis for political action. MEN-SHENG AND KU-LI During the second century, stelea erected to honor high court officials and the governors of provinces, commanderies, and counties often had on their reverse side a list of the person's clients, his men-shenga ("students") or kulib ("former subordinates").
