A Qin Standard Weight Inscribed with the First Emperor’s Edict
edict issued 221 B.C.E.; weights made 221–206 B.C.E.
 
TOC FIT China: From Warring States to Empire; Consolidating the Roman and Chinese Empires (textbook pp. 117–121).
WHY HERE A Qin-made object, not a later story: the unification of weights and measures announced on the tools people actually used.
TREATMENT Different-object open substitute: open photographs of a Qin bronze weight at the Qin Shihuang Mausoleum Museum and of an early-20th-century rubbing from another Qin weight; edict translated for this reader from the rubbing.

Historical Context

In 221 B.C.E., after conquering the last rival state, the king of Qin took the new title huangdi, “August Emperor,” and ordered that weights, measures, and standards be made uniform across the realm. Officials had the order cast or engraved on official weights (quan), grain measures, and bronze plaques attached to them. Many such objects survive from sites across northern China. They were working tools used to check the amounts of grain, cloth, and taxes that passed through markets and government offices.

Image 1 shows a bell-shaped bronze weight with a loop for hanging from a steelyard. Its sides carry two edicts: the First Emperor’s edict of 221 B.C.E. and a second edict added under the Second Emperor in 209 B.C.E., confirming that the standards remained in force. Image 2 is an ink rubbing of the 221 B.C.E. edict made in the early twentieth century from a different Qin bronze weight, now in the Yūrinkan Museum in Kyoto, Japan. The rubbing makes the small seal-script characters easier to read than a photograph can. Rubbings record the carved surface, but the paper, ink, and mounting are modern.

Editorial note. The planned source was a Qin inscribed measure listed at the Metropolitan Museum of Art; that link now returns “page not found,” and no Qin measure with the edict could be found in the Met’s open-access collection. The substitutes shown here are different objects of the same kind, carrying the same edict. The Chinese text below is transcribed by the editor from the rubbing (Image 2), reading the columns from right to left; the English translation was prepared for this reader.
Primary Source Images
Image 1. Qin bronze weight with inscribed edicts, displayed at the Qin Shihuang Mausoleum Museum, Lintong, Shaanxi. The characters are cut in small seal script, the standardized script promoted by the Qin.
Image 2. Early-20th-century ink rubbing of the First Emperor’s 221 B.C.E. edict on a Qin bronze standard weight (original weight in the Yūrinkan Museum, Kyoto). Read the columns from right to left, beginning at top right with 廿六年 (“twenty-sixth year”).
Document Text

CHINESE TEXT

廿六年，皇帝盡并兼天下諸侯，黔首大安，立號為皇帝，乃詔丞相狀、綰：灋度量則不壹歉疑 者，皆明壹之。

In the twenty-sixth year [of the king of Qin, 221 B.C.E.], the August Emperor completely united and annexed the feudal lords of All-under-Heaven. The black-headed people are at great peace. He established the title “August Emperor,” and then commanded the chancellors Zhuang and Wan: “Wherever laws, measures, and standards are not uniform or are in doubt, make them all clear and uniform.”
