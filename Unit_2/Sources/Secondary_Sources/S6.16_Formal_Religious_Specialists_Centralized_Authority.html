Urbanization and Religion in Ancient Central Mexico
David M. Carballo
 
SELECTION Chapter 6, Ritual and Religion in the Creation of Urban Landscapes; concluding discussion before Conclusion
SOURCE LOCATOR pp. 213–214
ESSAY PROMPTS 6.4
ARGUMENT ROLES 6.4.1 Qualifies; 6.4.2 Challenges
Excerpt

Continuity between the Formative and Classic takes many forms. Uruñuela and Plunket (2007; Plunket and Uruñuela 2012) trace the Formative origins of essential urban furnishings at Teotihuacan including talud-tablero architecture, triadic arrangements of structures with central shrines or altars, and the imported ceramic ware Thin Orange. Other researchers have noted the strong continuities in the orientation of ceremonial architecture and in depictions of specific deities, which were detailed to a greater extent in Chapters 4 and 5. We have also seen that a coherent suite of ritual paraphernalia known from temple offerings at Teotihuacan was used at earlier urban centers, and that depositional contexts of ritual effigies and elements of this suite were remarkably similar during the first and second pulses of urbanization. Distinctive central Mexican grammars for architecture and for temple offerings were thereby established during the later Formative.

In certain respects, the endurance of urban and religious principles is remarkable in continuing through Aztec cities and into the ethnographic record. The problems in tracing direct historical linkages discussed in Chapter 2 are most profitably reconciled when symbols and practices are demonstrated to be pervasive across ethnolinguistic boundaries and when form, function, and context are shown to converge. Accordingly, the centrality of the Storm God and Old God to Otomi religion (e.g., López Aguilar and Fournier García 2012:92) and the presence of linguistic couplets expressing the water–fire dualism (Wright Carr 2012) strengthens claims made here for linkages between better known conceptualizations and practices, whether by Nahuas or Otomis, and the Formative to Classic archaeological record. In this way, a major religious orientation following an internal cultural logic of elemental dualisms such as water–fire and earth–sky, and a monistic, pantheistic, and cosmogenic framework is pan-Mesoamerican (e.g., Carrasco 2014; Monaghan 2000) but also is expressed in its distinct way in central Mexico as a macroregion. Other distinctive traits include the emphasis on deities and supernatural themes at the expense of powerful individuals—not merely at Teotihuacan, as is typically discussed by Mesoamerican scholars, but in the vast majority of cases covered in this book. Individualism was the exception rather than the rule in the material expression of early central Mexican religion and political ideology.

Politics and religion were certainly entangled in early central Mexico, and through this study I have suggested potential reasons for its variability. The interplay between integrative and divisive elements of the religious system that were manipulated variably by groups and individuals as they created cities manifested in various ways. Since more is known of variability in the politics of Postclassic religion, it is worth looking to the differences in participation that Brumfiel (2006) and Tena (2012) identified between elites and commoners, or urban and rural dwellers. Political elites at Aztec urban centers emphasized solar and martial themes not seen to the same degree among commoners, who emphasized water and fertility rituals in both urban and rural settings. These were the more enduring concepts of collective concern. Tenochtitlan’s Templo Mayor expresses the essential water–fire dualism in Tlaloc and Xiuhtecuhtli, but also through Tlaloc’s pairing with Huitzilopochtli. The solar associations and ethnic patron identity of Huitzilopochtli were much more socially divisive expressions of this dualism. It is difficult to say when patron deities emerged in central Mexico, but there is much better evidence for their existence during the Postclassic than during the Formative, or even the Classic.

Martial themes are observable throughout the prehispanic sequence but intensify during the Terminal Formative period and the second pulse of urbanization, particularly associated with Teotihuacan. The fate of La Laguna and its ritual termination deposits on top of Structure 12L-1 are illustrative of these changes and the creation of a new urban landscape in central Mexico. For this second wave of urbanism there is better evidence that major cities also developed bureaucratic institutions and controlled domains consistent with the political designation of a state—again, particularly for Teotihuacan (Spencer and Redmond 2004). The mural art of the city includes frequent depictions of individuals dressed in elaborate regalia, occasionally identified by adjacent pictographs, making offerings or conducting other rites consistent with the centralization of religion into a hierarchy that included formal priests (Manzanilla 1992). Whether these individuals were directly part of the state bureaucracy or organized more autonomously is less clear, but the uniformity of their depiction in the art of the city and its broader style horizon bespeak strong political affiliations. A similar degree of religious centralization is less likely for earlier urban societies in central Mexico, which present much lower evidence of well-defined bureaucracies, even at the largest cities. Still, the strong convergence in the symbolic grammars of deities, ceremonial architecture, and ritual offerings attest to the development of formal religious specialists and strong regional interaction during initial urbanization.
