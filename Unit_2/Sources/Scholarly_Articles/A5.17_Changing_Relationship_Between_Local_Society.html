Selection Overview

Hsu argues that China's consolidation was not finished at unification but came in the middle of the Former Han, as the court moved from rule by the founder's comrades-in-arms, through Emperor Wu's coercion of local leaders, to a lasting tie with a local elite. He credits the regular Hsiao-lien recommendations and local staff posts with turning that elite into "the social base of political power," calling the recommendations "a formal cooptation" on which general acceptance of the central government depended.

THE CHANGING RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN LOCAL SOCIETY AND THE CENTRAL POLITICAL POWER IN FORMER HAN: 206 B.C.-8 A.D., The consolidation of China did not come immediately with China's unifica- tion. It was not fully accomplished until the middle of the Former (Western) Han. The monolithic2 nature of the political powers and a group of local elite3 were then forming. And the bureaucracy, becoming much elaborated during this era, served to link the two. The elite group functioned, on the one hand as the reservoir of candidates to officialdom, and on the other hand, as the leading element with education, prestige, and often wealth, in the community. Based on these concepts, this paper ventures to present the formation of the local elite group through the changing social base of political power during Western Han. The problems are threefold: the changing nature of the political power in different periods, the community order, and the local government structure, which provided the local elite group with certain circumstances favorable to its taking root. As one among many indications, the origins of the Chancellors who were the leading officials in the court in the earlier reigns and remained so at least nominally in the later reigns, reveal a distinctive phenomenon. Under the first five emperors (206-141 B.C.), all thirteen chancellors or co-chancellors were either the comrades-in-arms of the founding emperor Kao-tzu or their descendents. All of them were ennobled as Marquis. During the reign of Emperor Wu (140-87 B.C.), the chancellors either came from the college of Marquis or were the relatives of the imperial house, with few exceptions. At least three of them were military men. Most chancellors under the Em- peror Chao and Emperor Hsiian (86-49 B.C.) were veteran officials rising from the rank and file of civil service. The Han Shu author had a comment at the end of the biography of Kung-sun Hung, the first Chancellor who, when appointed, did not yet have a Marquis rank. He also had a long list of outstanding figures in Em- peror Wu's court who came from diverse backgrounds, some of quite humble origins. More significant is the indication of multi-channeled ascendancy.6 The above warrants a division into four periods: 1. Monarchy without close tie to local society - reigns of five early rulers. 2. Coercion - reign of Emperor Wu. 3. Transition - reigns of Emperor Chao and Emperor Hsian. 4. Establishment of local tie - reigns after that of Emperor Yuan. During the first period, the attitude of the Han court at Ch'ang-an toward the provinces was not quite that of a central government towards its unified parts.7 There was a dualism, it may be said, between the capital area on the one hand and the provinces and principalities on the East Plain on the other. The passes between the capital area (present Shensi province) and the eastern regions were carefully guarded, and passports were required of all travellers.8 Even horses of specified heights and ages produced in the western regions were banned from exportation to the eastern provinces.9 Suspicion towards principalities in the East, ruled by brothers and cousins of the emperor, was of course especially strong. Subjects of certain princi- palities were not allowed to serve as imperial court attendants.
