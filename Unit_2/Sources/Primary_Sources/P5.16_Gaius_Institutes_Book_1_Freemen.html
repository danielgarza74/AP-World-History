Historical Context

Gaius was a Roman teacher of law who wrote an introductory textbook, the Institutes, around 160 C.E., under the emperor Antoninus Pius. It survives in a single manuscript found at Verona in 1816 and was the model for Justinian’s Institutes. Book 1 begins with the “law of persons”: who is free, who is a slave, who is under another’s power.

The sections here give three kinds of information. Sections 9–19 describe how slaves became freedmen and which freedmen became citizens, with conditions on age and on the owner’s form of manumission, and harsher status for slaves who had been punished. Sections 52–53 say that slaves are in the power of their owners, who hold, Gaius says, power of life and death, and report two rulings of Antoninus Pius that limited it. Section 55 compares the Roman father’s power over his children. A law textbook says what a student should know, not how often rules were used; “proprietor” renders dominus, the owner.

Editorial note. Poste’s translation is reproduced as posted by the Roman Law Library (Université Grenoble Alpes), without his commentary or the site’s section headings. Section numbers are Gaius’s; Poste’s wording is kept. Jus gentium is the law common to all peoples; vindicta a ritual of freeing a slave before a magistrate; quiritary and bonitary dominion are two legal kinds of ownership. Freemen, slaves, and freedmen

1.9 The first division of men by the law of persons is into freemen and slaves.

1.10 Freemen are divided into freeborn and freedmen.

1.11 The freeborn are free by birth; freedmen by manumission from legal slavery.

1.12 Freedmen, again, are divided into three classes, citizens of Rome, Latins, and persons on the footing of enemies surrendered at discretion. Let us examine each class in order, and commence with freedmen assimilated to enemies surrendered at discretion.

1.13 The law Aelia Sentia enacts that slaves who have been punished by their proprietors with chains, or have been branded, or have been examined with torture on a criminal charge, and have been convicted, or have been delivered to fight with men or beasts, or have been committed to a gladiatorial school or a public prison, if subsequently manumitted by the same or by another proprietor, shall acquire by manumission the status of enemies surrendered at discretion.

1.14 Surrendered enemies are people who have taken up arms and fought against the people of Rome and having been defeated have surrendered.

1.15 Slaves tainted with this degree of criminality, by whatever mode they are manumitted and at whatever age, and notwithstanding the plenary dominion of their proprietor, never become citizens of Rome or Latins, but can only acquire the status of enemies who have surrendered.

1.16 If the slave has not committed offences of so deep a dye, manumission sometimes makes him a citizen of Rome, sometimes a Latin.

1.17 A slave in whose person these three conditions are united, thirty years of age, quiritary ownership of the manumitter, liberation by a civil and statutory mode of manumission, i.e. by the form of vindicta, by entry on the censor’s register, by testamentary disposition, becomes a citizen of Rome: a slave who fails to satisfy any one of these conditions becomes only a Latin.

1.18 The requisition of a certain age of the slave was introduced by the lex Aelia Sentia, by the terms of which law, unless he is thirty years old, a slave cannot on manumission become a citizen of Rome, unless the mode of manumission is by the form of vindicta, preceded by proof of adequate motive before the council.

1.19 There is an adequate motive of manumission if, for instance, a natural child or natural brother or sister or foster child of the manumitter’s, or a teacher of the manumitter’s child, or a male slave intended to be employed as an agent in business, or a female slave about to become the manumitter’s wife, is presented to the council for manumission.

Slaves in the power of their owners

1.52 Slaves are in the power of their proprietors, a power recognized by jus gentium, since all nations present the spectacle of masters invested with power of life and death over slaves; and (by the Roman law) the owner acquires everything acquired by the slave.

1.53 But in the present day neither Roman citizens, nor any other persons under the empire of the Roman people, are permitted to indulge in excessive or causeless harshness towards their slaves. By a constitution of the Emperor Antoninus, a man who kills a slave of whom he is owner, is as liable to punishment as a man who kills a slave of whom he is not owner: and inordinate cruelty on the part of owners is checked by another constitution whereby the same emperor, in answer to inquiries from presidents of provinces concerning slaves who take refuge at temples of the gods, or statues of the emperor, commanded that on proof of intolerable cruelty a proprietor should be compelled to sell his slaves: and both ordinances are just, for we ought not to make a bad use of our lawful rights, a principle recognized in the interdiction of prodigals from the administration of their fortune.

1.54 But as citizens of Rome may have a double kind of dominion, either bonitary or quiritary, or a union of both bonitary and quiritary dominion, a slave is in the power of an owner who has bonitary dominion over him, even unaccompanied with quiritary dominion; if an owner has only bare quiritary dominion he is not deemed to have the slave in his power.

1.55 Again, a man has power over his own children begotten in civil wedlock, a right peculiar to citizens of Rome, for there is scarcely any other nation where fathers are invested with such power over their children as at Rome; and this the late Emperor Hadrian declared in the edict he published respecting certain petitioners for a grant of Roman citizenship to themselves and their children; though I am aware that among the Galatians parents are invested with power over their children.
