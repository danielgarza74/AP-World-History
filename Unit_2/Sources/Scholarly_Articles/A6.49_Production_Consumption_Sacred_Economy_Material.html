Selection Overview

Renfrew develops a model of Chaco Canyon as "an essentially egalitarian society" centered on the canyon as a "Location of High Devotional Expression," where great houses and great kivas drew periodic pilgrimages and goods flowed in as offerings in exchange for religious experience. He sets this model against views of Chaco as a trading outpost of Mexico or as an elite power base, finding little evidence for either, while admitting he is "not in a position to contradict" the elite interpretation and offering his own as an alternative to test.

A cognitive-processual approach is used here to develop a model of Chaco as an essentially egalitarian society, centered on the Canyon as a Location of High Devotional Expression. The production and consumption of goods is understood in the context of an ideationalldevotional significance of the great houses and great kivas of Chaco and of periodic visits made to them for devotional purposes (i.e., pilgrimages). Consideration is given to the structure of regional pilgrimages and the function of the multiple great houses in Chaco Canyon. This model is compared with two others: Chaco as a secondary trading center and Chaco as an elite power base. Production and consumption in both sacred and profane contexts are exam- ined in order to distinguish among the three models. Among parallels for LHDE centers are the Ring of Brogar and Stones of Stenness in the Orkney Islands of Scotland, the image ahu of Easter island, the mediaeval pilgrimages to the Church of St. James of Compostella in north Spain and other pilgrimage centers of the Roman Catholic Church, and the Olympic Games (and other periodic games) of classical Greece. It is argued that the consideration of symbolic and ideological aspects of the culture system is neces- sary for a proper understanding of the economy. It does not necessarily require a full understanding of the semantic content of the beliefs involved. Nor does it preclude a systems analysis, integrating the functional and the cognitive, where due attention is given to the symbolic or projective system. Critique In my (admittedly restricted) reading concerning Chaco, it has seemed to me that the discussion of production, consumption, and exchange has been conducted to a large extent in what one might call "economic" terms, where it is assumed that the com- modities in question-whether foodstuffs, or prod- ucts (including pottery), or valuables such as turquoise-have some well-understood value within the system, which will govern the circumstances through which they are exchanged during face-to- face encounters. The possible role of elite groups is often inferred, and their hypothetical control of the production and exchange process is sometimes seen as conferring power upon them. The well-known relationship between long-distance exchange of valu- ables and elite power, as illustrated in the case of the European Iron Age by Frankenstein and Rowlands (1978) and for the Classic Maya by Rathje (1973), is perhaps often implicit in these discussions. So too is the World System view of Wallerstein (see Math- ien 1986:222) where Chaco's place may be seen largely in economic terms, perhaps as the periphery of a Mexican World System, significant to the latter principally for the supply of turquoise. This is a predominantly secular view. Attention has been drawn by a number of authors (Fritz 1978; Judge 1989; Sofaer 1997; Toll 1991) to thelikelyrole of Chaco as a major religious center, but the approach does not seem to have been exploited fully in order to facilitate further analysis of production and con- sumption. I am critical of the reluctance of many of those analyzing the Chaco phenomenon in terms of social hierarchies to recognize and exploit the clearly sacred aspect, which may be present in the very fact of the construction of a number of the great houses of Chaco and its region. The analysis is often cast in the context of a rather ill-defined notion of "com- plexity" (Redman 1991) of the structuring of "low level hierarchies" (Johnson 1982 quoted by Vivian 1989) and the solution of ecologically situated prob- lems of subsistence. Yet while the subsistence basis is indeed funda- mental to any analysis, it should be evident that the great kivas and the great houses themselves are actu- ated by a powerful and coherent belief system responsible also for the "roads" or ceremonial prom- enades. Many earlier discussions might be seen as a prod- uct of the functional-processual approach that has dominated much NorthAmerican archaeology since the 1960s (Renfrew and Bahn 1996). It has been sub- jected to criticism by archaeologists of the "post- processual" or "interpretive" school, to which I do not myself subscribe. But there is, I would argue, an alternative to their "hermeneutic" approach. This may be termed "cognitive processual" (Renfrew 1994; Renfrew and Bahn 1996). It implies no aban- donment of the basic processual approach, but car- ries with it a willingness to consider more carefully symbolic aspects, including religious aspects, and to try to integrate them more closely within a holistic analysis in which the cognitive dimension plays a central role along with other indispensable questions such as subsistence (Renfrew 1994). It is not neces- sary to reject the one in order to consider the other. In what follows I shall not hesitate to take a sim- plified, perhaps excessively romantic, view of the Chaco phenomenon and to suggest a third model, to set alongside the first two. The intention, however, is not to single out the religious or ritual aspects of the system for unduly preponderant emphasis. It is rather to seek to recognize them in a systematic man- ner, and tQ consider their influence upon other aspects of the -cultural system, notably production, exchange, and consumption.
