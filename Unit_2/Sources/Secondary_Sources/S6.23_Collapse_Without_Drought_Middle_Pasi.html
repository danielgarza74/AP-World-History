As virtually the entire Pasion–Usumacinta River system erupted in warfare and site after site was abandoned, the seat of power based at Cancuen was moved to Machaquila, far to the east and inland from the river system (Demarest and Fahsen 2003). The Classic western Maya world had come to an end, ultimately through warfare, but warfare rooted in elite status rivalry. The end of settlement in the Petexbatun was, truly, the end. Other than one or a few very limited exceptions (Johnston, Breckenridge, and Hansen 2001), the region was reoccupied only in the twentieth century, when a few scattered villages were established. If drought or anthropogenic climate change was the cause of abandonment, it might be expected that the region would have been resettled as the environmental stresses lifted. Instead, the processes that led to the abandonment of the region were cultural and part of a broad change in Maya civilization as a whole. The forest expanded, overgrowing the cities and fields, but the population never returned.

Underlying Causes of Petexbatun Abandonment

The extensive and multifaceted data recovered by the Petexbatun Regional Archaeological Project not only permit reconstruction of the culture history of the region, but also have allowed scholars to offer a coherent model of collapse based on internal, cultural factors (Demarest 2004a, 2004b, 2006; O’Mansky and Demarest 2007). In fact, the changes that swept the Maya lowlands in the Terminal Classic period can be and must be understood within the broad cultural context of ancient Maya civilization, with particular focus on the nature of Classic period rulership. At the center of Classic period Maya political civilization was the k’uhul ajaw, the divine or holy lord. The power of the k’uhul ajaw was based primarily in ideology and ritual performance with involvement also in warfare and acquisition of prestige-enhancing trade goods. Polities were not clearly defined political territories but instead fluctuated in size based largely on the charisma of individual rulers (Demarest 2004b; Freidel 1992). Such a system demanded competition between k’uhul ajaws. Status rivalry between k’uhul ajaws of competing centers took a variety of forms. The most obvious form of competition was warfare, and evidence for warfare is abundant across the Maya world throughout the Classic period (e.g., Armillas 1951; Demarest et al. 1997; Graham 1967; Rands 1952; Trejo 2000; Webster 1976, 1978). Other forms of status rivalry included construction of monumental architecture, particularly as a stage for ritual performance, and erection of monuments and other artwork. The authority of rulers and the legitimization of their claims to power were further reinforced in site layout, architecture, artwork, and the regalia of the k’uhul ajaw (Demarest 2004a; O’Mansky and Demarest 2007). Many of the essential items that constituted the costume and accoutrements of the ruler were goods from the highlands of Guatemala and Chiapas, such as jade, pyrite, obsidian, and quetzal feathers (Demarest 2004b; Freidel, Reece-Taylor, and David Mora-Marín 2002). From the sources, goods could flow east through the Motagua River Valley to the Caribbean Sea and from there north along the Yucatan coast. Alternatively, trade routes followed natural land routes through the highlands to the edge of the Alta

Verapaz and the headwaters of the Pasion River. The Pasion flows north past Cancuen, then turns to the West just past Seibal before joining the Salinas River to form the Usumacinta, which flows into the Gulf of Mexico. This route of trade and communication was of critical importance to the entire western and central Maya world (Demarest and Barrientos 1999, 2000; Hammond 1972, 1973). Whichever polity controlled the river also controlled, or at least had great influence over, the flow of highland goods to many of the great lowland cities. For this reason, from at least the Early Classic period the river system was a target for control by the major powers of the Maya world. For the underlying causes of the warfare and collapse in the Petexbatun, then, we must look to the political and economic stresses created by the demands of the k’uhul ajaw system itself. The seventh-century entrada (venture) by Tikal at Dos Pilas appears to have been a strategic plan to attempt to gain control of the Pasion River and, therefore, access to the highland goods that were not merely valuable but were essential to legitimizing the authority of k’uhul ajaws. The Dos Pilas rulers then began to expand their kingdom in the Petexbatun and along the Pasion, first as part of the Tikal kingdom, then under the control of Calakmul, and finally as an independent polity. While the rulers of Dos Pilas expanded their hegemony, across the Maya lowlands the demands of the k’uhul ajaw system were exacerbated in the seventh and eighth centuries by increasing interelite status rivalry, the growing proportion of elites in the population due to elite polygyny, and the consequent increase in interelite competition for limited positions of royal power and for status-reinforcing exotic goods (Demarest 2004a, 2004b; Demarest et al. 2004a; O’Mansky and Demarest 2007). In the Petexbatun, this cycle led first to intercenter warfare and rapidly devolved into more widespread conflict as the basic infrastructure of the region was disrupted (Demarest 2004a; O’Mansky and Dunning 2004). The siege at Dos Pilas and subsequent endemic warfare in the Petexbatun should not be seen as a unique historical event. Rather, it is an example of processes that may have been taking place throughout the West and perhaps other areas of the Maya world at the end of the Classic period. The warfare that led to the abandonment of the region, then, was only the ultimate event in a long process rooted in the k’uhul ajaw system of rulership.

Petexbatun Environment in the Eighth Century: Collapse without Drought

The disintegration of the Petexbatun polity and abandonment of the region have been explained in great detail based on archaeological, epigraphic, and other lines of evidence. It is not merely that the evidence allows researchers to discuss the collapse of the region without resorting to drought or other climatic catastrophes; there is no clear evidence of such climate change in the region at the time of abandonment. The most direct evidence for drought, if it existed, would be expected to come from the Laguna Tamarindito cores. Yet these cores show no clear evidence of drought. The only possible evidence for a Terminal Classic drought is an increase in charcoal at that level of the core (Dunning, Rue, et al. 1998:144; Dunning and Beach n.d.). Furthermore, while the Laguna Petexbatun and Tamarindito cores do show evidence of severe deforestation, the periods of major clearing date to the Late Preclassic and Early Classic periods. The conservation methods employed at Tamarindito beginning in the Early Classic were adopted in other parts of the region so that in the Late Classic, despite population growth, the environment was stable (Dunning and Beach n.d.; Dunning, Rue, et al. 1998). Not only is there no evidence of drought, then; there is also no evidence of significant anthropogenic environmental degradation. The stability of the Petexbatun environment in the Late and Terminal Classic is also reflected in the osteological record. A significant increase in deforestation should be reflected in an increase in maize consumption and a decrease in protein consumption as the habitat for deer and other animals is destroyed. However, stable isotope analysis and alkaline earth ratios in human bone collagen indicate that protein levels in the diet remained stable over time. In addition, paleopathological and paleodietary analyses indicate no increase in anemia, infectious disease, or dental growth disruption in the Late and Terminal Classic periods, as might be expected in times of severe drought (Wright 1997, 2006). Overall, the osteological record reveals general stability in health and diet in the Petexbatun across time, even during the late eighth and early ninth centuries.
