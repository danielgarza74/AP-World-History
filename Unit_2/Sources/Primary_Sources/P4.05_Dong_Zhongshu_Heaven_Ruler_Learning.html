Dong Zhongshu: Heaven, the Ruler, and Learning in Han Government
Dong Zhongshu (ca. 195–ca. 115 B.C.E.; traditional dates 179–104), under Emperor Wu (r. 141–87 B.C.E.)
 
TOC FIT China and the Search for Order: The Confucian Answer; Han government and learning (textbook pp. 145–146).
WHY HERE A Han scholar’s account of what Heaven expects of a ruler, of how Heaven responds when a ruler fails, and of his proposal to base state learning on one teaching.
TREATMENT Fresh translation from the public-domain Chinese: part of one paragraph of the Chunqiu fanlu and three short selections from Dong’s memorials in the Han shu.

Historical Context

Dong Zhongshu was a scholar of the Spring and Autumn Annals, the old chronicle of the state of Lu, which Han Confucians read through the Gongyang commentary as a manual of political judgment. He held office under Emperor Wu, whose long reign (141–87 B.C.E.) was a defining age of the Former Han dynasty. Two sorts of writing carry his name. The Chunqiu fanlu (“Luxuriant Dew of the Spring and Autumn Annals”) is a collection of essays attributed to him; historians debate which chapters he wrote. The Han shu (History of the Former Han, compiled by Ban Gu in the first century C.E. and completed after his death) preserves three “responses” he made when the emperor asked recommended scholars about Heaven’s will and good government. Tradition dates them to about 134 B.C.E.; modern historians question both the date and how closely Ban Gu’s text preserves what Dong wrote.

Read the passages for how Dong says a ruler must serve Heaven, Earth, and humankind; what Heaven does, in his account, when a ruler fails, and who is able to act on that warning; and what he asks the emperor to do about schools and about teachings outside the Confucian curriculum. The emperor founded an imperial academy for the classics in 124 B.C.E., but historians disagree about how far Dong’s advice caused that step or changed how the Han state governed.

Editorial note. Translated for this reader by the editor from the Chinese text of the Chunqiu fanlu (juan 6, chapter 19, “Li yuanshen” 立元神) and of Dong’s three responses (Han shu 56), as transcribed on Chinese Wikisource and cross-checked against two other transcriptions (the Siku quanshu text of the Chunqiu fanlu; Wikisource’s separate “Xianliang ce” pages for the responses). The first Wikisource transcription of the Chunqiu fanlu misreads “pillow” (zhen 枕) as “river” (he 河) twice; the translation follows the Siku reading. The Chunqiu fanlu excerpt begins in the middle of the chapter’s first paragraph and stops before that paragraph’s last sentences; the chapter’s two further paragraphs, on the ruler’s concealment and “spirit-like” rule, are omitted. Each memorial selection is a continuous passage, but the second begins at “Therefore,” in the middle of an argument. The Chinese beginning and end of each selection are given in the citation block. Words in square brackets are added for clarity; the Chinese of one clause (xin, “firm in conviction”) is uncertain. This is a new translation, not an adaptation of the translation in de Bary and Bloom, Sources of Chinese Tradition.
Document Text

1. From the Chunqiu fanlu, chapter 19 (excerpt from the first of its three paragraphs): the ruler and the three roots

[. . .]

The ruler of men is the root of the state. In governing a state, no influence for transforming [the people] is greater than honoring the root. If he honors the root, the ruler’s power to transform [the people] is like that of a spirit. If he does not honor the root, the ruler has no means of drawing the people together; and if he has no means of drawing the people together, then even if he imposes harsh punishments and heavy executions, the people will not obey. This is what is called driving the state out and throwing it away. What disaster is greater than this?

What is meant by the root? Heaven, Earth, and humankind are the root of the ten thousand things. Heaven gives them birth, Earth nourishes them, and humankind completes them. Heaven gives birth through filial piety and brotherly respect; Earth nourishes through clothing and food; humankind completes through ritual and music. The three serve one another as hands and feet do, joining to make one body, and not one of them can be missing. Without filial piety and brotherly respect, people lose what gives them life; without clothing and food, they lose what nourishes them; without ritual and music, they lose what completes them. If all three are lost, the people become like deer, each following his own desires and each household making its own customs; fathers cannot direct their sons and rulers cannot direct their ministers. Even though there are walls around it, the city will be called an “empty town.” In such a case the ruler lies down with a clod of earth for a pillow and stiffens in death. Though no one endangers him, he is endangered of himself; though no one destroys him, he is destroyed of himself. This is called “natural punishment” [punishment that arises of itself]. When natural punishment arrives, even if he wraps himself in a stone chamber and shuts off the dangerous passes, he cannot escape it.

An enlightened master and worthy ruler is sure to hold firmly to this. He therefore reverently attends to the three roots. He performs the suburban sacrifice with utmost respect and serves the ancestral shrines with reverence; he raises up and honors those of filial piety and brotherly respect and singles out filial conduct for distinction. In this way he serves the root that is Heaven. He takes up the plow handle and tills in person; he plucks mulberry leaves and tends the silkworms himself; he clears the wasteland and plants grain, opening new land so that there is enough clothing and food. In this way he serves the root that is Earth. He sets up the Royal Academy and local schools, cultivates filial piety, brotherly respect, reverence, and deference, enlightens the people through teaching, and moves them through ritual and music. In this way he serves the root that is humankind. If all three are served, the people will be like sons and younger brothers who dare not act on their own, and the state will be like a father and mother, loved without any need for favors and obeyed without any need for severity. Even if the ruler dwelt in the wilds and slept in the open, it would be better than living in a palace. Such a ruler lies down on a peaceful pillow; though no one helps him, he becomes strong of himself; though no one soothes him, he is at ease of himself. This is called “natural reward.” When natural reward arrives, even if he were to step aside and leave the state, the people would carry their children on their backs and follow him and make him their lord, and the ruler himself would not be able to leave them. Thus a state governed by virtue is sweeter than honey or malt-syrup and firmer than glue or lacquer. This is why sages and worthies strive to honor the root and dare not lose it.

2. From Dong Zhongshu’s first response (Han shu 56): Heaven warns a failing ruler

I have examined the Spring and Autumn Annals and looked at what happened in earlier ages, in order to observe how Heaven and humankind act upon one another; it is truly fearsome. When a state is about to lose the Way and fall into ruin, Heaven first sends disasters to rebuke it. If the rulers do not look into their own conduct, Heaven sends strange portents to alarm them. If they still do not change, then ruin and defeat arrive. From this we see that Heaven’s mind is humane and loving toward the ruler of men and wishes to stop his disorder. Unless it is an age utterly without the Way, Heaven wants to support [the ruler] and keep him safe; everything depends on how hard he strives. If he strives at learning, what he hears and sees will grow broad and his understanding will grow clearer. If he strives at following the Way, his virtue will rise day by day and he will accomplish great things. All of this can be brought about quickly and will show results.

3. From the second response: schools

[. . .] Therefore, in nurturing men of ability, nothing is greater than the Grand Academy (taixue). The Academy is the gate through which worthy men come forward and the root and source of moral teaching. Yet now, from a whole commandery or kingdom, not one man answers when the summons comes; this means the Kingly Way is being cut off here and there. I wish that Your Majesty would found a Grand Academy, appoint distinguished teachers, nurture the scholars of the realm, and examine them often so as to bring out their full talents. Then outstanding men can surely be found. [. . .]

4. From the third response: one teaching

The Annals’ exaltation of “unity” is the constant norm of Heaven and Earth and the common principle of past and present. Now teachers follow different ways and people hold different views; the hundred schools differ in method, and their aims are not the same. For this reason those above have no means of upholding unity, and since laws and regulations change again and again, those below do not know what to follow. In my foolish opinion, everything that is not within the curriculum of the Six Arts [the Six Classics] and the teaching of Confucius should have its path cut off and not be allowed to advance alongside them. When deviant and perverse doctrines have been extinguished, then the ruling framework can be unified, the laws and standards made clear, and the people will know what to follow.
