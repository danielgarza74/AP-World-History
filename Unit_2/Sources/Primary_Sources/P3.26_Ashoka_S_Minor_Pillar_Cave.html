Ashoka’s Minor Pillar and Cave Inscriptions: The Schism Edicts, Lumbini, Nigali Sagar, and Barabar
dated inscriptions from the 12th to the 20th year after Ashoka’s consecration (ca. 256–249 B.C.E.); the schism edicts are undated (Ashoka r. ca. 268–232 B.C.E.)
 
TOC FIT Intermittent Empire: The Case of India (textbook pp. 125–128).
WHY HERE The emperor’s own short inscriptions on the unity of the Buddhist monastic order and on places tied to the Buddha, set beside his gifts of caves to a rival order of ascetics.
TREATMENT Public-domain translation (Hultzsch, Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum 1, Oxford, 1925), seven short inscriptions in full as translated, with gaps in the stone marked; Prakrit texts and notes not reproduced.
ESSAY PROMPTS 3.3, 4.5
ARGUMENT ROLES 3.3.1 Supports; 3.3.2 Qualifies; 3.3.3 Qualifies; 4.5.1 Supports; 4.5.3 Supports
Historical Context

Besides the long Rock and Pillar Edicts (P3.19, P3.20), Ashoka had shorter texts carved on pillars at Buddhist sites and in caves. The translations here are from Eugen Hultzsch’s 1925 edition, still the standard scholarly edition, which is more literal than the translation in P3.19. Three pillars, at Sarnath (near Varanasi, where the Buddha first taught), at Sanchi, and at Kosam (ancient Kausambi), carry versions of one order: monks or nuns who split the Buddhist monastic order (the Samgha) are to be put into white robes, the dress of lay people rather than monks, and made to live outside the monastery. The Sarnath version adds instructions telling the king’s officials (Mahamatras) how to circulate the order. The pillars at Rummindei (ancient Lumbini, in the Tarai of Nepal) and Nigali Sagar record the king’s visits to the Buddha’s birthplace and to the stupa of Konakamana, a Buddha believed to have lived before Gotama. The Barabar Hill caves near Gaya, cut into hard granite, were given to the Ajivikas, an order of ascetics that rivaled the Buddhists.

These texts show what the king did and ordered, in his own words. They show him worshipping at Buddhist holy places, granting a village a tax reduction, and using his officials to keep the monastic order united; they also show gifts to a non-Buddhist group. Whether they reveal personal faith, a ruler managing powerful religious institutions, or both is a matter of inference. An order is not proof that it was carried out: later Buddhist chronicles from Sri Lanka tell of Ashoka expelling false monks, but they were written centuries afterward. Words in square brackets are Hultzsch’s restorations of damaged letters, dots mark letters lost on the stone, and parentheses are his words supplied for sense. Hultzsch read the Lumbini tax phrase as “paying (only) an eighth share,” a reduction rather than full exemption; earlier scholars read it differently. He also noted that someone had tried to chisel the word “Ajivikas” out of the first two cave inscriptions.

Editorial note. The translation is E. Hultzsch’s in Inscriptions of Asoka, new edition, Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, vol. 1 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1925), pp. 161–165 and 181–182, read in the Internet Archive’s OCR text and checked against a second scan of the same edition (archive.org item in.ernet.dli.2015.107103). Only the English translations are printed; Hultzsch’s Prakrit texts, his footnotes, and his note numbers are left out, and his printed page numbers are marked [p. 162] and so on. His section letters (A), (B), etc. are kept. Diacritical marks are not reproduced (Hultzsch prints Devānāmpriya, Priyadarśin, Saṁgha, Mahāmātra, Śākyamuni). The cave labels “First cave,” “Second cave,” and “Third cave” and all subheadings are the editor’s. OCR errors are corrected against the second scan: “Bevanampriya” to “Devanampriya,” “Tillage” to “village,” “ting” to “king,” “Smhgha” and “Sathgha” to “Samgha,” the section letters “(O)” and “(P)” to “(C)” and “(F),” “Priyadar£in,” “FriyadarMn,” and similar forms to “Priyadarsin,” and, in the third cave inscription, a garbled line to “given by me for (shelter during).” The dot-runs that mark lost letters are given as in Hultzsch, slightly regularized. Not included: the fragmentary Kosam (Kausambi) version of the schism edict, whose surviving words repeat the Sanchi and Sarnath texts, and the Calcutta-Bairat edict to the Samgha, which P3.19 prints complete.
Document Text

Sanchi pillar: the schism edict (p. 161)

(A) . . . . . . . . . .

(B) [cannot] be divided.

(C) The Samgha both of monks and of nuns is made united as long as (my) sons and great-grandsons (shall reign, and) as long as the moon and the sun (shall shine).

(D) The monk or nun who shall break up the Samgha, must be caused to put on white robes and to reside in a non-residence.

(E) For my desire is that the Samgha may be united (and) of long duration.

Sarnath pillar: the schism edict, with orders for making it known (pp. 162–164)

[p. 162] (A) Deva[nampriya] . . . . . . .

(B) . . . . . . . . Pata[liputra] . . . . . . . .

(C) . . . . . the Samgha [cannot] be divided by any one.

(D) But indeed that monk or nun who shall break up the Samgha, should be caused to put on white robes and to reside in a non-residence.

[p. 163] (E) Thus this edict must be submitted both to the Samgha of monks and to the Samgha of nuns.

(F) Thus speaks Devanampriya:

(G) Let one copy of this (edict) remain with you deposited in (your) office; and deposit ye another copy of this very (edict) with the lay-worshippers.

(H) These lay-worshippers may come on every fast-day (posatha) in order to be inspired with confidence in this very edict; and invariably on every fast-day, every Mahamatra (will) come to the fast-day (service) in order to be inspired with confidence in this very edict and to understand (it).

(I) And as far as your district (extends), dispatch ye (an officer) everywhere according to the letter of this (edict).

[p. 164] (J) In the same way cause (your subordinates) to dispatch (an officer) according to the letter of this (edict) in all the territories (surrounding) forts.

Rummindei (Lumbini) pillar: the Buddha’s birthplace (pp. 164–165)

(A) When king Devanampriya Priyadarsin had been anointed twenty years, he came himself and worshipped (this spot), because the Buddha Sakyamuni was born here.

(B) (He) both caused to be made a stone bearing a horse (?) and caused a stone pillar to be set up, (in order to show) that the Blessed one was born here.

[p. 165] (C) (He) made the village of Lummini free of taxes, and paying (only) an eighth share (of the produce).

Nigali Sagar pillar: the stupa of an earlier Buddha (p. 165)

(A) When king Devanampriya Priyadarsin had been anointed fourteen years, he enlarged the Stupa of the Buddha Konakamana to the double (of its original size).

(B) And when he had been anointed [twenty] years, he came himself and worshipped (this spot) [and] caused [a stone pillar to be set up].

Barabar Hill: three caves given to ascetics (pp. 181–182)

[p. 181] First cave. By king Priyadarsin, (when he had been) anointed twelve years, this Banyan-cave was given to the Ajivikas.

Second cave. By king Priyadarsin, (when he had been) anointed twelve years, this cave in the Khalatika mountain was given to the Ajivikas.

[p. 182] Third cave. When king Priyadarsin had been anointed nineteen years, this cave in the very pleasant Kha[latika mountain] was given by me for (shelter during) the rainy season.
