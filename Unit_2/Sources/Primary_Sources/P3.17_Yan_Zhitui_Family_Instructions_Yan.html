Historical Context

Yan Zhitui (531–after 590 C.E.) lived through the long division of China after the fall of the Han. Born into an educated southern family, he served the Liang dynasty in the south, was captured when Liang fell in 554, and later served the Northern Qi, whose rulers came from the Xianbei, a people of the northern steppe frontier. He lived to see China reunified under the Sui in 589. Near the end of his life he wrote Family Instructions for the Yan Clan (Yanshi jiaxun) as advice for his sons.

The passages below come from the chapters “Teaching Children” and “Encouraging Learning.” The first describes an official at the Northern Qi court who taught his son the Xianbei language and the pipa (a stringed instrument) to win favor with the powerful. The second and third contrast the fates of aristocrats with and without education during the wars that destroyed the Liang. Yan wrote as an interested party: he belonged to the educated class whose value he defends.

Editorial note. Translated for this reader by the editor from the Chinese text of the Yanshi jiaxun on Chinese Wikisource (chapter 2, “Jiaozi” 教子, in juan 1; chapter 8, “Mianxue” 勉學, in juan 3). Omissions are marked [. . .]; words added for clarity are in square brackets.
Document Text

From “Teaching Children” (Jiaozi)

There was a gentleman-official at the court of [Northern] Qi who once said to me, “I have a son who is now seventeen and quite skilled at writing official letters. I have been teaching him the Xianbei language and how to play the pipa, and he is beginning to master them. With these he can serve the high ministers, and there is no one who will not favor him. This too is an important matter.” At the time I lowered my head and did not answer. How strange is this man’s way of teaching his son! Even if such skills could make you high ministers, I would not want you to take that path.

From “Encouraging Learning” (Mianxue)

When the Liang dynasty was at its height, most of the young men of noble families had no learning. [. . .] They all perfumed their clothes and shaved their faces, wore powder and rouge, rode in carriages with long canopies, walked in high-soled clogs, sat on checkered square cushions, leaned on armrests of patterned silk, and set out curios to their left and right. They came and went at their ease and looked like immortals. When they sat for the examinations on the classics, they hired others to write their answers; at the banquets of the high officials, they had others compose their poems for them. At such times they seemed fine gentlemen indeed. But after the disorder and scattering, when the court and the markets changed hands, those who selected men for office were no longer their relatives, and those who held power were no longer their old allies. They looked to themselves and found nothing there; they tried to be of use to the world and had no use. They were like men in coarse clothing who have lost the pearl they carried, like animals stripped of their fur with bare hide showing. Stiff as dead trees, drifting like streams run dry, they wandered alone among the war horses and died in the ditches. At such times they were truly worthless. But those with learning and skills found a place wherever they landed. Since the time of chaos began, I have seen many people taken captive. Even those whose families had been commoners for a hundred generations, if they knew how to read the Analects and the Classic of Filial Piety, could still become teachers. But those whose families had worn officials’ caps for a thousand years, if they could not read or write, all ended up plowing fields and tending horses. Seeing this, how can you not exert yourselves? If you can always keep a few hundred scrolls of books, then even in a thousand years you will never become lowly people.

Understanding the meaning of the Six Classics and reading widely in the books of the Hundred Schools may not make you more virtuous or improve the customs of the age, but it is still a skill that can support you. You cannot always rely on your father and elder brothers, and you cannot always count on your home district and your state. Once you are cast adrift, with no one to shelter you, you will have to depend on yourself alone. The proverb says, “Better a small skill in your hands than ten million in stored-up wealth.” And of all skills that are easy to learn and worth valuing, none surpasses reading books.
