Selection Overview

Holcombe argues that after non-Chinese peoples took north China in the early fourth century, the exiled Eastern Jin court faced a real identity crisis, lacking a secure state, homeland, or common language among its mostly non-Chinese southern subjects. Yet Confucian ideals of ritual and of a universal order under Heaven gave the office-holding elite (the shi) a firm identity not tied to territory or ethnicity, "ensuring that the idea 'China' would be able to survive the political turmoil of the great age of division," though that identity remained the prerogative of a narrow elite.

RE-IMAGINING CHINA: THE CHINESE IDENTITY CRISIS AT THE START OF THE SOUTHERN DYNASTIES PERIOD CHARLES HOLCOMBE UNIVERSITY OF NORTHERN IOWA At the beginning of the fourth century much of northern China fell under the control of ethnic non-Chinese. The remnants of the Chinese court fled to the still exotic region south of the Yangtze River, where they established an Eastern Chin dynasty in exile. Even there, a variety of ethnic "mi- norities" may in fact have constituted an absolute majority of the population. The 6migre leaders of this first southern dynasty therefore confronted the serious challenge of having to recreate the "Chi- nese" state under distinctly unfavorable conditions. But if the imperial pattern of centralized autoc- racy had been sharply eroded, cultural expectations enshrined in the Confucian classics continued to provide the elite office-holding class known as the shih-ta-fu with a firm sense of their own identity and self-worth, ensuring that the idea "China" would be able to survive the political turmoil of the great age of division.
