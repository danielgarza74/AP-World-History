The Mawangdui map covers the southwestern part of the early Western Han regional state of Changsha.103

In 111 BCE, the new commandery of Lingling was established in this area. By the beginning of the common era, its registered population amounted to 139,378 persons in 21,092 households,104 a 23-fold increase over some 170 years. This suggests an annual growth rate of 13%, which could not have been purely natural. While it was probably partly due to immigration, another key factor is likely to have been the successful registration of indigenous people during the Han era. This often involved more or less voluntary resettlement from the highlands to agricultural valleys, where the government administration and tax collection were easier (see Chapter 7).

This process was just beginning at the time when the Qin established its administration in the “new territories” south of the Yangzi. During the fifteen years before the collapse of the Qin Empire, the vast majority of local people probably remained beyond the government’s reach. County authorities in Qianling had but a vague idea of the number of “barbarians” on the mountain plateaus surrounding the river valley where the Qin officials, soldiers, and settlers found security behind town walls. The central government’s anxiety about hostile incursions from the “new territories” owed much to the lack of control and understanding of the populations and resources in the recently conquered lands outside the nodes of Qin administrative and military presence. The distribution and composition of this state network are the subject of the next chapter.

Concluding remarks: A house divided against itself?

When news of the outbreak of an anti-Qin rebellion reached Shandong in October of 209 BCE, a certain Tian Dan paid visit to the county town of Di, some 40 km northwest of the former capital of the state of Qi, Linzi. This man belonged to the royal house of Qi, whose defeat at the hands of Qin in 221 BCE had signified the end of the Warring States era. Claiming that he had come to apply for official permission to execute a misbehaving slave, Dan met the county magistrate, whom he immediately assassinated. Dan wasted no time in summoning the local bigwigs and the young men (probably useful as a fighting force) and addressed them with an energetic speech: “The lords of the former states have all revolted against Qin and enthroned themselves. As Qi was a state founded in ancient times and I, Dan, belong to the Tian clan, I should be made King [of Qi].” Not only was this enough for Tian Dan to be immediately proclaimed ruler of Qi, he was also soon commanding a sizeable military force and established his authority over the entire Qi territory.105

Other such episodes of the meteoric rise of more or less obscure scions of ruling and aristocratic families dot the Shiji narrative of the last days of the Qin Empire. To destroy the empire’s rule in the “new territories,” it was often sufficient to kill a local Qin official, a commandery governor, or even a county magistrate, as in Tian Dan’s case.106 Besides casting grave doubt on the efficacy of Qin’s measures to uproot the political class of the conquered states, as well as on its ability to monitor the situation on the ground,107 these records imply that the entire communication and logistical infrastructure necessary for mobilizing, organizing, supplying, and leading to battle large masses of troops was readily available to the rebel leaders. This tallies well with the imperial Qin legislation’s concern about the presence in the “new territories” of military forces powerful enough to stage invasions across the old Qin frontier.

It is an understatement to say that the “new territories” – which comprised roughly half of the empire’s territory and probably well over half of its population – were never fully integrated. It may be more appropriate to consider them as a space where people and resources were contested by coexisting political forces – the imperial administration, concentrated in the state spaces around fortified towns and along crucial communication routes, as against regional and local power holders, including aristocratic clans, members of the deposed royal families, and leaders of non-Sinitic groups – who exerted control over much of the hinterland. In less than fifteen years, Qin imposed at least three major revisions of its administrative borders, while its peripatetic commandery authorities were in a frantic quest for the optimal configuration of control over the territories and populations, one which would maximize their logistical, military, and fiscal advantages.

On the southern frontier, these efforts were frustrated by the ongoing warfare, which diverted already thinly stretched resources toward different priorities. The quality of these resources left much to be desired: officials, soldiers, and colonists had to be brought in by force, often as criminals or debtors with little choice but to redeem themselves through service in the perilous “new territories.” The efficiency and loyalty of such personnel always remained in question, so the local governments were additionally burdened with monitoring their subordinates and reporting to their superiors, adding to the transaction costs of the administrative machine.

What we should wonder at is not so much why this system collapsed as how it managed to endure as long as it did, and, moreover, how it was able to leave behind a legacy that contributed to the reintegration of southern East Asia into the Sinitic empire less than a century after the fall of Qin. Despite all odds, local Qin officials were not just surviving but exploring new environments, mapping landscapes, engineering communities, transforming economies, and finding ways to make their remote locales relevant to the imperial project. How they achieved this is the central topic of the next two chapters.
