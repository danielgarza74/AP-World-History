Selection Overview

Blenkinsopp compares the Egyptian official Udjahorresnet's inscription with the memoirs of Ezra and Nehemiah, arguing that all three men were pro-Persian local notables sent home as agents of imperial policy to restore temple cults and reorganize local law. He sets their missions within an Achaemenid policy that "tended to support local priesthoods" and codified traditional law codes, while noting that the sources are self-serving and that Ezra may have "overstepped his commission."

It was copied by Champollion following on Napoleon's Egyptian expedition and has been frequently studied since by Egyptologists.2 The statue, restored somewhat ineptly in the eighteenth century, comes from the early years of the reign of Darius I. Since it records that Darius was in Elam at the time of Udjahorresnet's mission and does not refer to the royal visit to Egypt, it must have been inscribed and set up shortly before that visit in the fourth year of the reign (518 B.C.). The author of the inscription assigns himself a whole range of honorific titles of which the most frequently recurring is that of chief physician conferred on him by Cambyses for services rendered after the conquest of Egypt in 525. Among the other titles is that of scribe, and he informs us that his responsibilities included super- vision of scribes in the great outer hall. After the invasion of Cambyses, an event to which he alludes in deliberately vague terms as "the very great turmoil,' he actively collaborated with the Persians, initiating Cambyses into Egyptian customs, religious beliefs and observances: I informed His Majesty about the greatness of Sais, residence of the great Neith who gave birth to Re and began childbearing when there was yet no childbearing. I also informed him about the greatness of Neith's temple, which is heaven in every respect, and about the greatness of the castles of the red crown and of the gods and goddesses who are in them, and how great was "the palace of the bee," residence of the Sovereign, the Lord of Heaven.... (lines 15-17)5 He also served as master of protocol, composing the titulary of Cambyses as heir to the pharaonic throne, and for these services, as noted, he was appointed chief physician and majordomo of the palace. He is at some pains to point out that he acted in this way to spare his family, and the country in general, the worst effects of the "turmoil,' the like of which had never been seen in the land. Udjahorresnet goes on to inform his readers-in the first place the gods-that he used his new position to influence Cambyses to carry out a thorough restoration of the cult at the dynastic sanctuary of Sais: I petitioned the Majesty of the king of Upper and Lower Egypt, Cambyses, concerning all the foreigners who had settled in the sanctuary of Neith, that they be expelled from it, so that the sanctuary of Neith be restored to all its former splendor. So His Majesty commanded that all the foreigners who had settled in the sanctuary of Neith be expelled, and that all their houses be demolished together with all their impure things which were in this sanctuary.... His Majesty commanded that the sanctuary of Neith be cleansed, and that all its personnel be returned to it, the [ ] and the hour-priests of the temple. His Majesty commanded that divine offer- ings be made to the great Neith, mother of the god, and to the great gods that are in Sais, as it had been before. His Majesty commanded that all their festivals and processions be carried out as had been done previously. (lines 19-25) The restoration, therefore, included the following: expulsion of foreigners from the temple precincts: elimination of all ritual impurities; installation of legitimate cult personnel; reestablishment of traditional religious observ- ances; provision of the necessary support from the Persian government, a point more explicitly made in the next section (lines 29-31). It will be already apparent that the inscription gives a very different portrait of Cambyses from that of Herodotus and other authors of Greek and Roman antiquity.
