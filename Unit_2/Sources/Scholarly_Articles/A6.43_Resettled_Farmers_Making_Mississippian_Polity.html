Selection Overview

Pauketat argues from survey and excavation of the upland "Richland Complex" that thousands of farmers resettled near Cahokia at the very moment of its abrupt founding around 1050 C.E., and that this resettlement was part of how the Mississippian order was made rather than a mere result of it. Cahokian leaders may have had a hand in the movement, he allows, but the process was "collaborative, consensual, and communal," a two-way negotiation in which "Mississippian farmers had agency."

In this paper, I argue that the localized move- ments of people are inseparable components of the cultural processes that we must explain; they are not merely the consequences of causal forces that exist outside the doing or being of the people involved (cf. Barth 1969:21). To show how resettlement can be part and parcel of cultural process, I conduct an analysis of population density, house longevity, and site occupation span based on (1) a re-examination of the pre-Columbian residential-administrative cen- ter of Cahokia, located in southwestern Illinois, and (2) large-scale excavations of a series of outlying rural villages in the "Richland Complex" (Figure 1). With the density, longevity, and occupation-span measures, I argue that the Richland villagers were physically displaced immediately prior to or simul- taneous with Cahokia's abrupt coalescence. The extent and timing of their resettlement relative to various cultural changes constitute the principle lines of evidence to argue that Richland village farmers, to an important degree, shaped Cahokia's "Missis- sippian" cultural order. The construction of this order was inseparable from resettlement in the Greater Cahokia region. The Greater Cahokia case illustrates the chang- ing principles of explanation in archaeology that I have elsewhere labeled "historical processual" per- spectives (Pauketat 2001a, 2001b).
