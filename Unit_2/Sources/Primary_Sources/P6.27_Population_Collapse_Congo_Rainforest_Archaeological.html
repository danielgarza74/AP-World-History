Population Collapse in the Congo Rainforest: An Archaeological Study of the Bantu Expansion (2021)
Modern study (published February 2021) of 1,149 radiocarbon dates and 115 pottery styles from the Congo rainforest, covering about 1000 B.C.E. to 1900 C.E.
 
TOC FIT Alternatives to Civilization: Bantu Africa: Cultural Encounters and Social Variation (textbook pp. 247–249).
WHY HERE Modern archaeological evidence on whether settlement of the Congo rainforest by farming, pottery-making communities was one continuous process or was broken by a collapse and a later resettlement.
TREATMENT Excerpts from an open-access scientific article (CC BY-NC 4.0, noncommercial use only), reproduced with attribution and disclosure of changes. This is modern research, not ancient evidence.

Historical Context

This is not an ancient document. It is a 2021 article in the journal Science Advances by seven archaeologists, ecologists, geneticists, and linguists based in Belgium and Sweden, led by Dirk Seidensticker and Koen Bostoen of Ghent University, used here as evidence because no writing records the Bantu expansion. Its evidence is mainly archaeological. The authors collected every published radiocarbon date from pottery-making sites in the Congo rainforest and the woodlands next to it, kept the 1,149 they judged reliable, and sorted 115 pottery styles (“pottery groups”) by date and place. The “SPD” (summed probability distribution) adds all the dates together into one curve; more dated sites in a century is read as more human activity, and so, roughly, more people. One short section adds DNA evidence: a computer estimate of past population size from the genomes of living Bantu-speaking communities in Gabon. The Early Iron Age and Late Iron Age are the archaeologists’ names for the first and second periods of village settlement.

This evidence differs from the other entries on this debate. U2-P184 and P6.6 read DNA, mostly of living people: U2-P184 finds a spread at a roughly constant rate with local mixing, and P6.6 finds forager ancestry in two Zambian communities. P6.5 and P6.7 are objects and paintings from southern Africa. This study reads where and when villagers left datable pottery and charcoal, and finds a break in the rainforest: a fall in dated sites between about 400 and 600 C.E., very few from 600 to 1000, and new, different pottery styles after about 1000 C.E. Separate what is measured from what is inferred. The measurements are counts and dates of sites and pottery styles. That the potters spoke Bantu languages (the authors write “putative Bantu-speaking”), that fewer dates mean fewer people rather than less research, and that an epidemic caused the fall are inferences, and the authors call the epidemic a “hypothesis” with “no firm evidence.” In 2021 other archaeologists and environmental scientists disputed the collapse in eLetters (online responses) on the journal’s page, titled in part “there is no evidence of a population collapse in Congo rainforest from 400 to 600 CE,” and the authors replied. The study covers the rainforest only; it says nothing about forager peoples such as the Batwa, and it does not show whether the remnant villagers were absorbed by newcomers or simply died out.

Document Text

Abstract

The present-day distribution of Bantu languages is commonly thought to reflect the early stages of the Bantu Expansion, the greatest migration event in African prehistory. Using 1149 radiocarbon dates linked to 115 pottery styles recovered from 726 sites throughout the Congo rainforest and adjacent areas, we show that this is not the case. Two periods of more intense human activity, each consisting of an expansion phase with widespread pottery styles and a regionalization phase with many more local pottery styles, are separated by a widespread population collapse between 400 and 600 CE followed by major resettlement centuries later. Coinciding with wetter climatic conditions, the collapse was possibly promoted by a prolonged epidemic. Comparison of our data with genetic and linguistic evidence further supports a spread-over-spread model for the dispersal of Bantu speakers and their languages.

From the Results: “Bimodal temporal pattern of 14C ages across the Congo rainforest”

[. . .] The logistic growth model is probably most pertinent in the context of the Bantu Expansion, which is often presented as a large-scale and exceptionally rapid process followed by a continuous presence of Bantu-speaking people after the initial expansion. Our analysis shows that the SPD of Congo rainforest 14C dates deviates from this hypothetical pathway. Instead, it shows clear periods of significantly higher and lower degrees of human activity than predicted.

[. . .] The SPD of all screened archeological 14C dates from within the Congo rainforest (n = 1075) indicates that the Early Iron Age ended with a collapse between ~400 and 600 CE, followed by a period of markedly low human activity between ~600 and 1000 CE, during which the SPD falls short of all four tested hypothetical growth models. Only 61 sites scattered across the Congo rainforest have class I 14C dates from the period 600 to 1000 CE, suggesting that these represent remnant communities.

From the Results: “Repetition of a two-phase settlement pattern in pottery-style distributions”

Analysis of the temporal distribution of the 115 recognized pottery groups in the Congo rainforest shows a marked repetition of the same two-phase settlement pattern during both the Early Iron Age and Late Iron Age. Each expansion phase (~1000 BCE to 0 CE and ~1000 to 1400 CE) is characterized by an initially low but increasing number of pottery groups, which are stylistically homogeneous across large numbers of sites and consequently tend to have a large distribution area. In contrast, the subsequent periods of high activity (~0 to 400 CE and ~1400 to 1900 CE) are characterized by a large number of pottery groups, of which many are found at only a few sites and consequently have a smaller mean area of distribution, indicative of a regionalization process.

[. . .] In addition, pottery styles prevailing during the Late Iron Age are always substantially different from those of the Early Iron Age. [. . .]

From the Results: “Reconstructing demographic patterns with genetic evidence”

[. . .] Thus, genetic data support our archeological inferences that today’s Bantu-speaking communities in Gabon did not move into that area of the rainforest before the second expansion phase (1000 to 1500 CE) and do not descend from its Early Iron Age inhabitants. [. . .] Collectively, our findings indicate that the original (semi)sedentary inhabitants of region B may have either moved elsewhere during the collapse period (400 to 600 CE), ceased to exist as distinct populations, or shrank to very low numbers in the period 600 to 1000 CE and were subsequently absorbed by the newly immigrating ancestors of present-day Gabonese Bantu-speaking communities.

From the Discussion: “Spread-over-spread model of Bantu Expansion”

Until now, the dispersal of Bantu-speaking communities in the Congo rainforest has tended to be seen as a single, and long-term continuous, macroevent. In this traditional view, today’s Bantu speakers descended in a direct line from those who originally settled the rainforest ~2700 years ago, and current-day Bantu languages developed directly from the ancestral languages of those first settlers. However, our results show that this initial wave of putative Bantu-speaking Early Iron Age communities largely vanished from the entire Congo rainforest region by ~600 CE, with the persistence of only a few scattered populations. The population decline was rapid between ~400 and 600 CE and then continued at a slower pace to eventually result in about 400 years of very limited sedentary activity (~600 to 1000 CE) before a second wave of immigration and new settlements developed into the Late Iron Age.

This spread-over-spread model of Bantu-speaking communities in the Congo rainforest, already hinted at by integration of genetic and linguistic evidence, is now firmly corroborated by the archeological data. [. . .] These distinct breaks in material culture confirm the existence of two clearly distinct periods of settlement in the rainforest.

From the Discussion: “Possible drivers of population collapse”

[. . .] At present, there is no firm evidence for persistent vector-borne diseases to have afflicted the Congo rainforest communities during the period of population collapse. [. . .] More genetic research would be needed to corroborate our hypothesis that the strong population decline during 400 to 600 CE revealed by our data may have been caused by a vector-borne epidemic.

Our regional SPD analysis of archeological 14C dates strongly supports the notion of long-term connectivity between Early Iron Age agricultural communities inhabiting different regions of the Congo rainforest, corroborating the evidence for the introduction of iron metallurgy and cereal cultivation to previously settled Central African communities. [. . .]

From the Discussion: “Reassessment of the Bantu Expansion in linguistics and paleoecology”

Genealogical classifications of present-day Bantu languages are commonly interpreted as reflecting the initial wave of migration through sub-Saharan Africa. Our results imply that some of the ancestral languages spoken between ~1000 BCE and ~400 CE have almost certainly become extinct during the period of low human population density ~1000 years ago and can no longer be factored in to reconstruct the original routes of Bantu Expansion. [. . .] Although patchy persistence of remnant populations during ~600 to 1000 CE suggests that not all Early Iron Age forest communities disappeared, present-day Bantu languages in the Congo rainforest may descend from languages that were (re)introduced during the second migration wave and could thus be up to 1000 years younger than previously thought.

[. . .] Our integrated assessment of the archeological evidence reveals a supraregional decline of Early Iron Age Bantu-speaking communities across the Congo rainforest, with notable loss of both their material culture and linguistic heritage. This widespread population collapse created a nearly blank canvas for the rise of new communities, material culture, and languages during the Late Iron Age. These groundbreaking insights urge reassessment of the Bantu Expansion as a spread-over-spread process rather than a single and long-term continuous macroevent.
