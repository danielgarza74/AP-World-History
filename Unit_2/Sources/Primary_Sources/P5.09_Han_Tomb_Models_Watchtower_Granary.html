Primary Source P5.9
Han Tomb Models: A Watchtower and a Granary Tower
Eastern Han dynasty, ca. 1st–early 3rd century C.E. (Met dating: 1–233)
 
TOC FIT Society and the State in China: The Landlord Class (textbook pp. 190–191).
WHY HERE Models placed in Han tombs show how a wealthy family wanted its estate remembered: grain stored in towers, and guards with bows watching from above.
TREATMENT Open photographs of the actual objects (Metropolitan Museum of Art, Open Access, CC0): three views of the Watchtower with Four Archers and one view of a Granary Tower.
ESSAY PROMPTS 5.5, 5.2
ARGUMENT ROLES 5.5.3 Supports; 5.5.2 Supports; 5.2.2 Qualifies
Historical Context

In the Eastern Han period (25–220 C.E.), families who could afford it buried their dead with pottery models of what a household needed in the next life: houses, wells, animals, mills, granaries, and towers. Potters made these objects for burial, usually in lead-glazed earthenware. They show what buyers valued and what craftsmen could supply, not an ordinary farmer’s home, since most farmers could not have bought glazed models for a grave.

The tall tower with a bowl-shaped base is called a Watchtower with Four Archers: figures with bows look out of its upper windows, and small modeled water birds and other animals stand in the basin. The smaller cylinder with a tiled roof is a Granary Tower. Together they suggest an estate with its own storage and armed guards, as one family imagined it. Compare them with the accounts of great landholders in this reader’s Han shu entry, and ask what a model can and cannot show about who held land and force in the countryside.

Editorial note. The photographs are modern museum photographs of the objects as they exist today. The museum’s online photograph of the Granary Tower is black and white; the object is glazed earthenware. The museum gives both objects the date range 1–233 C.E. Images are resized for print; nothing else is altered.
