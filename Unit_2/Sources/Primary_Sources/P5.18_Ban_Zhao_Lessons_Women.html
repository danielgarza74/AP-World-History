Historical Context

Ban Zhao (ca. 45–116 C.E.) came from a scholarly family. She married at fourteen and was widowed young; her biography presents her as living by strict propriety. After her brother Ban Gu died she completed the eight tables and the treatise on astronomy that he had left unfinished (the court later had Ma Xu continue the work), taught the empress and the ladies of the court, and advised the regent Empress Dowager Deng. Between about 100 and 110 C.E., probably after the regency began in 106, she wrote Nüjie (Lessons for Women), seven short chapters addressed to her daughters before they married. It was an early and very influential work of its kind, and later generations used it as a manual for girls.

The text comes to us in her biography in the Hou Han shu, written by Fan Ye in the fifth century. It is a woman’s own words, but also a prescriptive text from an elite woman who accepted the Confucian order, not a description of how most women lived. In chapter 2 she asks why girls should not be taught from the age of eight as boys are, and in chapter 3 she says that quarrels that lead to scolding and beating destroy a marriage. Think about what her demands are for and whom they serve.

Preface

I am a coarse and dull person, of a slow nature, who received the leftover favor of my late father and relied on the instruction and teaching of my mother and my teachers. At fourteen I carried the dustpan and broom into the Cao household [that is, became a wife there], and now more than forty years have passed. Trembling and fearful, I was always afraid of being sent away in disgrace, which would add to the shame of my parents and be a burden on those within and outside [the household]. Night and day I wearied my heart and worked hard without telling of weariness; only now do I know that I have escaped [disgrace]. By nature I am careless and obstinate, and my own teaching has lacked grounding, so I constantly fear that my son Zigu [Cao Cheng] will bring shame on the pure court. The imperial favor has been heaped on [us] beyond measure, and the gold [seal] and purple [ribbon] have unworthily been bestowed [on my son]; this is truly not what a person like me could have hoped for. As for my son, he can plan for himself, and I no longer worry about him. But I grieve over my daughters, who are about to marry and have not been taught step by step or heard the rites of a wife; I fear that they will lose their composure in another family and bring shame on the clan. Now my illness is long and heavy and life is uncertain; thinking of you in this condition, I am often sad. In spare moments I have written seven chapters of Lessons for Women, and I wish each of my daughters to copy out one set, in the hope that it will be of use and will help you in your own lives. From now on, work hard at it!

Chapter 1: Humility and weakness

In ancient times, when a girl was born, on the third day they laid her under the bed, gave her a tile [a spinning-tile] to play with, and made an announcement with a purifying fast. Laying her under the bed made clear that she is lowly and weak, and that her business is to put herself beneath others. Giving her a tile to play with showed that she should practice hard work and take charge of diligence. Announcing it to the ancestors showed that she should take charge of continuing the sacrifices. These three are the constant way of women, the canonical teaching of ritual and law. To be modest and yielding, respectful and reverent; to put others first and oneself last; to claim no credit for what is good and decline no blame for what is bad; to endure insult and swallow shame, always as if in fear: this is called being lowly and humble before others. To go to bed late and rise early, not shrinking from night or morning; to take up her tasks, private or otherwise, not refusing hard or easy, completing what she does, and keeping her handiwork neat and in order: this is called taking charge of diligence. To keep a correct face and upright conduct in serving her husband, to be pure and quiet and keep herself in order, not to be fond of jokes and laughter, and to prepare wine and food clean and ready to offer to the ancestors: this is called continuing the sacrifices. If these three are fully met, I have never seen a woman who then feared that her good name would not be heard or that disgrace would settle on her. If these three are lost, how can a good name be heard, and how can disgrace be kept away?

Chapter 2: Husband and wife

The relation of husband and wife is a pairing of yin and yang that reaches the spirits; it is truly the broad meaning of Heaven and Earth and the great norm of human relations. So the Rites value the boundary between men and women, and the Odes make the meaning of “Guan ju” plain. From this it follows that it cannot be taken lightly. If a husband is unworthy, he lacks the means to govern his wife; if a wife is unworthy, she lacks the means to serve her husband. If a husband does not govern his wife, his bearing and dignity decline and fall away; if a wife does not serve her husband, moral duty is broken and abandoned. Compare these two matters: their purpose is one. Looking at the gentlemen of today, they merely understand that a wife must be governed and that dignity must be kept in order, and so they instruct their sons and test them by books and records. They entirely fail to understand that a husband must be served and that ritual and rightness must be preserved. To teach boys and not teach girls—is this not being blind to the principle of both sides? According to the Rites, a child begins to be taught writing at eight, and by fifteen reaches formal study. Why can this alone not be taken as the rule [for girls]?

Chapter 3: Respect and caution

Yin and yang differ in nature, and men and women differ in conduct. Yang takes firmness as its virtue and yin takes softness as its use; in a man, strength is what is prized; in a woman, weakness is what is called beautiful. So the common saying has it: “Bear a boy like a wolf, and you still fear he will be feeble; bear a girl like a mouse, and you still fear she will be a tiger.” Thus for cultivating oneself nothing equals respect, and for avoiding [clashes of] strength nothing equals compliance. So it is said: the way of respect and compliance is a woman’s great observance. Respect is nothing other than steadiness that lasts; compliance is nothing other than being broad-minded. Lasting steadiness means knowing where to stop and when enough is enough; broad-mindedness means valuing deference and lowliness. Husband and wife are fond of each other and do not part for life. In the bedchamber they go about together, and so familiarity and indecorum arise. Once these arise, speech goes too far; once speech goes too far, license will follow; once license has begun, a heart that holds the husband in contempt is born. This comes from not knowing where to stop and when enough is enough. In any matter there is right and wrong, and in words there is true and false. The one in the right cannot but contend, and the one in the wrong cannot but argue back. Once quarrel and contention are under way, there will be angry rages. This comes from not valuing deference and lowliness. When contempt for the husband is not restrained, reproach and scolding follow; when angry rage does not cease, the beating rod follows. For those who are husband and wife, righteousness is what makes them close and harmonious, and kindness is what makes them well matched. Once beating has begun, what righteousness is left? Once scolding has been spread, what kindness is there? When kindness and righteousness are both discarded, husband and wife are parted.

Chapter 4: Womanly qualifications

A woman has four practices: first, womanly virtue; second, womanly speech; third, womanly appearance; fourth, womanly work. Womanly virtue does not require brilliant talent or exceptional ability; womanly speech does not require eloquence and sharp words; womanly appearance does not require a beautiful face; womanly work does not require skill beyond that of others. To be quiet and at ease, chaste and still, to keep her integrity and be orderly, to act with a sense of shame, and to move and be still by rule: this is called womanly virtue. To choose words when she speaks, not to use bad language, to speak at the right time so as not to tire others: this is called womanly speech. To wash away dust and dirt, to keep her garments and adornments fresh and spotless, to bathe at the proper times, and not to let her body be soiled and shamed: this is called womanly appearance. To give her mind to spinning and weaving, not to be fond of jokes and laughter, and to prepare wine and food clean and ready to serve guests: this is called womanly work. These four are the great virtues of a woman, and none of them can be lacking. Yet they are very easy to do; all that matters is to keep them in mind. The ancients had a saying: “Is humaneness far away? If I wish for humaneness, then humaneness has arrived.” That is what this means.

Chapter 5: Whole-hearted devotion

By the Rites, a husband has the principle of marrying again, but no text allows a wife to marry twice; therefore it is said, “The husband is Heaven.” Heaven certainly cannot be escaped, and a husband certainly cannot be left. If her conduct goes against the spirits, Heaven punishes her; if ritual and rightness are breached, her husband slights her. So the Pattern for Women says: “To win the favor of one person is called being settled for life; to lose the favor of one person is called being ended for life.” [The two Chinese verbs are near synonyms meaning “finished”; the likely sense is security versus ruin, though both could be read as “the end of life.”] From this it follows that a wife cannot but seek her husband’s heart. But what she seeks is not flattery and fawning to gain closeness; nothing is better than single-minded devotion and a correct demeanor. To live by ritual and rightness and purity, with ears that do not listen to idle talk and eyes that do not look at what is improper, not going out with a showy appearance and not neglecting her dress at home, not gathering with groups of companions and not looking out at doors and gates: this is called single-minded devotion and a correct demeanor. Chapter 6: Yielding and following

[ . . . ] How can a woman afford to lose the heart of her husband’s parents? Things may come apart through [loss of] affection, and they may break apart through [a clash of] righteousness. Even if the husband says he loves her, if her parents-in-law say she is wrong, this is what is meant by breaking apart through righteousness. What then is to be done about the hearts of the parents-in-law? Nothing is better than yielding and following. If her mother-in-law says “not so” and is right, she should certainly obey the order; if her mother-in-law says “so” and is wrong, she should still comply with the command. She must not contradict what they call right and wrong or argue over who is straight and who is crooked. This is what is called yielding and following. So the Pattern for Women says: “If a wife is like an echo and a shadow, how could she fail to be rewarded [with approval]?”
