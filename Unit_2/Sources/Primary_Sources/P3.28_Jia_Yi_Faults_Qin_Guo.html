Jia Yi, The Faults of Qin (Guo Qin lun), Part 1
written probably in the 170s B.C.E. (Jia Yi, 200–168 B.C.E.); surveys Qin history from 361 to 206 B.C.E.
 
TOC FIT China: From Warring States to Empire; The Collapse of Empires (textbook pp. 117–118, 122–124).
WHY HERE A Han scholar’s verdict, written about a generation after the fall, on how Qin built its power over six reigns and conquered the world, and on why it fell to a revolt begun by a band of conscripts.

Historical Context

Jia Yi (200–168 B.C.E.) was a young scholar from Luoyang whom Emperor Wen of Han (r. 180–157 B.C.E.) brought to court as an Erudite (boshi) when he was in his early twenties. He proposed sweeping reforms of ritual, titles, and the power of the kings who held territories inside the empire; senior officials resented him, and he was sent away as tutor to a king in the south. He died at thirty-three. Guo Qin lun (The Faults of Qin, or Finding Fault with Qin) is a three-part essay, probably written in the 170s B.C.E., about thirty years after the Qin dynasty fell in 206 B.C.E. It was famous within a century: Sima Qian copied it into the Shiji as the closing judgment on the First Emperor (chapter 6), and Ban Gu copied Part 1 into the Han shu (chapter 31). Part 1, translated here, follows Qin from Duke Xiao (r. 361–338 B.C.E.) and his minister Lord Shang to the unification of 221 B.C.E. and the rising of Chen She in 209 B.C.E.

Read it as an argument, not a chronicle. Jia Yi was an official of the dynasty that replaced Qin, writing to teach Han rulers a lesson, and he uses balanced phrases and deliberate exaggeration (a million corpses, a wall “a hundred million zhang” high). Many of the actions he lists, such as the new southern commanderies, Meng Tian’s wall, the burning of books, and the twelve metal statues cast from confiscated weapons, are also reported in the Shiji; the explanation in the last sentence is his own judgment. Notice what he admires in Qin’s rise as well as what he condemns, and that he says nothing here about the standard laws, weights, and script that the Han went on to use. Parts 2 and 3 of the essay, not reproduced, blame the Second Emperor and the last Qin ruler for failing to change course.

Editorial note. This is a new translation prepared for this reader by the editor from the Chinese text of Jia Yi’s Guo Qin lun as transcribed on Chinese Wikisource (過秦論, Part 1, 上). The text there is a composite: words found in only some versions (the Shiji, the Han shu, and the anthology Guwen guanzhi) are printed in parentheses, and they are translated here as part of the text (for example “hesitated” and “in the north”). It has not been checked by a specialist. Omitted, and marked [. . .]: in paragraph 1, the sentence naming the strategists, envoys, and generals of the six states (Ning Yue, Su Qin, Wu Qi, Sun Bin, Lian Po, and others); and all of paragraph 2, a single sentence saying that the next two kings, Xiaowen and Zhuangxiang, reigned only briefly and that nothing of note happened (they are the fifth and sixth of the “six generations” in paragraph 3). Paragraph numbers are the editor’s and follow the five paragraphs of the Wikisource text. Square brackets mark words added for clarity. Two different states are romanized Wei (魏 and 衞); the second is marked “[the smaller state of] Wei.” Uncertain readings: “formed the horizontal alliance” (連衡) uses a term from the later diplomacy of the Warring States, so Jia Yi may be loose here; the blood “floated the shields” (漂櫓) could also be read “floated the oars”; “challenged all who passed” (誰何) could mean “dared anyone [to oppose them]”; “one of a band of men being moved” (遷徙之徒) may mean a conscript or a banished convict; “[its ruler] died at the hands of others” (身死人手) has no stated subject; and in the last sentence 勢 is rendered “conditions,” though it can also mean “position” or “strategic power.”
Document Text

Part 1 (上篇): how Qin rose, and how it fell

1. Duke Xiao of Qin [r. 361–338 B.C.E.] held the strongholds of Mount Xiao and the Hangu Pass and possessed the land of Yongzhou. Ruler and ministers held it firmly and watched for their chance at the house of Zhou. They had the intention of rolling up the world like a mat, gathering up everything under the sky, and bagging the four seas, and the heart to swallow the eight distant regions. At this time Lord Shang assisted him. At home [Lord Shang] set up laws and standards, made [the people] devote themselves to plowing and weaving, and put in order the equipment for defense and war; abroad he formed the horizontal alliance and set the feudal lords fighting one another. And so the men of Qin, with folded hands, took the lands beyond the Western River [the Yellow River].

After Duke Xiao died, [Kings] Huiwen, Wu, and Zhaoxiang inherited the old enterprise and followed the plans he had left. In the south they took Hanzhong; in the west they seized Ba and Shu; in the east they cut off rich and fertile lands; in the north they gathered in commanderies of strategic importance. The feudal lords were afraid. They met in alliance and plotted to weaken Qin. Not sparing rare vessels, precious treasures, or rich and fertile land, they used them to draw in the able men of the world. They formed the vertical alliance, bound themselves together, and joined as one. At this time Qi had [Lord] Mengchang, Zhao had [Lord] Pingyuan, Chu had [Lord] Chunshen, and Wei had [Lord] Xinling. These four lords were all clear-sighted and wise, loyal and trustworthy, generous and kind to others, and they honored worthy men and valued men of ability. They pledged the vertical alliance and broke up the horizontal one, uniting the hosts of Han, Wei, Yan, Chu, Qi, Zhao, Song, [the smaller state of] Wei, and Zhongshan. [. . .] Once, with ten times the territory and a host of a million, they came knocking at the pass to attack Qin. The men of Qin opened the pass and invited the enemy in, and the armies of the nine states hesitated and did not dare advance. Qin spent not so much as a lost arrow or a dropped arrowhead, and yet the feudal lords of the world were already in distress. Then the vertical alliance scattered and its pact failed, and they competed to cut off land to bribe Qin. Qin, with strength to spare, took advantage of their exhaustion. It pursued the fleeing and chased the routed; the corpses lying on the ground numbered a million, and the flowing blood floated the shields. Following its advantage and seizing its opportunities, it carved up the world and divided its mountains and rivers. Strong states begged to submit, and weak states came to its court.

[. . .]

3. When it came to the First Emperor, he carried on the achievements left by six generations [of Qin rulers], cracked his long whip and drove the universe before him, swallowed the two Zhou [courts] and destroyed the feudal lords, mounted the supreme position and controlled the six directions, and took up the cudgel and the rod to flog the world; his might shook the four seas. In the south he took the lands of the Hundred Yue and made them the commanderies of Guilin and Xiang. The lords of the Hundred Yue bowed their heads, tied cords around their necks, and placed their lives in the hands of [Qin’s] lower officials. Then he sent Meng Tian north to build the Long Wall [the Great Wall] and guard its fences [the frontier], and drove the Xiongnu back more than seven hundred li [a li was about a quarter of a mile]. The Hu [the northern peoples] did not dare come south to pasture their horses, and their warriors did not dare bend their bows to take revenge. Then he abolished the way of the former kings and burned the words of the hundred schools, in order to make the black-headed people [the common people] ignorant. He tore down famous city walls, killed the powerful and bold, and collected the weapons of the world, gathering them at Xianyang. He melted down the blades and arrowheads and cast them into twelve metal statues, in order to weaken the people of the world. Then he trod on Mount Hua to make it his wall and used the [Yellow] River as his moat. He held a wall a hundred million zhang [a zhang was about seven and a half feet] high and looked down on an abyss whose depth could not be measured, and made these his defenses. Good generals with strong crossbows guarded the strategic places; trusted ministers and picked soldiers set out sharp weapons and challenged all who passed. The world was now settled. In the First Emperor’s heart he believed that the strongholds within the passes, a city of metal walls a thousand li around, were an enterprise for his sons and grandsons to rule as emperors for ten thousand generations.

4. After the First Emperor died, his lingering might still awed lands of distant customs. Yet Chen She was the son of a house with a broken jar for a window and a rope for a door hinge, a field laborer and a menial, one of a band of men being moved [to garrison the frontier]. His talents did not match those of an ordinary man; he had neither the worthiness of Zhongni [Confucius] or Mo Di [Mozi] nor the wealth of Tao Zhu or Yi Dun [two famously rich men]. He set foot among the ranks of soldiers and rose up suddenly from among the paths between the fields. Leading worn-out and exhausted soldiers, at the head of a band of a few hundred, he turned and attacked Qin. They cut down trees for weapons and raised poles for flags. The world gathered like clouds and answered like an echo; [men] shouldered their own grain and followed him like shadows. The bold and outstanding men east of the mountains rose up together, and in the end they destroyed the house of Qin.

5. Now, the world was no smaller or weaker [than before]; the land of Yongzhou and the strongholds of Xiao and Hangu were just as they had been. Chen She’s position was not more honored than that of the rulers of Qi, Chu, Yan, Zhao, Han, Wei, Song, [the smaller] Wei, and Zhongshan; hoes, rakes, and staves of thornwood were not sharper than hooked halberds and long lances; a band of men banished to garrison duty was no match for the armies of the nine states; and in deep plans and far-reaching thought, and in the art of moving armies and using troops, they did not come up to the men of earlier times. And yet success and failure turned out differently, and the results were the reverse. Why? If we tried measuring the states east of the mountains against Chen She for length and size, and compared their power and weighed their strength, they could not even be spoken of in the same breath. Yet Qin, starting from a tiny territory, rose to the power of ten thousand chariots, set the eight provinces in order, and made those of equal rank come to its court, for more than a hundred years. After that it made the six directions its home and Xiao and Hangu its palace walls. Yet when one common fellow made trouble, its seven ancestral temples fell, [its ruler] died at the hands of others, and it became the laughingstock of the world. Why? Because humaneness and righteousness were not practiced, and the conditions for attacking and for holding [what has been won] are different.
