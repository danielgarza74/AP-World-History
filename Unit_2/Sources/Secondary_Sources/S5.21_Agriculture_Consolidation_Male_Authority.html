Excerpt

Agriculture decisively changed the gender balance that had so long prevailed. Instead of a degree of equality, male superiority began to be more decisively asserted. In most cases, men took over the production of basic foods, particularly grains. Women might assist, and often took care of gardens and any livestock around the home, but their role diminished. A key factor was a change in birth rates. With greater food supplies, families could have more children—six to eight children per family would become a common norm— and they also needed more children to serve as the family labor supply in an economy where family members, collectively, served as the basic production unit. With more children, women spent more time in pregnancy and in care for the young, which reduced their overall economic range and confined them more closely to home. Some scholars have argued that a second factor—besides family size and work roles—helped generate new inequality. With farming, families increasingly sought to define property, so that whatever work they put into clearing trees and building wells would be passed on to their own children and subsequent generations, rather than a larger group. But with property transmission, concern about assuring paternity became more important: men began to seek greater control over women’s sexuality so that they would have greater certainty that they had sired the children of the union. Certainly, patriarchal systems characteristically included great attention to the regulation of women’s sexual activity, with efforts to limit premarital sex and adultery that were much less severe where male behavior was involved. This “double-standard” sexuality was an important measure of patriarchy, and of its remnants even in more contemporary settings. With growing economic and sexual inequality, patriarchy of some sort became firmly established in virtually every agricultural economy, though the adjustment may have taken some time. And with patriarchy, male dominance extended more fully. Men and male qualities were defined as not only different from but clearly superior to female weakness, and this was decisively translated into childrearing. Men were seen as clear heads of households and as politically dominant, with many systems largely forbidding women from even inheriting a political role. Property control was disproportionately—sometimes exclusively—male as well. Patriarchal systems often sought to limit women’s public activities even more generally, their lives centered on family and household. Patriarchal systems placed huge, often severe burdens on women, but they offered some partial compensations as well, which is one reason they survived so long. Individual women might assert a strong personality within a family, even if their larger roles were restricted. Inequality was more severe within the upper classes than in the peasant majority, where women’s work was still essential if less valuable than that of men. Birth rates were usually higher in the upper classes as well, another important gender factor. Working families might well need women to take on additional functions, for example in helping to market some produce, in ways that would modify the most confining patriarchal limitations. On another front, inheritance or talent would see some individual women gain opportunities for education or even leadership in certain circumstances, even when the larger system continued to favor men. Powerful women rulers thus dot the history of many agricultural societies, despite their infrequency. Women could also gain some voice through relationships, not only with husbands but even more with sons and daughters-in-law. Resultant family power could be quite real, and in the upper classes this sometimes extended to wider political roles as well; the power of “queen mothers” in agricultural kingdoms in Africa and elsewhere was a key example. Still, inequality and division of roles legitimately characterized gender relations in agricultural societies and in the long centuries in which agricultural economies would set the basic framework for human life. We do not know exactly how patriarchy spread: as groups of hunter-gatherers adopted agriculture from a neighboring region, did they deliberately copy some aspects of gender relations, or did new inequalities simply spring from the birth rates and labor needs of families once they became agricultural? Whatever the balance, agriculture and patriarchy unquestionably ultimately went hand in hand, creating some common core features in societies that otherwise seemed, and might fancy themselves as, quite distinct. As late as the early twenty-first century, world societies continue to deal with the powerful legacy of patriarchal systems, creating a set of key issues that we will be dealing with in Part III .

The Emergence of Civilizations

Over time, and well after patriarchal gender relations were established in some fashion, a number of agricultural societies began to generate conditions and needs that would usher in more complex forms of organization. By the fourth millennium BCE , a number of societies were beginning to move toward that phase of organization called “civilization.” While contacts among different groups were virtually as old as the existence of the human species, most early civilizations formed somewhat separately. Mesopotamian civilization, arising after 3500 BCE , thus differed from Egyptian civilization, which emerged soon after in North Africa, not too far to the south. Civilizations would usually deepen patriarchy, and at the same time they would define its details in distinctive ways that fitted the wider beliefs and institutions of each individual region. In this sense, in putting a particular stamp on patriarchy, each civilization linked gender issues to aspects of their cultural and institutional structure. This complex pattern would set the framework for the impact of interregional contacts on gender systems. Agriculture allowed the generation of some surplus production over immediate needs. On the strength of surplus, small numbers of people could specialize in non-agricultural activities, such as craft manufacturing, religion or government. [. . .]
