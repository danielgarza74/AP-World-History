Selection Overview

Spence uses survey data on hundreds of obsidian workshops to argue that the Teotihuacan state controlled the supply of green obsidian, took a levy from the workshops most dependent on it, and protected the city's monopoly over a wide market, but that it "protected and encouraged the obsidian industry but did not attempt to dominate all aspects of it." He traces the workshops' independence to strong corporate social groups that predated the state, while allowing that he may have underestimated how much control the state held.

OBSIDIAN PRODUCTION AND THE STATE IN TEOTIHUACAN Michael W. Spence Obsidian production was a major component of Classic period Teotihuacan's economy, with hundreds of workshops producing cores and blades of green obsidian and bifaces of grey obsidian for distribution through- out central Mexico. Although green obsidian was evidently obtained through a state network, grey obsidian procurement was conducted independently by most workshop groups. Most production took place in residen- tial areas beyond the major public structures of the city, but some of these craftsmen also worked periodically within the public precincts where their output would have been at the disposition of state officials. The burden of this levy fell primarily upon those sectors of the industry that were most dependent on the state for support and most involved in external distribution. However, despite this evidence of an important administered ele- ment in the Teotihuacan obsidian industry, the workshops apparently maintained their independence from the state. This may have been due to their organization in strong social units that had a long history of independent existence, and to their self-reliance in procurement and distribution during the earlier stages of the industry's development. THE ROLE OF THE STATE in the economic systems of archaic civilizations has been subject to considerable discussion (cf. Polanyi et al. 1957; Adams 1974). Much debate has focused upon long- distance trade, exchange beyond the borders of individual state systems, and upon the literate societies of the Near East and the contact period civilizations of the New World. We now need to obtain comparable data on the predecessors of these latter societies, and to explore production and exchange systems not only at an earlier point in their development but also from a more inter- nal perspective. Recent work oriented in such a direction has already provided some fresh in- sights (cf. Wright and Johnson 1975; Johnson 1975; Blanton 1978; Charlton 1978a). Teotihuacan, indisputably one of the world's great archaic civilizations, has considerable potential for an investigation of this sort. Situated in the northeast corner of the Valley of Mexico, it was a major urban center and a state from the Tzacualli phase (A.D. 0-150) to the end of the Metepec phase (A.D. 750). At its height it covered an area of 20 km2 and encompassed a popula- tion of 150,000 people (R. Millon 1973). It probably controlled most of central Mexico and may have held dominion over some areas as distant as highland Guatemala, some 1,000 km to the south. A large proportion of the city's population, perhaps up to 35%, was made up of nonagri- cultural specialists, many of them obsidian workers. Obsidian is a highly visible material and pro- vides a better data base for a study of craft specialization and economic patterns than do most materials. It therefore received a good deal of attention in our survey of the city. I will discuss in some detail the role of the Teotihuacan state in the procurement, processing, and distribution of obsidian. The state is viewed here as the bureaucracy that ruled both the city itself and the larger geopolitical area of which the city was undoubtedly the capital. For the pur- poses of this study the locus of state authority is necessarily defined in spatial and architectural terms. It must be admitted that there are some problems with this. It is possible of course, that some of these were associated with separate, even rival, institutions-different groups of priests, priests versus secular rulers, etc. Although it is quite likely that the structures did indeed represent distinguishable institutions, all will be treated here as manifestations to some degree of the Teotihuacan state. First, all of these structures were located along the Street of the Dead, which was partially closed off from the rest of the city (R. Millon 1973:39); and second, Clara Millon's work (1973) suggests that religious and secular authority were combined in Teotihuacan. Another point of uncertainty lies in the political identity of the smaller pyramids scattered widely throughout the city, several of which were associated with obsidian workshop3. It is not clear to what degree these represented locally derived institutions, separate from and perhaps even opposed to the state, rather than extensions of the state designed to maintain its authority on the local level. The course followed here is to treat them, in the absence of any clear evidence to the contrary, as "barrio" temples with an essentially local orientation. Workshops associated with these temples, then, will not be viewed as state production areas. Finally, there is the difficulty of translating architectural arrangements into patterns of inter- action and relationship. It is assumed below that workshops lying just beyond the precincts of the major public structures were free of direct state supervision over their production and exchange. It may be that their proximity to the public structures would have allowed fairly effective supervi- sion by state representatives, but I believe that the architectural separation has some signifi- cance. It left the workshops open to access by potential consumers from the city at large, and much of the craft activity occurred within the apartment compounds-enclosed residences that would not have been freely entered by public officials.
