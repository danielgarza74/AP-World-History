Selection Overview

Lubin argues that Aśoka's Rock Edict IX describes domestic ceremonies in the same terms as the Brahmanical Gṛhyasūtras, which Aśoka dismissed as women's folk rites while some Brahmin codes validated them as dharma. Because Aśoka honors Brahmins as teachers of a dhamma without linking them to ritual or to varna, Lubin concludes that "in his experience at least (i.e., in Magadha?) Brahmins' religious authority had nothing to do with domestic ritual," and that the Gṛhyasūtras were priestly efforts to "Vedicize" customary practice.

T. Lubin 30 At least since Hultzsch1 it has been recognized that king Asoka Maurya, that singular linchpin of early Indian history, contemned Brahmanical usage in Rock Edicts I and IX, as well as the ceremonies performed by women, advocating instead the practice of ethical virtues. What has not been noted is that degree to which Asoka's remarks identify distinctive aspects of the Vedic domestic ceremonies as represented in the Grhyasutras. Close attention to the wording of Rock Edict IX shows that he and the Grhyasutras talk about domestic rites in very similar terms. Moreover, both of them note the special role of women as a source of knowledge of such ceremonies. They differ only in how they evaluate the value of such ceremonial: Asoka disparages women's rites, while some Grhyasutras explicitly validate women as authorities in such matters. A comparison of these sources highlights the distinctive role of the term dhamma in Asoka's usage, and may provide some guidance for dating the Grhyasutras. Given the fact that the Brahmin ritualists who developed the rulebooks of domestic practice held views diametrically opposed to Asoka's, we may wonder at the well-known fact that he seems to have a very favorable view of Brahmins, regarding them as representatives of a venerable "dhamma" (albeit not the Buddha's dhamma, which Asoka himself had embraced).2 Asoka proudly records his generosity to Brahmins and ascetics (samana / samana), and urges his subjects to be generous to them as well. We will begin, therefore, by reconsidering what Asoka had to say about Brahmins. What was a "Brahmin" in the Maurya-Era Magadha? One hears about Brahmins from diverse sources in the last centuries bce, some of them Brahmanical (that is, Sanskrit works endorsing the Veda) and some of the markedly non-Brahmanical. The dating of most works of this period is approximate at best, but there is substantial circumstantial evidence at least to make some general comparative observations. The Zeitgeist of this phase of Indian history was shaped by the political and economic changes in north India—the emergence of cities as the hubs of territorially defined states, the concentration of wealth, power, and patronage in those cities in the hands of ràjans who were no longer tribal chieftains but overlords with courts, and the consequent weakening of the old village priest patron symbiosis. The most consequential of these new polities was the Maurya kingdom that arose at Pàtaliputra, in Magadha, which became the hub of India's first historical empire. 1 In his edition of the edicts (Hultzsch 1925, 1—1i). 2 Although in many other places Asoka's use of the word dhamma is informed by his devotion to the Buddhist sangha, in Rock Edict XII he clearly articulates an ecumenical conception of dhamma. He says that members of different sects—pâsanda*\ the term is not pejorative, unlike in Buddhist and Brahmanical texts—should not only listen to but also heed each other's dhammas, which will result in the "illumination of dhamma." He further posits an underlying "essence" (sâra) that can thereby be cultivated (-vadhi), and is the basis for a common dhamma shared by the distinct, individual dhammas of the various sects.
