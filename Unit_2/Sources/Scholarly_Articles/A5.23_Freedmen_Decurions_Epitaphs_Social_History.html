Selection Overview

Mouritsen asks why freed slaves make up most of the people named on Roman tombstones (about three quarters at Rome and Ostia) and, studying the cemeteries of Ostia and Pompeii closely, argues that freedmen commemorated their families in stone for reasons rooted in their own experience of passing from slavery to freedom, often privately and out of affection rather than to display status, while the local elite withdrew into public honorific monuments. He concludes that the epitaphs reveal separate social worlds, with freedmen forming "a marginal sub-community," and have "little value as a demographic source."

Freedmen and Decurions: Epitaphs and Social History in Imperial Italy"' HENRIK MOURITSEN I INTRODUCTION Hundreds of thousands of inscriptions, mostly epitaphs, have been preserved from the Roman Empire, and this material offers the ancient historian a rare opportunity to move beyond the anecdotal and, it would seem, study Roman social history using proper statistical methods. In principle, the funerary inscriptions ought to be able to give us an idea of the social make-up of important sections of the Roman population, i.e. those who could afford epigraphic commemoration. However, it has long been noted that these docu ments in many respects present a very peculiar profile of the Roman population. The age patterns indicated by the epitaphs are quite unrealistic. There are too many males in relation to females, as well as a marked preponderance of children over parents.1 These features alone warn against literal readings of the material, which is unlikely to present a direct reflection of socio-demographic structures and developments. But perhaps the most striking aspect relates to social and legal status, since the large majority of Romans com memorated in stone appear to come from a servile background. In the city of Rome it has been estimated that at least three quarters of those com memorated in funerary inscriptions were former slaves, while most of the freeborn appear to be first generation ingenui.2 In the imperial port of Ostia, closely linked to the capital, a sample of funerary inscriptions suggests a very similar picture with freedmen making u no less than 75 per cent of the individuals in the material, the remaining quarter largely consisting of their immediate relatives and descendants. This simple point provided an important new insight, which is the realizatio that inscriptions are the result of individual initiatives and personal motives that may n have been uniformly shared by all members of society. For that reason the material cann be approached as a demographic database.7 In recent decades there has been a growing realization that the production of inscrib stones in the Roman world represented a very specific cultural practice, which some peop might embrace and others ignore. Most attention has focused on the chronological limit ations of this 'habit', and attempts have been made to explain the 'rise and fall' of Rom epigraphy in broad historical terms.8 The arguments advanced have often been very general in their scope and application, and to further the debate we may have to engag more directly with the evidence itself, rather than with the modern concept of t 'epigraphic habit', which has taken on a life of its own within the scholarly discourse detached from the actual inscriptions. This paper reconsiders the basic question of why people put up inscriptions an epitaphs in particular, which make up the large majority of Roman documents on stone The answer may seem obvious ? to be remembered and to commemorate ? but wh faced with deceptively simple questions it is important to avoid 'common sense' response which may be little more than unreflected projections of contemporary practices and assumptions. Methodologically any attempt at addressing the issue will also have to st as close to the ground as possible. This study, therefore, attempts a fairly detailed study at micro-level of individual sites which provide an extensive epigraphic and archaeologica record.
