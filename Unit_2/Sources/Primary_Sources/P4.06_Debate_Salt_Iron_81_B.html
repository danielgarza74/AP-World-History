The Debate on Salt and Iron, 81 B.C.E.
court debate of 81 B.C.E.; written up by Huan Kuan, mid-1st century B.C.E.
 
TOC FIT China and the Search for Order: The Legalist Answer and The Confucian Answer; Han government (textbook pp. 144–146).
WHY HERE The fullest surviving Han record of a court conference at which Confucian scholars and state officials argued over state monopolies, profit, war finance, and the Qin statesman Shang Yang.
TREATMENT Fresh translation from the public-domain Chinese: selections from chapters 1 and 7 of the Yantie lun.
ESSAY PROMPTS 4.2, 5.2
ARGUMENT ROLES 4.2.3 Supports; 4.2.2 Qualifies; 5.2.1 Challenges
Historical Context

In 87 B.C.E. Emperor Wu died after a reign of costly wars against the Xiongnu nomads of the northern steppe. To pay for them the government had set up monopolies on salt and iron (and later on liquor) and a system of state trading called “equal transport” (junshu), run by officials, not private merchants. In 81 B.C.E. a court conference was convened under the regent Huo Guang. Sixty or so “worthy and good” men and “literati” (wenxue), scholars recommended from the provinces and trained in the Confucian classics, met Sang Hongyang, the Grand Secretary (also called the Imperial Secretary), the official who had managed the fiscal policies, and other ministers. The conference ended the liquor monopoly but kept the salt and iron monopolies. The Chinese text of the argument is the Yantie lun (“Discourses on Salt and Iron”), put into literary form by Huan Kuan some decades later.

The book is not a transcript: Huan Kuan shaped, arranged, and probably expanded the speeches, and the speakers are named by title rather than by name. Read for which arguments each side uses: moral teaching, ancient precedent, and the sayings of Confucius on the literati’s side; frontier defense, revenue, and the results Qin achieved on the officials’ side. In chapter 7 the quarrel turns to Shang Yang, the Legalist statesman of Qin, whom the Grand Secretary admires and the literati blame for Qin’s ruin.

Editorial note. Translated for this reader by the editor from the Chinese text of the Yantie lun as transcribed on Chinese Wikisource (chapters 1 and 7), compared with the Siku quanshu transcription of the same book. The two transcriptions differ in several variant characters and a few readings; this translation follows the first, with two kinds of exception. (1) The saying of Confucius quoted in the first chapter follows the standard wording of Analects 16.1 (the first transcription swaps “few” and “poor”); the second quotation of Confucius in chapter 1 is also from Analects 16.1. (2) At three places the translation keeps the Wikisource reading although the Siku quanshu text and a third transcription (the Sibu congkan text) read differently: “upset the funds for the frontier” (they read “worry over the frontier’s expenses”), “Even if it is hard to put on armor” (“even granting that they must put on armor”), and “when the branch flourishes, the substance is diminished” (“the root is diminished”). It is a new translation, not an adaptation of the one in de Bary and Bloom, Sources of Chinese Tradition, or of Esson Gale’s 1931 translation. Square brackets mark words added for clarity, including the identification of “the Grand Secretary” as Sang Hongyang. Passages from the two chapters are printed in the order of the book; [. . .] marks omitted exchanges and the omitted end of the final speech.
Document Text

From Chapter 1, “Basic Discussion” (Ben yi)

In the sixth year of the Shiyuan era [81 B.C.E.], an imperial edict directed the chancellor and the Grand Secretary to speak with the “worthy and good” men and the literati whom the provinces had recommended, and to ask about the sufferings and hardships of the common people.

The literati answered: “We have heard that the way to govern people is to block the source of excess and dissipation, to widen the beginnings of moral virtue, to curb the profits of the branch [trade and crafts] and open the way for benevolence and righteousness, and never to show people profit. Only then can moral teaching flourish and the customs of the people be changed. Now the commanderies and kingdoms have monopolies on salt, iron, and liquor, and the ‘equal transport’ system, which compete with the people for profit. They scatter the people’s honest simplicity and create a culture of greed and meanness. As a result, those among the common people who turn to the root [farming] are few, and those who rush after the branch are many. When ornament multiplies, substance declines; when the branch flourishes, the substance is diminished. When the branch is cultivated, the people become dissolute; when the root is cultivated, the people are sincere. When the people are sincere, goods and resources are sufficient; when the people are extravagant, hunger and cold arise. We ask that the monopolies on salt, iron, and liquor and the equal transport system be abolished, so as to advance the root and push back the branch and to enlarge the benefits of agriculture. That would be advantageous.”

The Grand Secretary [Sang Hongyang] said: “The Xiongnu have rebelled and will not be subjects, and again and again they have raided and plundered our borders. If we prepare against them, we wear out the soldiers of the central states; if we do not prepare, the raids and thefts will never stop. The late emperor pitied the border people, who had long suffered and had been bitterly harmed by being seized as captives by the enemy, and so he repaired the forts and barriers, readied the beacon fires, and stationed garrisons to guard against them. The frontier’s expenses were not covered, and so he set up the salt and iron monopolies, established the liquor monopoly, and instituted equal transport, to multiply goods and increase wealth in order to help pay the costs of the frontier. Now those who discuss these matters want to abolish them. At home that would empty the stores of the treasuries; abroad it would leave nothing for the defenders. The men who guard the passes and man the walls would go hungry and cold on the frontier. How could we then supply them? To abolish them would not be advantageous.”

The literati said: “Confucius said: ‘Those who have a state or a family do not worry that [their people] are few but that [goods] are not evenly shared; they do not worry about poverty but about insecurity.’ Therefore the Son of Heaven does not speak of more or less, the lords do not speak of benefit and harm, and the grand masters do not speak of gain and loss. They store up benevolence and righteousness and so influence the people; they spread virtuous conduct and so win their hearts. In this way those who are near are attached to them and those who are far away submit gladly. So one who is good at conquering does not fight; one who is good at fighting does not use armies; one who is good at using armies does not draw up battle lines. If he cultivates [virtue] in the temple hall, he can turn aside the enemy’s thrust and bring the army home. A true king practices humane government and has no enemy under Heaven. Why spend so much?”

The Grand Secretary said: “The Xiongnu are fierce and cunning. They enter our passes as they please, attack the central states, and kill and attack the officers of our commanderies and counties and the commandant of Shuofang [a northern frontier commandery]. They are truly rebellious and lawless, and they should have been punished long ago. His Majesty extends great kindness: he pities the common people who are still not supplied and cannot bear to leave his officers exposed in the open fields. Even if it is hard to put on armor and take up sharp weapons, there remains the aim of turning north to take revenge on the Xiongnu. Yet you also want to abolish the salt, iron, and equal transport [measures], to upset the funds for the frontier and damage military planning, with no concern for the frontier. That, on principle, is not advantageous.”

The literati said: “In ancient times people honored virtue and looked down on the use of soldiers. Confucius said: ‘If people from far away do not submit, cultivate culture and virtue to draw them in. Once they have come, give them peace.’ Now you abandon the Way and virtue and rely on armor and weapons. You raise armies and attack, post garrisons to guard, expose your troops in the open to hold out for years, and move grain and supplies without end, so that the soldiers at the frontier go hungry and cold outside while the common people suffer toil within. To set up the salt and iron monopolies and begin enlarging the offices of profit in order to supply [all this] is no long-term plan. Therefore to abolish them would be advantageous.”

From Chapter 7, “Against Shang Yang” (Fei Yang)

The Grand Secretary said: “In the past, when Lord Shang was chancellor of Qin, at home he set up laws and standards, made punishments severe, and tightened administration and instruction, so that cheats and liars had no place to hide. Beyond that he created profits a hundredfold and collected the taxes of the mountains and marshes. The state became rich and the people strong; weapons and equipment were complete and in good order; there were stores to spare. So Qin could attack enemies and conquer states, push back its rivals and extend its borders, and the armies were supplied without any levy on the common people. The profits it used were never exhausted and the people did not know it; its land reached the Western River [the west bank of the Yellow River] and the people did not suffer. The profits of salt and iron likewise help the people in emergencies, meet the costs of the armies, and build up stores against want; they supply a great deal, benefit the state, and harm no one. Why should the common people suffer, and what are the literati worried about?”

The literati said: “In the time of Emperor Wen [r. 180–157 B.C.E.] there was no profit from salt and iron and the people were rich. Now there is, and the common people are in want. We have not seen the benefit of the profit; we have seen only the harm. Moreover, profit does not come down from Heaven or come out of the earth; it is all taken from the people, and to call it ‘a hundredfold’ is a mistake in calculation. It is no different from a fool who wears his fur coat inside out and carries firewood on his back, loving the fur and not realizing that the hide is wearing away. When the plum and the apricot bear heavily, next year’s crop declines; when the new grain ripens, the old stores shrink. Not even Heaven and Earth can fill both sides; how much less can human affairs? So what benefits one side must drain the other, just as yin and yang do not shine together and day and night differ in length. Shang Yang made the laws harsh and enlarged profit, and the people of Qin could not make a living; together they wept for Duke Xiao [of Qin]. Wu Qi [a reforming minister of Chu] enlarged the army and took territory, and the people of Chu were thrown into turmoil; together they wept for King Dao [of Chu]. Afterwards Chu grew more endangered each day and Qin weaker each day. Profit piled up and so did resentment; the land widened and disaster was built. Where is there any ‘profit that is used and never exhausted while the people do not know it,’ or ‘land that reached the Western River while the people did not suffer’? Now the policies of Shang Yang are applied at home and the troops of Wu Qi abroad; travelers toil on the roads and those who stay at home are in want; old mothers cry out and resentful women sigh. Even if the literati wished not to worry, could they?”

The Grand Secretary said: “Qin employed Lord Shang, and the state became rich and strong; afterwards it finally annexed the six states and completed the imperial enterprise. In the time of the Second Emperor, wicked ministers seized the power to decide, the public way was no longer followed, the lords rebelled and fell away, and the ancestral temples were destroyed. [. . .] To blame Shang Yang for what Zhao Gao did to destroy Qin is like blaming Yi Yin for the disorder Chong Hu brought upon Yin [the Shang dynasty].”

The literati said: “One who is good at cutting mortises fits the joint so tightly all around that it cannot be pulled out; one who is good at laying foundations can build to a great height without falling. Yi Yin took the Way of Yao and Shun as the foundation of the Yin state; his descendants succeeded to the throne and it was not broken for a hundred generations. Shang Yang took heavy punishments and harsh laws as the foundation of the Qin state, and so after two reigns it was taken from them. Punishments were already severe; he also made the law of mutual liability, invented the crime of slander, and increased mutilating punishments, so that the common people shook with fear and did not know where to put their hands and feet. Taxes and levies were already heavy and frequent; he also shut off the resources of the mountains and marshes outside and set up profits a hundredfold within, so that the people had no way to open their mouths and speak. He honored profit and made little of righteousness, exalted force and prized achievement. It is not that he did not widen the land and add territory; but it was like a man sick from water who adds more water and makes his illness worse. They know that he opened the imperial enterprise for Qin; they do not know that he also brought Qin onto the road to ruin. [. . .]”
