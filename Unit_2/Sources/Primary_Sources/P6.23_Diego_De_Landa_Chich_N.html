Diego de Landa on Chichén Itzá, Mayapan, and Maya Books
written ca. 1566 (surviving copy later and abridged); traditions of Chichén Itzá (ca. 750–1100 C.E.) and Mayapan (ca. 1200–1450 C.E.)
 
TOC FIT Civilizations of Mesoamerica: Maya (textbook pp. 238–239).
WHY HERE A Spanish friar in sixteenth-century Yucatán records Maya traditions about Chichén Itzá and Mayapan, describes how Maya priests made and taught books, and reports burning those books.

Historical Context

Diego de Landa (1524–1579) was a Franciscan friar who arrived in Yucatán in 1549, soon after the Spanish conquest of the peninsula, and learned the Yucatec Maya language. In 1562, as head of the Franciscans there, he led a violent campaign against what he called idolatry: Maya people were tortured and imprisoned, some died, and at Maní he burned Maya books and religious images. Called to Spain to answer for these actions, he wrote this account there about 1566, from his own observations and from what Maya nobles told him of their lineages; he later returned as bishop of Yucatán. The text survives only in an abridged copy made after his death, whose title calls it “taken from what Father Fray Diego de Landa wrote.” He is a hostile witness, who calls Maya writings the devil’s falsehoods, and also the source of much of what is known about Maya life at the time of the conquest.

Landa says nothing about the southern lowland kingdoms that failed between about 750 and 950 C.E. He writes about the north: Chichén Itzá, which flourished from about 750 to after 1000, and Mayapan, which archaeologists date to about 1200–1450 and which, by his informants’ count, was abandoned about 120 years before he wrote. What his account shows is the Maya world that came after the southern collapse: walled cities, ruling lineages (Cocom, Xiu, Chel), a priesthood that kept and taught screenfold books, and new provinces founded after Mayapan fell, whose families were still feuding in his day. Separate what Landa saw (ruins, the sacred well, worn inscribed stones in Mayapan’s plaza, the books he burned) from what he was told centuries after the events (the stories of Cuculcan and of Mayapan’s founding and fall). Cuculcan, “feathered serpent,” is the Maya name of the god called Quetzalcoatl in Nahuatl; whether a real ruler bore the name is debated, and the numbers of years in these traditions should not be read as exact.

Document Text

§ V (end): Chicheniza

The second most important buildings are those of Tikoch and Chicheniza, which will be drawn later. Chicheniza is a very good site, ten leagues from Yzamal and eleven from Valladolid, where they say that three lords who were brothers reigned, who came to that land from the west. They were very religious, and so they built very handsome temples and lived very chastely without women; and one of them died or went away, on account of which the others became partisan and dishonorable, and for this they were killed. We will draw the picture of the largest building later, and we will describe the well into which they threw living men in sacrifice, and other precious things. It is more than seven estados [fathoms] deep to the water and more than a hundred feet across, made round in a cut rock, which is a marvel; and the water looks green, which they say is caused by the grove that surrounds it.

§ VI: Of Cuculcan and the building of Mayapan

It is the opinion among the Indians that with the Izaes who settled Chicheniza there reigned a great lord named Cuculcan, and the principal building, which is called Cuculcan, shows that this is true. And they say that he entered from the west, and they differ as to whether he entered before or after the Izaes, or with them; and they say that he was well disposed, and that he had no wife or children, and that after his return he was held in Mexico to be one of their gods and was called Cezalcouati; and that in Yucatán they also held him to be a god because he was a great statesman, and that this was seen in the order that he established in Yucatán after the death of the lords, to calm the dissension that their deaths had caused in the land.

This Cuculcan went on to settle another city, arranging with the native lords of the land that he and they should come there, and that all affairs and business should come there; and for this they chose a very good site eight leagues farther inland than where Merida is now, and fifteen or sixteen from the sea. There they enclosed it with a very wide wall of dry stone, about half a quarter of a league [around], leaving only two narrow gates, and the wall not very high; and in the middle of this enclosure they built their temples, and the largest, which is like the one at Chicheniza, they called Cuculcan; [. . .] and inside this enclosure they built houses for the lords alone, among whom they divided all the land, giving towns to each one according to the antiquity of his lineage and his personal standing. And Cuculcan gave the city a name, not his own, as the Ahizaes had done at Chicheniza, which means the Well of the Aizaes, but he called it Mayapan, which means the Banner of the Maya, because they call the language of the land Maya; and the Indians call it Ychpa, which means Within the Walls. [. . .]

§ VII: Government, priests, sciences, and books

After Cuculcan had departed, the lords agreed, so that the commonwealth would last, that the house of the Cocomes should hold the chief command, because it was the most ancient or the richest, or because the one who headed it then was the man of greatest worth. [. . .]

The people of Yucatán were as attentive to matters of religion as to those of government, and they had a high priest whom they called Ahkin-Mai, and by another name Ahau-Can-Mai, which means the Priest Mai or the High Priest Mai. He was much revered by the lords; he had no allotment of Indians, but besides the offerings the lords gave him presents, and all the priests of the towns contributed to him. His sons and nearest relatives succeeded him in the office, and in him was the key of their sciences, and these were what they worked at most; and they gave counsel to the lords and answers to their questions. [. . .] He provided priests for the towns when they were lacking, examining them in their sciences and ceremonies; he charged them with the duties of their office and with [setting] a good example for the people, and he provided them with their books and sent them out. And these attended to the service of the temples and to teaching their sciences and writing books about them. [. . .]

The sciences that they taught were the count of the years, months, and days, the festivals and ceremonies, the administration of their sacraments, the fateful days and times, their ways of divining and their prophecies, events, and remedies for illnesses, and antiquities, and reading and writing with their books and characters, with which they wrote, and with figures that signified the writings.

They wrote their books on a long sheet folded in pleats, which closed up entirely between two boards that they made very handsome; and they wrote on both sides in columns, following the folds; and they made this paper from the roots of a tree, and gave it a white gloss on which one could write well. And some principal lords knew these sciences out of interest, and for this they were more esteemed, although they did not use them in public.

§ VIII: The tyranny of Cocom and the fall of Mayapan

[. . .] The governor Cocom began to covet riches, and for this he negotiated with the garrison troops that the kings of Mexico kept in Tabasco and Xicalango [to say] that he would hand the city over to them; and so he brought Mexican people to Mayapan, and oppressed the poor and made many slaves, and the lords would have killed him if they had not feared the Mexicans. And the lord of the Tutuxios never consented to this. And the people of Yucatán, finding themselves in this situation, learned the art of arms from the Mexicans, and so they became masters of the bow and arrow, and of the lance and hatchet, and their shields and strong jackets of salt and cotton, and other equipment of war. [. . .]

Among the successors of the house of Cocom there was one who was very proud, an imitator of Cocom, and he made another league with the people of Tabasco and brought more Mexicans into the city, and began to tyrannize and to make slaves of the common people. For this reason the lords joined the party of Tutuxiu, who was a great statesman like his forebears, and they agreed to kill Cocom; and so they did, killing also all his sons, leaving only one who was away; and they sacked his house and took from him the estates of cacao and other fruits that he had, saying that they were repaying themselves for what he had robbed from them. And the factional strife between the Cocomes, who said they had been unjustly driven out, and the Xiuis lasted so long that, after having been in that city more than five hundred years, they abandoned and depopulated it, each going to his own land.

§ IX: After Mayapan

By the Indians’ count it will be one hundred and twenty years since Mayapan was depopulated. In the plaza of that city there are seven or eight stones, ten feet long each, round on one side, well worked, which have some lines of the characters that they use, and which cannot be read because they are worn by the water; but they think that it is a memorial of the founding and destruction of that city. [. . .]

The chief thing that these lords who abandoned Mayapan took to their lands was the books of their sciences, because they were always very subject to the counsel of their priests, and that is why there are so many temples in those provinces.

The son of Cocom, who escaped death because he was away on his trading business in the land of Ulua, which is beyond the town of Salamanca, when he learned of his father’s death and the ruin of the city, came very quickly, and he gathered his relatives and vassals and settled a place that he called Tibulon, which means “We were tricked”; and they built many other towns in those forests, and many families descended from these Cocomes; and the province where this lord rules is called Zututa. [. . .]

Among these three houses of principal lords, which were the Cocomes, Xiuies, and Cheles, there were great factional quarrels and enmities, and there are still today, though they are Christians.

[. . .]

§ XLI: Maya writing and the burning of the books

This people also used certain characters or letters, with which they wrote in their books their ancient matters and their sciences; and with these, and with figures, and some signs in the figures, they understood their affairs, and made them understood, and taught them. We found a great number of books in these letters of theirs, and because they had nothing in which there was not superstition and falsehoods of the devil, we burned them all, which they felt to an extraordinary degree, and which caused them grief.
