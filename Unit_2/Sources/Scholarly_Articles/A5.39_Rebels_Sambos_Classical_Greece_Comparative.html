Selection Overview

Cartledge uses comparison with slavery in the American South to ask why the chattel slaves of classical Greece never revolted while the Helots of Sparta did. He argues that the Helots' ethnic and political solidarity and numbers made revolt possible, while the absence of chattel-slave revolts was "not therefore a sign that they were mostly happy" but "a mark of the success with which their owners conducted a conscious and unremitting class struggle against them," a struggle that combined force with paternalism and other factors.

The following, recent quotation may serve as an appropriate starting-point: It is not my intention to give anything like a complete account, even in outline, of slavery in the ancient Greek world . . . But I think I ought at least to explain why at Athens and in the other Greek cities where slavery was already highly developed in the Classical period we never hear of slave revolts . . . The reason is simple and obvious: the slaves in each city (and even in many cases within single families and farms and workshops) were largely imported 'barbarians' and very heterogeneous in character, coming from areas as far apart as Thrace, South Russia, Lydia and Caria and other parts of Asia Minor, Egypt, Libya and Sicily, and sharing no common language or culture.
