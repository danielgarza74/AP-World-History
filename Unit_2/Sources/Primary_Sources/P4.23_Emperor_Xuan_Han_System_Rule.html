Emperor Xuan on the Han System of Rule, with Related Passages from the Han shu
Han shu compiled by Ban Gu, ca. 58–92 C.E.; the exchange between Emperor Xuan and his heir took place after 54 B.C.E.; related passages on 141–33 B.C.E.
 
TOC FIT China and the Search for Order: The Legalist Answer and The Confucian Answer; Han government (textbook pp. 144–146).
WHY HERE A Han emperor, as the official history reports him, describes how his dynasty combined law and punishment with the Confucian ideal of rule by virtue, set beside a record of what happened when his Confucian-minded son tried to change course.
TREATMENT Translated for this reader by the editor from the Chinese text (Wikisource), selected passages from Han shu chapters 8, 9, and 89.
ESSAY PROMPTS 4.2, 4.5
ARGUMENT ROLES 4.2.3 Supports; 4.2.2 Qualifies; 4.5.1 Supports
Historical Context

The Han shu (History of the Former Han) was compiled by Ban Gu (32–92 C.E.), a court historian of the restored Han dynasty, from about 58 C.E. until his death; his sister Ban Zhao completed parts of it. Chapter 8 is the annals of Emperor Xuan (r. 74–48 B.C.E.), chapter 9 those of his son Emperor Yuan (r. 48–33 B.C.E.), and chapter 89 a group of biographies of “upright officials.” Xuan, a great-grandson of Emperor Wu, grew up among commoners after his family was destroyed in a court purge; as emperor he ruled strictly and relied on officials trained in law. Yuan, raised on the Classics, gave Confucian scholars the highest offices. The exchange between father and son opens Yuan’s annals; it must have taken place after the execution of Yang Yun in 54 B.C.E.

Ban Gu wrote a century and more after these events, and the emperor’s words are a remembered conversation that he placed at the start of Yuan’s reign; his appraisal of Yuan says that “the achievement of Emperor Xiaoxuan declined,” so the scene also foreshadows that judgment. Ban Gu was himself a classically trained scholar, yet he praises Xuan for making “rewards reliable and punishments certain,” a phrase also found in the Legalist Han Feizi. The entries on the salt and iron offices are bare records from the annals, which makes them evidence of what the court did and not only of what people said. Separate what Xuan is reported to have said about how Han rule worked from what the record shows happened when a ruler tried to govern by Confucian advice.

Editorial note. This is a new translation prepared for this reader by the editor from the Chinese text of Ban Gu’s Han shu as transcribed on Chinese Wikisource (without commentary): chapter 9, “Yuan di ji” 元帝紀 (the opening, the edict of the fourth month of Chuyuan 5 [44 B.C.E.], the entry for winter of Yongguang 3 [41 B.C.E.], and the appraisal); chapter 8, “Xuan di ji” 宣帝紀 (the appraisal); and chapter 89, “Xunli zhuan” 循吏傳 (the preface). It has not been checked by a specialist. Headings and the bracketed summaries that introduce some passages are the editor’s; other square brackets mark words added for clarity. Omitted, and marked [. . .]: in the edict of 44 B.C.E., its opening, in which the emperor blames himself for famine and disease and quotes the Classic of Poetry, part of the list of offices abolished (palaces in the Shanglin park, the clothing offices in Qi, the farm-land office at Beijia), and the gifts and rules that follow; the rest of Xuan’s appraisal (on crafts and on the submission of the Xiongnu); the opening of Yuan’s appraisal (Ban Gu’s family source and Yuan’s skill in script and music); and from the preface to chapter 89, its first and third paragraphs (on the early Han and on Emperor Zhao), Xuan’s youth and his saying about good governors, and the list of governors who brought prosperity. The Wikisource text has typographic slips, read here as 蓋寬饒 (for 盍寬饒) and 純任 (for 純住). Uncertain readings: “punishments and names” (刑名) can also be read as a general term for law and penal administration; “the way of the hegemon” and “the way of the king” (霸王道) are glossed in brackets with their usual sense; “did not turn his back on [the heir]” (終不背焉) could refer to the Xu family; “generous to those below him” (寬弘盡下) could mean that he let his ministers speak fully; “observed how they meant to proceed” (觀其所繇) could mean “observed their background”; and “governor of the capital region” renders 內史.
Document Text

1. Emperor Xuan and his heir (Han shu 9, opening of the “Annals of Emperor Yuan”)

Emperor Xiaoyuan [Emperor Yuan, r. 48–33 B.C.E.] was the heir apparent of Emperor Xuan [r. 74–48 B.C.E.]. His mother was Empress Xu, [posthumously titled] Gong’ai; he was born among the common people when Emperor Xuan was still of humble station. When he was two years old, Emperor Xuan took the throne; at eight he was made heir apparent. When he grew up he was gentle and humane and fond of the Confucians. He saw that many of those Emperor Xuan employed were officials skilled in documents and law, who held those below them to the line by “punishments and names” [xingming, the Legalist method of holding officials to their titles and words], and that high ministers such as Yang Yun and Gai Kuanrao had been convicted for words of criticism and mockery and put to death. Once, attending [his father] at a banquet, he remarked at his ease: “Your Majesty applies punishments too severely. You should employ Confucian scholars.”

Emperor Xuan’s face changed, and he said: “The house of Han has its own system. At root it mixes the way of the hegemon with the way of the king [rule by force and law with rule by virtue]. How could we rely purely on moral teaching and use the government of Zhou? Besides, run-of-the-mill Confucians do not understand what suits the times. They like to approve of the ancient and condemn the present, and they confuse people about names and realities so that they do not know what to hold to. How are they fit to be entrusted with office?” Then he sighed and said: “The one who will bring disorder to my house is the heir apparent!”

From this time he kept his distance from the heir apparent and favored the King of Huaiyang, saying: “The King of Huaiyang is clear-sighted and fond of law; he is fit to be my son.” And the king’s mother, the Favored Beauty Zhang, was especially in favor. The emperor had it in mind to put the King of Huaiyang in the heir apparent’s place, but because in his youth he had depended on the Xu family, and they had risen together from humble beginnings, in the end he did not turn his back on [the heir].

2. Emperor Yuan abolishes and then restores the salt and iron offices (Han shu 9, 44 and 41 B.C.E.)

[In the summer of 44 B.C.E., after a comet appeared, the emperor issued an edict blaming himself for famine and disease in the east and ordering:] [. . .] “Abolish the wrestling shows, [. . .] the salt and iron offices, and the ever-normal granaries. Set no fixed number for the students of the Erudites, so as to widen [the ranks of] the learned. [. . .]” More than seventy provisions on punishment were reduced. [. . .]

[In 41 B.C.E.:] In winter, the salt and iron offices and the fixed number of students of the Erudites were restored, because expenditures were insufficient: many of the people had been exempted [from service], and there was nothing to supply labor service at the capital and in the provinces.

3. Ban Gu’s appraisals of Emperor Xuan and Emperor Yuan (Han shu 8 and 9)

[On Emperor Xuan:] The government of Emperor Xiaoxuan made rewards reliable and punishments certain, and checked names against realities. Men skilled in administration, in letters and learning, and in the principles of law all perfected their abilities. [. . .]

[On Emperor Yuan:] [. . .] In his youth he was fond of the Confucians, and when he came to the throne he summoned Confucian scholars to office and entrusted government to them; Gong [Yu], Xue [Guangde], Wei [Xuancheng], and Kuang [Heng] served in turn as chief ministers. But the emperor was held back by [the letter of] texts and their meanings, and he was easygoing and indecisive, so the achievement of Emperor Xiaoxuan declined. Yet he was broad-minded and generous to those below him, out of respectfulness and frugality, and his commands were mild and refined, with the manner of the ancients.

4. From the preface to the “Biographies of Upright Officials” (Han shu 89)

[. . .] In the age of Emperor Xiaowu [r. 141–87 B.C.E.], abroad [the state] drove back the barbarians of the four directions, and at home it changed the laws and standards; the people were worn down and impoverished, and wrongdoing could not be stopped. At that time few could be praised for governing by moral transformation. Only Dong Zhongshu, chancellor of [the kingdom of] Jiangdu, and Gongsun Hong and Ni Kuan, [each] governor of the capital region, had official careers worth recording. All three were Confucians who understood the affairs of the world and were well versed in documents and law, and they used classical learning to adorn the business of officials; the Son of Heaven valued them. [. . .]

When it came to Emperor Xiaoxuan, [. . .] after Huo Guang died [68 B.C.E.], he began to handle the myriad affairs of state himself and threw his energy into governing. When he appointed regional inspectors, governors, and [kingdom] chancellors, he always saw and questioned them in person and observed how they meant to proceed; afterward he examined what they did to test it against their words, and if name and reality did not match, he was sure to find out why. [. . .] Therefore the good officials of the Han were at their most numerous at this time, and it was called a restoration. Men such as Zhao Guanghan, Han Yanshou, Yin Wenggui, Yan Yannian, and Zhang Chang all lived up to their positions, but they relied on punishments, and some were convicted and executed. [. . .]
