Selection Overview

Sharma argues that from Gupta times land grants to brahmanas, which handed over revenues, police powers, and forced labor, "paved the way for the rise of brahmana feudatories" and broke up the centralized state, while declining trade, scarce coins, and self-sufficient local economies completed an Indian form of feudalism. He stresses its differences from Europe, noting that free peasants still seem to have outnumbered those under intermediaries, and calls his conclusions "tentative."

SHARMA (University of Patna) I It is very difficult precisely to define the term feudalism, which is attributed to stages of historical development far removed from one another in time and place, such as the Interregnum (2475-z260 B.C.) after the Old Kingdom in Egypt and the Chou period in China (C. 22 zz- C. z2o B.C.); but generally it is applied to society in mediaeval Europe, from the 5th to the 15th century A.D. It is therefore in the light of certain broad features of feudalism in the last sense that we will investi- gate the origins of feudalism in India. From the post-Maurya period, and especially from Gupta times, cer- tain political and administrative developments tended to feudalise the state apparatus. The most striking development was the practice of making land grants to the brahmainas, a custom which was sanctified by the injunctions laid down in the Dbarmaidstras, the didactic portions of the Epic, and the Purdnas; the Anusdsana Parva of the Mahbbhdrata devotes a whole chapter to the praise of making gifts of land (bbhlmi- ddna-praiazmsd). The early Pili texts of the pre-Maurya period refer to the villages granted to the brdhmanas by the rulers of Kosala and Magadha, but they do not mention the abandonment of any administrative rights by the donors.
