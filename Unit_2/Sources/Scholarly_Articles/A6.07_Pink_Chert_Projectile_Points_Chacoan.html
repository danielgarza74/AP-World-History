Selection Overview

Cameron uses chipped-stone evidence from the Chaco Project to test new models of the Chacoan regional system. Pink Narbona Pass chert was carried 75 km from the Chuska Mountains yet used mostly for simple flake tools and dumped in great-house trash mounds, which she reads as support for "periodic communal gatherings" at the great houses with ceremonial deposits tied to Puebloan ideas of renewal; the modest volume of chert suggests that, if it was part of a staple-finance system, "it was not a major part."

The most unusual characteristic of the chipped stone assemblage at Chaco is that it includes significant quantities of nonlocal material. Although these mate- rials were imported into the canyon from a consid- erable distance (more than 70 km), they were used almost exclusively for informal flake tools. Over the past 20 years, our understanding of the Chacoan Regional System has changed consider- ably as a result of new field research, artifact analy- ses, and a vast new body of interpretative studies relating to Chaco. The conference to which this paper was contributed provided an exciting opportunity to examine chipped stone recovered during Chaco Pro- ject excavations within the framework of the new models of the organization of production offered by conference participants Timothy Earle, Melissa Hagstrum, Peter Peregrine, and Colin Renfrew (this issue). The usefulness of these models is evaluated here using chipped-stone data. Chipped stone found in Chaco Canyon was, for the most part, gathered from sources within 5-20 km of the canyon. Throughout most of the occupation of the canyon, nonlocal materials make up only a small fraction of the assemblage. As with other arti- fact classes, during the Chacoan Era (A.D. 900-1150) some chipped-stone materials seem to have been imported into the canyon from consider- able distances, especially Narbona Pass chert from the Chuska Mountains (Color Photo 7, p. 68). The distribution of this material at sites in the canyon sup- ports the suggestion made by Earle, Peregrine, Ren- frew, and others (this issue) (Judge 1989; Loose and Lyons 1976; Toll 1985) of periodic communal gath- erings at great houses. Nonlocal chipped-stone mate- rial was not manufactured into tools for export to the surrounding region, nor is there evidence of the work of craft specialists, except perhaps religious spe- cialists with skills in stone tool production. At least some of the nonlocal bulk material seems to have been deposited in great house trash mounds along with food and utilitarian pottery, as part of ceremo- nial events. The total volume of imported chipped stone is lower than other imported goods like ceramics (Toll, this issue) and roof beams (Windes, this issue), mak- ing its role in a system of staple finance supporting a corporate chiefdom (as proposed by Earle and Pere- grine, this issue) more difficult to interpret. The vast numbers of pots and roof beams imported to Chaco would have required organization above that of the household, while the more modest quantities of chipped stone suggest no such organization. It is pos- sible, however, that the unusual beauty and distinct source of the imports (especially Narbona Pass chert) may have given them uncommon value as offerings in the religious system envisioned by Renfrew (this issue). Puebloan beliefs concerning the recycling of domestic goods may also have resulted in the depo- sition of this material in the large, artificial mounds ("trash middens") found at great houses. Projectile points, in addition to their usual function as hunting tools, may also have been a special class of artifacts that were placed as ritual caches especially at Pueblo Bonito (Color Photo 1, p. 65), and possibly Pueblo Alto (Earle, this issue:Figure 1), providing further support for Renfrew's suggestion that Chaco Canyon was a location of high devotional expression. The Sample Chipped stone was recovered from 16 sites that were excavated or tested by the Chaco Project (Table 1; see also Cameron 1997:Table 3.6). These materials were analyzed using procedures described in Cameron (1997). Lekson's study of projectile points, knives, and drills from Chaco also included formal tools from collections that were made prior to the Chaco Project (1997:659-660). Chaco Project sites include components that range from the Basketmaker III period (A.D. 500-700) to the Pueblo III period after collapse of the Chacoan System (A.D. 1150-1300). The period from A.D. 900-1150 is emphasized here because the goal of this paper is to understand the organization of chipped-stone pro- duction in Chaco Canyon during the time when the Chacoan Regional System was operating. When the Chaco Research Project began in the early 1970s, the research design called for excavation of a sample of sites from each time period, beginning with the earliest sites and ending with the monumen- tal great houses. During the first years of the project, few deposits were screened, limiting recovery of chipped stone and other small artifacts and making comparisons between early sites (those excavated first) and later sites (excavated toward the end of the pro- ject) more difficult. Furthermore, while the selection of sites for excavation was intended to provide a chronological sequence of occupation in the canyon, not all site types and time periods are represented.
