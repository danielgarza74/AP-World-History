Rainfall Records from a Yucatan Lake and a Belize Cave
Climate records covering the Classic Maya period; data published 1995–2012
 
TOC FIT Civilizations of Mesoamerica: Maya (textbook p. 239).
WHY HERE Two natural records of past moisture, one from the northern lowlands and one from the south, that scientists compare with the end of Classic Maya courts.
TREATMENT Editor-prepared charts from two data sets archived by NOAA (no license stated; cited as NOAA asks), with short quotations from NOAA’s documentation. This is modern scientific data, not an ancient document.

Historical Context

Between about 750 and 950 C.E., the royal courts of the southern Maya lowlands stopped carving dated monuments and many large cities were abandoned. Historians debate why. One long-discussed factor is drought, and climate scientists have tried to measure past rainfall from natural archives. Two of the best known are shown here. Lake Chichancanab lies in the northern lowlands of the Yucatan Peninsula; its mud holds the shells of small animals whose oxygen chemistry (the ratio of heavy to light oxygen, called δ18O) changes with the lake’s water balance. Yok Balum Cave, in southern Belize, near the area of the collapse, holds a stalagmite whose layers record rainfall year by year.

In records of this kind, higher δ18O values in lake shells are generally read as drier conditions (more evaporation), and, in tropical cave records, lower values are generally read as wetter. The authors of each study explain their own calibration, and the original publications should be consulted for it. The data contributors link the lake record to drought between 800 and 1000 C.E. The stalagmite’s authors connect their record to political events dated from monuments: they describe unusually high rainfall between 440 and 660 C.E., drying afterward, and an extended drought between 1020 and 1100 C.E.

Before you rely on the charts, notice what they cannot show. They measure moisture, not harvests or kings. The two sites are far apart, in different environments, and their age scales have uncertainties. A drought can be a cause of political change, a stress that weakened courts already at war, or only a coincidence of timing. A museum timeline (The Met’s Heilbrunn Timeline) also reports that the northern Maya centers were unaffected when the dynasties of the central region collapsed, so a drought record from the north does not map neatly onto the southern collapse.

Editorial note. The charts were prepared by the editor for this reader from NOAA’s archived data files, selecting 400–1300 C.E. for the lake chart and 300–1300 C.E. for the cave chart. In the lake chart every dated sample in that window is plotted as measured for two of the species the contributors report, and a line is broken wherever a value is missing; in the cave chart the thin line is the annual series and the heavy line is a 21-year running mean calculated for this reader. No values were altered. The 800–1000 C.E. shading marks the interval named in NOAA’s readme for the lake data; it is not an interpretation added by the editor. Quotations from NOAA’s documentation are verbatim. The journal articles themselves (Nature 1995; Science 2012) are copyrighted and are cited but not reproduced. PRIMARY SOURCE DATA (CHARTS)
Chart 1. Oxygen-isotope values (δ18O) in gastropod and ostracod shells from Lake Chichancanab, northern Yucatan, plotted against the calendar age assigned to each sediment sample, 400–1300 C.E. Data: Hodell, Curtis, and Brenner, as archived by NOAA. There are 48 dated samples in this window, unevenly spaced; Physocypria values are missing for 12 of them (including every sample from 789 to 891) and Pyrgophorus for one (871), so the lines are broken there. The units are per mil, as in the NOAA file, which does not state the reference standard.
Chart 2. Oxygen-isotope values (δ18O, per mil VPDB) of stalagmite YOK-I, Yok Balum Cave, Belize, 300–1300 C.E. Data: Kennett et al., as archived by NOAA. The annual series is shown with a 21-year running mean.
FROM THE DATA PROVIDERS’ DOCUMENTATION

Lake Chichancanab (NOAA readme): “Oxygen isotope and sediment composition data from Lake Chichancanab, Mexico. The data are interpreted as a moisture balance proxy, and indicate drought conditions in the years 800-1000 AD, corresponding to the collapse of the Classic Maya civilization.”

Yok Balum Cave (NOAA study notes): “Over 4,200 d18O and d13C measurements were performed on the upper 415 mm of the YOK-I stalagmite dated between 40 BC and AD 2006.” “The available data indicate that fluctuations in rainfall intensity are reflected in the d18O record.”
