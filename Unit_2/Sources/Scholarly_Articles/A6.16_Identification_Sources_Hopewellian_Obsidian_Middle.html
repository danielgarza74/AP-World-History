Selection Overview

Griffin, Gordus, and Wright use neutron activation analysis of trace elements to show that the obsidian found in Ohio and other Middle Western Hopewell mounds came from two sources in Yellowstone Park, one of them Obsidian Cliff, and they find no evidence that central Mexican obsidian ever reached the region before modern collecting. They stress that obsidian appears in the Middle West only during part of the Hopewell period and that Ohio and the Upper Mississippi Valley used it differently.

AMER I CA N ANT I QUITY VOLUME 34 JANUARY, 1969 NUMBER 1 IDENTIFICATION OF THE SOURCES OF HOPEWELLIAN OBSIDIAN IN THE MIDDLE WEST JAMES B. GRIFFIN, A. A. GORDUS, AND G. A. WRIGHT ABSTRACT One hundred and twenty years ago, the first obsidian implements were reported from Hopewellian mounds by Squier and Davis (1848). Since that time, a number of regions have been suggested as the source area: Alaska, the Pacific Coast, Yellowstone National Park, New Mex- ico, and central Mexico. Neutron activation analysis of the elemental composition of Hopewellian obsidian indi- cates two separate element groups. One of these, the 150 Group, has its source at Obsidian Cliff in Yellowstone. The second, the 90 Group, is also located in Yellowstone, but the exact flow has not yet been discovered. JN THE first Contribution to Knowledge of lthe Smithsonian Institution, Squier and Davis (1848) referred to the obsidian in Ohio Hope- well mounds as "a subject of wonder" and asked "Whence was this singular product obtained?" It is now possible to say with reasonable certainty that the obsidian from the Ohio mounds and from related sites in the Middle West is from two sources in Yellowstone Park. This iden- tification has been made in the last three years and followed a comprehensive summary of the distribution and nature of Middle Western Hopewellian obsidian by Griffin (1965). A program of neutron activation analysis of trace elements in archaeological materials was initiated at the University of Michigan by J. B. Griffin early in 1964, with the necessary and essential analytical responsibility being assumed by A. A. Gordus of the Chemistry Department. The initial support was provided by the Phoenix Project Grant which was in operation for two years. Since that time a more active and ex- panded program has been supported by the National Science Foundation Grant No. GS- 1196. Our first efforts were concentrated on an attempt to identify centers of ceramic manufac- ture and the possible trade and distribution activities connected with those centers. Efforts along these lines suggested that limestone-tem- pered Hopewellian pottery was made in the lower Illinois Valley and that some of it was then traded or carried into central and northern Illinois and western Michigan, as archaeologists have long believed on stylistic grounds. Other results suggested that the Late Woodland Pul- cher limestone-tempered ceramic complex in the Cahokia, Illinois, and Missouri area near St. Louis was made of a considerable variety of clays and was not made only at the Pulcher site near Dupo, Illinois. The Pulcher series of pottery types is one of the ingredients of the ancestry of the Old Village complex at Cahokia. Our initial results on some Mesoamerican ceramics sug- gested that Culhuacan Black-on-orange and Teotihuacan Thin Orange from the Valley of Mexico were also made from a variety of clays. The ceramic study was handicapped, however, because we did not have samples of local clay sources to compare with the finished products. For this reason we temporarily suspended work on the pottery. Interest in obsidian was stimulated by Griffin's paper referred to above and by the fact that a program of analyses of Hopewell obsidians at the Argonne National Laboratory was not being actively pursued. This program was by Dr. A. M. Friedman of Argonne and by George I. Quimby, then of the Field Museum of Natural History. The value of obsidian studies had been demonstrated by Cann and Renfrew (1964) in identifying obsidian sources in the Mediterranean basin and the Near East by the study of trace elements in samples by optical spectrography. To begin the study we had obsidian specimens collected by Griffin and others from village sites and mounds in Illinois, and then we received extremely valuable cooperation from Gregory Perino of the Gilcrease Foundation, Stuart Struever, and from other archaeologists in the Middle West. We also had access to Hopewell obsidian in the Field Museum, the Ohio State Museum, U.S. National Museum, the Mil- waukee Public Museum, and other institutional and private sources. We have been able to obtain source material from 44 locations in the western United States, 4 in Mesoamerica, 2 from South America, and 2 from Alaska. We also have many source specimens from the Old World including Iceland, Teneriffe, and Ascension Is- lands, so that any possible prehistoric travelers by boat across the Atlantic bringing obsidian to the New World can be identified. METHOD OF ANALYSIS In order to relate obsidian artifacts to par- ticular sources, characteristics must be deter- mined which serve to identify one, and only one, natural obsidian flow. The obsidian flow must be homogeneous within a certain stated range of variation for the investigated properties, and the same set of characteristics must not be shared quantitatively and qualitatively by any other flow. Cann and Renfrew (1964) initially consid- ered six different properties of appearance for Mediterranean obsidians: color in transmitted light, color in reflected light, fracture, translu- cency, transparency with internal structure, and luster (cf. also Renfrew and others 1965, 1966). After an examination of several hundred speci- mens, Renfrew and his associates (1965: 234) concluded for Mediterranean obsidians that "any attribution on the grounds of appearance would be very unwise." The same statement will hold equally well for the New World obsidian samples examined by us. For example, obsidian from a single flow at Obsidian Cliff in Yellowstone National Park ranges from clear, to gray, to black in color, with areal variations from red to brown. Thus, while "green" obsidian specimens from archaeological sites in Mexico may be generally shown by ele- mental analysis to have originated from the Cerro de las Navajas in the state of Hidalgo, "black" and "gray" specimens from the sites in the same area could originate from a number of separate flows, or even the same flow. Color in obsidian is a difficult attribute with which to work and should be used for source identifica- tion of artifacts with extreme caution. Studies of opaqueness and translucency on obsidian samples from western North American sources and archaeological sites on the north- western Plains demonstrated that these are not valid criteria for characterization purposes. "In other words, the same source can produce both opaque and translucent obsidian" (Frison and others 1968). The type of analysis which has produced the most positive results is a consideration of the chemical composition of the samples. Between 80% and 90% of the element composition of a typical obsidian sample may be attributed to SiO.2 and Al2 03. Other major constituents include K20, Na2O, Fe2O3, and FeO. In addi- tion, more than two dozen trace elements have been observed in detectable quantities (Cann and Renfrew 1964; Gordus and others 1968). A number of techniques have been utilized for determining element contents of obsidian. Renfrew and his associates have utilized optical spectrography. Green and his colleagues (1967) have analyzed New Zealand obsidian by emis- sion spectroscopy. Parks and Tieh (1966) have achieved satisfactory results with X-ray spectro- scopy on obsidian samples from three different regions in California. Our method, neutron activation analysis, has been used successfully on obsidian from both the Near East (Wright, Gordus, and Benedict 1968; Wright and Gordus 1968) and North America (Wright, Griffin, and Gordus 1968; Frison and others 1968). The particular form of activation analysis devised by Gordus for the analysis of obsidian has been discussed in detail elsewhere (Gordus and others 1968; Gordus, Wright, and Griffin 1968). The samples are irradiated with thermal neutrons for 40 seconds. The initial activity dur- ing the first 30 to 60 minutes is due mainly to 28Al. However, the knowledge of the Al con- tent (and Si and 0) is not particularly useful for characterizing obsidians, as it does not vary greatly between flows. Nearly all of the 28Al activity is dissipated in about one hour. During the period of 2 to 24 hours following the irradia- tion, over 99% of the observed activity is due to 24Na and 56Mn. The remaining activity is due mainly to 42K, with very small amounts of 140La, 31Si, and 46SC. Analysis for the Na and Mn contents is per- formed using a 2 in. X 2 in. well NaI (T1) detector coupled to a single channel analyser (Baird-Atomic Co., Model 530 Spectrometer). The entire procedure is automated, and a total of 100 obsidian samples and chemical standards are analyzed together. A series of measurements of the activity is taken over a period of 22 hours.
