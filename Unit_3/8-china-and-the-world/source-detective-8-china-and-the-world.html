Part 1

Background Reading

China and Its Neighbors, 600–1300

After the Han dynasty collapsed around 220 C.E., China broke apart for more than three centuries. Then the Sui (581–618), Tang (618–907), and Song (960–1279) dynasties put the country back together. They built a government of ministries, inspectors, and civil service examinations that lasted for a thousand years, and under the Song a boom in farming, industry, and trade made China the richest, most skilled, and most populous country on earth. Educated Chinese called their land the “middle kingdom” and looked down on outsiders as “barbarians,” yet they also believed that outsiders could become civilized by learning Chinese ways. That belief shaped how China dealt with everyone around it.

To manage foreign rulers, China used the tribute system. Delegations came to the emperor’s court, performed the kowtow, a series of ritual bowings, and presented gifts. In return the emperor allowed trade and handed back “bestowals” that were often worth more than the tribute. On paper, the foreign ruler became a respectful junior. In practice, China could not always dictate the terms. The Uighurs traded poor horses for huge quantities of Tang silk. After the Tang fell, the Khitan and then the Jurchen conquered parts of northern China and made the Song pay silk, silver, and tea every year, payments that Chinese officials described as gifts to the barbarians but that really bought peace. Influence moved both ways: the founders of the Sui and Tang had mixed nomad and Chinese ancestry, and Tang elites enjoyed steppe music, clothing, and food.

Korea, Vietnam, and Japan borrowed heavily from China, including writing, Confucian ideas, Buddhism, and methods of government, but each did so in its own way. China ruled Vietnam’s Red River valley directly for more than a thousand years, until 939 C.E., which produced a Chinese-educated elite and repeated rebellions. After winning independence, Vietnam kept sending tribute and kept a Chinese-style government, yet it held on to its own language, a larger role for women, and a “southern script” for writing Vietnamese. Japan lay across open sea and was never conquered, so its borrowing was voluntary and selective. Around 600, the aristocrat Shotoku Taishi sent monks, scholars, and students to China and tried to build a Chinese-style state. Japan took Chinese court rituals, a calendar, law codes, and capitals modeled on Chang’an, but it kept its own gods, and produced great literature in Japanese, much of it by women. Elite Japanese women also escaped strict Confucian rules, such as the ban on widows remarrying, that pressed harder on women in Korea.

Chinese papermaking, printing, and gunpowder spread across Eurasia, but China received as much as it gave. Cotton and sugar came from India, and a fast-ripening rice from Vietnam fed a growing south. Buddhism is the biggest example. It arrived from India and at first had little appeal, but after the Han collapse it spread among rulers and ordinary people, and translators recast it in Chinese terms. Its tax-free monasteries grew rich, and critics called the religion a foreign “state within a state.” After a rebellion led by a general of foreign origin in the 750s, suspicion of outsiders grew. In 819 a Confucian official named Han Yu attacked the emperor for honoring a relic of the Buddha, and between 841 and 845 the government closed thousands of monasteries and sent about 260,000 monks and nuns back to tax-paying life. Buddhism survived by blending into Chinese popular religion. Because giving and receiving were so tangled together, the chapter’s author argues that sharp lines between “internal” and “external” sources of change in China’s history are misleading.
