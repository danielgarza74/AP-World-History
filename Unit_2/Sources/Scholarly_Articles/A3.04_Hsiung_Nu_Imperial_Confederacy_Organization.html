Selection Overview

Barfield argues that the Xiongnu (Hsiung-nu) state arose not from internal developments on the steppe but from its relationship with Han China: it was an "imperial confederacy," autocratic in foreign affairs but consultative at home, whose ruler (the shanyu) financed the state by raiding China and then extorting trade and subsidies. He concludes that the Xiongnu "had no need or desire to conquer China," since their policy aimed at "exploiting the Han state from a distance," and that later steppe empires succeeded when they followed this model.

VOL. XLI, No. 1 JOURNAL OF ASIAN STUDIES NOVEMBER 1981 The Hsiung-nu Imperial Confederacy: Organization and Foreign Policy THOMAS J. BARFIELD T he emergence of nomadic states in Inner Asia seems to be built on contradictions. At the top of a nomadic empire there is an organized state led by an autocrat, but most of the tribesmen within the nomadic polity seem to retain their traditional political organization, which is based on kinship groups of various sizes-lineages, clans, tribes. In the economic sphere there is a similar paradox-a state based on an economy that is both extensive and relatively undifferentiated. Two sets of theories have been offered to resolve these contradictions. Based on his observations among the Kazakh and Kirghiz in the nineteenth century, Radloff saw political organization among nomads as the replication of local politics at ever-higher levels of incorporation. The basic herding unit was at the core of both pastoral production and politics. Differences in wealth and power within these small groups enabled certain men to claim leadership positions; they regulated conflict within the group and organized it for defense or aggression against external enemies. Radloff saw the growth of larger units as an attempt by ambitious strongmen to combine ever-larger numbers of nomads under their control. This could eventually result in a nomadic empire, but the power of a steppe autocrat was purely personal. It stemmed from his own skillful manipulation of force and wealth within an elaborate tribal network. Such a ruler was an usurper of power, and at his death his personal empire dissolved back into its component parts (Radloff 1893, 1: 513-17). Barthold accepted Radloff's analysis, but, using the evidence of the Orkhon inscription that detailed the rise of the Second Turkish Empire in the seventh century A.D., he argued that a nomadic leader could also be the popular choice of a political movement within a nomadic society. He stressed the importance of choice as a complement to coercion in creating a nomadic state (Barthold 1935: 11-13). Both Radloff and Barthold stressed that nomadic states were inherently ephemeral, the state organization disap- pearing with the death of its founder. In their view the nomadic state only temporarily dominated a tribal political organization that remained the basis for social and economic life on the steppe. In 1952 Harmatta rejected this explanation, arguing that it was not possible for a state to emerge while a tribal political organization remained intact. A nomadic state could only emerge from a process in which the tribal basis of a nomadic society was first destroyed and then replaced by class relations. Krader went a step further, claiming that because the state could not exist without class relations-direct producers supporting nonproducers-the historical existence of nomadic states presupposed such relations (1979: 221-34). In this set of theories the paradox of the state being based on a tribal organization was resolved by positing that tribal organizations were destroyed in creating the state, even if this new relationship was disguised by the use of the old tribal terminology. If these states lacked stability, it was because their resource base on the steppe was too narrow for any degree of permanency. There are problems with both sets of theories. They have in common the assumption that the creation of a nomadic state was the result of internal develop- ment. Yet historically known nomadic states were organized at a level of complexity far beyond the needs of simple nomadic pastoralism. Radloff and Barthold stress the nomadic state's ephemeral nature, but many steppe empires long outlived their founders, notably those of the Hsiung-nu, the Turks, and the Uighurs. Finally, although nomadic aristocracies were common, there is little evidence to support the claim that strong class relations developed on the steppe in the premodern period. Krader and Harmatta are forced to argue from the basis of the theoretical necessity of class relations, but they fail to provide any convincing proof of how such relations existed in a relatively undifferentiated and extensive nomadic pastoral economy. A potential answer to this dilemma has emerged from comparisons of recent anthropological research in Africa and southwestern Asia. It throws doubt on the assumption that nomadic states were the result of any sort of internal dynamics. In a comparative study of African nomadic pastoralists, Burnham concluded that low population density and ease of geographic mobility made the indigenous develop- ment of any institutionalized hierarchy in such societies improbable. Under these conditions, Burnham found, segmentary opposition would provide the most advan- tageous model for political organization. The development of the state among nomads, therefore, was not a response to internal needs. Rather, it developed when nomads were forced to deal with more highly organized sedentary state societies (Burnham 1979: 349-60). Using cases from southwestern Asia, Irons also came to the same conclusion and reduced it to a hypothesis: Among pastoral nomadic societies hierarchical political institutions are generated only by external relations with state societies and never develop purely as a result of internal dynamics of such societies. (1979: 362) This argument has a number of far-reaching implications for the understanding of nomadic states in Inner Asia. It is not a diffusionist explanation. The nomads did not "borrow" the state from their neighbors; rather, they were forced to develop their own peculiar form of state organization in order to deal effectively with their larger and more highly organized sedentary neighbors. These relations required a far higher level of organization than was necessary to handle livestock problems and political disputes within a nomadic society. It was no accident that the least formally organized nomads were found in Africa where they encountered few state societies, and that the most formally organized nomadic states emerged facing China, the world's largest and most complex traditional sedentary state. It was this relationship between China and the steppe that supported a state hierarchy among Inner Asian nomads.
