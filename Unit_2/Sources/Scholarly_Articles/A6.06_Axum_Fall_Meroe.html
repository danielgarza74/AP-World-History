Selection Overview

Burstein re-reads the fragmentary Greek victory inscription of a pagan Aksumite king found at Meroe and argues that it records not a sack of Meroe but a political settlement in which a Meroitic king kept his throne as a tributary vassal of Aksum. He concludes, tentatively, that Ezana's mid-fourth-century raid was one incident in "a relatively extended period of Axumite influence in the upper Nile Valley" and may have been "the crushing of an unsuccessful Meroitic rebellion against Axumite suzerainty."

Axum and the Fall of Meroe* Stanley M. Burstein In accounts of the fall of Meroe a major role is usually assigned to the devastating invasion of Meroitic territory mounted about the middle of the fourth century a.d. by Ezana, the first Christian king of Axum,1 an invasion that may, according to an attractive suggestion by Kirwan,2 have ex- tended as far north as Kawa. A vivid account of this important event is preserved in the great GeDez inscription set up by Ezana to commemorate his victory over the Noba in which he relates how, after defeating the Noba, he "arrived at the Kasu," that is, Kushites, "slaying some of them and taking others prisoner at the junction of the rivers Seda and Takkaze," i.e., of the Nile and Atbara. "And on the day after my arrival I dispatched into the field" troops "up the Seda against their towns of masonry and of straw; their towns of masonry are called Aiwa, I, Daro, I. And they slew and took prisoners and threw them into the water . . . And I erected a throne at the junction of the rivers Seda and Takkaze, opposite the town of masonry which is on this peninsula."3 Although identification of the places men- tioned remained controversial, particularly the identification of Aiwa with Meroe,4 until recently most scholars would have agreed with A. J.Arkell's view that the inscription indicated that "Meroe town was sacked and the kingdom destroyed by an army sent by Ezana, who probably used the excuse of some border incident ... to put an end to a trade rival whose increasing weakness, poverty, and inefficiency had left her at his mercy."5 Serious objections to this reconstruction of the end of the Meroitic monarchy were first raised by P. L. Shinnie in 1955,6 who argued that at the time of Ezana's invasion the Meroitic monarchy no longer existed, it having been overthrown by the Noba decades earlier when they had occupied the island of Meroe. This interpretation, how- ever, cannot be correct, since it ignores the distinction made by Ezana in his GeDez inscription between settlements of the Noba, towns of the Kasu and towns of the Kasu "which the Noba had taken," a distinction which clearly implies that, although the Noba had occupied some Meroitic territory, a significant portion still remained under Meroitic control in the mid-fourth century.7 While Shinnie's overall interpretation of the end of the Meroitic monarchy must, there- fore, be rejected, his observation that already prior to his invasion Ezana was King of Kasu has led more recently to the proposal of a radically different and seemingly better founded assessment of the significance of Ezana's actions and of the role played by Axum in the fall of Meroe in general. According to this theory, it was not Ezana, but one of his pagan predecessors who overthrew the kingdom of Meroe.8 He, instead, only completed the work of destruc- tion begun by that unnamed Axumite king whose conquest of Meroe is thought to be commemorated in SEG, 24, 1246, a frag- mentary Greek victory inscription, dated probably to the 21st or 24th year of an Axumite king, discovered by Sayce at Meroe, published by him in 1909, and re-edited by 47

Thus, it is highly doubtful that Begrawiyah North 25, the last Meroitic royal pyramid, can be dated as late as the mid-fourth century, the probable date of Ezana's raid.13 In addition, although Heli- odorus,14 the only source to comment directly on relations between Meroe and Axum, indicates that they were friendly in the early third century, the potential for conflict certainly existed by the late third or early fourth century. By then the Axumite king responsible for the Adulis inscription copied by Cosmas Indicopleustes in the sixth century and thus preserved to us had extended his kingdom to the borders of Meroitic territory15 and subdued some of the Beja tribes of the eastern deserts, thereby opening a caravan route from Axum to Egypt that avoided the Nile valley entirely and hence could only harm Meroitic trade.16 Indeed, the very fact that an Axumite king could set up a victory inscription at Meroe, that is, in the capital of another kingdom, is itself strongly suggestive of conquest and subsequent rule, a conclusion that is sup- ported by Ezana's use of the title King of Kasu prior to his campaign against the Noba17 and by his failure to mention a Meroitic king in his GeDez inscription celebrating his victories in that campaign. Finally, a new and important piece of evidence was provided by the announcement at the Fourth International Conference of Meroitic Studies in Berlin by Professor Thomas Hagg of the University of Bergen of the discovery at Meroe of a second monu- ment commemorating a victory by a pagan Axumite king, this time a fragment from the dedicatory inscription of a throne found by Professor Peter Shinnie during the 1975/ 1976 excavation season.18 Paradoxically, however, while this new inscription greatly strengthens the case for Axumite sovereignty over Meroe prior to the reign of Ezana, it also requires that the interpretation of SEG, 24, 1246 as commemorative of the conquest of Meroe be reconsidered, since its content and epigraphical style suggest that it refers to events prior to those dealt with in that inscription. Elucidation of SEG, 24, 1246 has long been frustrated by the lack of a reliable text, a deficiency that unfortunately still remains unremedied despite the recent publication of two new editions of it, and by its extremely fragmentary character. Of its fourteen lines, there is preserved complete not one sentence, clause or even phrase. Fortunately, however, its basic structure can be discerned. After the royal titulary, lines 2 through 4 refer to a dispute of some sort and the military opera- tions connected with it and include an allusion to the pillaging of a number of settlements. A change of subject and locale is then marked at line 5 by the phrase parelthon enth[auta] or enth[ade], "arriving here."19 Allusions follow to the movements of a basileus or king, to generals and children, the imposition of tribute and the erection of a bronze statue, and, most importantly, in line 11 to a declaration of future obligations by a now unidentifiable speaker.20 These items, however, and, in particular, the remains of a declaration of future obligations and the reference to tribute suffice to indicate that this portion of the inscription dealt with the political settlement imposed on the area by the King of Axum. In addition, they also permit answers to be given to two key questions: first,whether or not the military campaign alluded to in lines 2 through 4 was directed at Meroe, and, second, the identity of the king mentioned in line 7. The answer to the firstquestion is provided by Altheim and Stiehl's establish- ment of the reading parelthon enth[auta] or enth[ade], "arriving here" in line 5 since "here" in the context of this inscription can only refer to Meroe, the place where it was displayed.
