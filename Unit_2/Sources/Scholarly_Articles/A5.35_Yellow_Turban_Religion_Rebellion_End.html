Selection Overview

Levy traces how a Taoist faith-healing movement led by the three Chang brothers grew into the Yellow Turban rebellion of 184, arguing that years of flood, drought, famine, and epidemic created "a dispossessed peasantry, ready to flock to the standards of anyone who offered to alleviate its misery." He sets the revolt against court factionalism and the rise of provincial generals and concludes that, although suppressed, the Yellow Turban revolts "did hasten the downfall of the Han and encouraged the fortunes of warlords."

INTRODUCTION THE WANING YEARS of the Later iEl:an dynasty were characterized by agrarian distress and inter- necine political conflict. Emperor Ling-ti,2 who reigned from 168 to 189, placed great confidence in his numerous palace eunuchs. They came to constitute an influential court faction, but were frequently opposed by a group of scholar-officials at the court. This internal struggle became more intense and bitter with each passing year. The preoccupation of the court with factional strife aided the ernergence into prominence of provincial generals who were sufflciently distant from the weakened throne to defy its authority. These gen- erals, in command of loyal provincial troops, ma- neuvered among themselves to gain control of the government. The divisive forces mentioned above, consisting both of those within the court and those independent of it, accelerated a gradual worsening of economic conditions throughout the empire. The deterioration of the economy was especially notable in the eastern provinces, for there the peasants suffered repeated agrarian disasters.3 Amidst these disturbances, a popular religious movement known as Neo-Taoism 4 arose and rap- idly spread. This late Han Taoism was comparable to nineteenth century Taiping Christianity in its scope and enthusiasm, and it indirectly led to re- bellion against the throne.
