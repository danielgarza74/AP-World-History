Teotihuacan in Aztec Memory: Sahagún’s Historia general
Spanish text ca. 1575–1577, from Nahuatl accounts gathered from about 1558; describes Teotihuacan (flourished ca. 100 B.C.E.–650 C.E.)
 
TOC FIT Civilizations of Mesoamerica: Teotihuacan (textbook pp. 240–241).
WHY HERE Nahua traditions written down in the sixteenth century that remember Teotihuacan as the place where the sun and moon were made and where lords were buried and became gods.
TREATMENT Translated for this reader by the editor from the Spanish text (Bustamante edition, México, 1829–1830; Internet Archive), selected passages from Books 7 and 10.

Historical Context

Bernardino de Sahagún (ca. 1499–1590) was a Franciscan friar who spent about sixty years as a missionary in New Spain. From about 1558 he had Nahua elders in Tepepulco, Tlatelolco, and Mexico City describe their gods, calendar, history, and customs; Nahua students he had trained wrote down their answers in Nahuatl, and Sahagún organized the material into twelve books, adding a Spanish version in the 1570s. He said he collected it so that missionaries could recognize and uproot the old religion, and in his note to the reader of Book 7 he warns that the elders gave their account of the heavens “in a low style” and that it was translated the same way. The Spanish text was first printed in 1829–1830 by Carlos María de Bustamante from a copy of the manuscript; this translation is made from his edition.

Teotihuacan flourished from about 100 B.C.E. to about 650 C.E. and was in ruins long before the Mexica (Aztecs) settled in the Basin of Mexico in the 1200s and 1300s, so Sahagún’s informants spoke roughly nine hundred years after the city fell and a thousand or more after its pyramids were built. Even the name Teotihuacan (“place of the gods”) is Nahuatl, and the names Pyramid of the Sun and Pyramid of the Moon come from this tradition. These passages are therefore direct evidence of how later Nahuas remembered and used the ruins: as the place where the gods burned themselves to make the sun and moon, and where lords were buried and became gods. As evidence of how Teotihuacan itself was ruled they are at most a distant tradition to be tested against archaeology, which has not identified a ruler’s tomb in either great pyramid (see P6.8 and P6.9). Separate what the text observes (mounds “still seen today,” the pits where stone was dug) from what it infers (that giants built them, that lords lie under them), and notice that Book 10 reports an Aztec ruler, Itzcoatl, ordering the old painted histories burned.

Editorial note. This is a new translation prepared for this reader by the editor from the Spanish text of Sahagún’s Historia general de las cosas de Nueva España as printed by Carlos María de Bustamante (México: Alejandro Valdés, 1829–1830): Book 7, chapter 2 (“De la luna”), vol. 2, pp. 246–250, and Book 10, chapter 29, paragraph 12 (“De los mexicanos”), vol. 3, pp. 140–142 and 144, read in the Internet Archive’s OCR text of the John Carter Brown Library copy. It has not been checked by a specialist. Omitted, and marked [. . .]: in Book 7, the opening of chapter 2 on the moon’s phases, the dressing of the two gods for the fire and their arrangement in two rows, the eagle and jaguar that followed them into it (the origin of the name for the eagle and jaguar warriors), the gods’ search for the place of sunrise and the sun’s first rising, the moon’s dimming with a rabbit, the god Xolotl’s flight from death, and the closing lines on eclipses; in Book 10, the start of the sentence on Tamoanchan, a comparison with the man-made hill of Cholula, and the stories of the Olmeca, the invention of pulque, and the Cuextecas (pp. 142–144). The OCR has many misreadings (for example “Teeuzistccatl,” “JYanaoatzin,” “hm este” for “en este”), corrected where the sense is clear. The edition spells the moon-god Tecuzistecatl and Tecuciztecatl, and the town Teutioacan and Teuhtioacan; one form of each is used, except where Book 10 explains the name. Nahuatl terms garbled by the OCR (tzaqualli, teutexcalli, quetzalli) are given in the forms the edition appears to print. Words in parentheses are glosses printed in the 1829–1830 edition, probably Bustamante’s (“the town of San Juan between Chiconauhtlan and Otumba,” “today Teotihuacan, six leagues north of Mexico”); square brackets are the editor’s. Bustamante’s footnotes are omitted, including one calling the story “a mythological account, like the metamorphoses of Ovid.” Uncertain readings: in Book 10 the printed text explains the name as “place where they made señales” (signs), while standard modern editions of the Spanish read señores (lords), which fits the preceding clause better; the page image was not checked. The gods’ question “¿hemos de vivir entre los villanos?” is rendered “among base people,” and “rechinar y respendar” as “crackle and sizzle”; both are approximate. El aire, which kills the gods, is rendered “the air”; the next sentence calls it el viento, “the wind.”
Document Text

Book 7, chapter 2 (“Of the moon”): the gods at Teutioacan

They said that before there was day in the world, the gods gathered in that place called Teutioacan (which is the town of San Juan between Chiconauhtlan and Otumba). They said to one another: “Gods, who will take charge of lighting the world?” Then, at these words, a god called Tecuciztecatl answered and said: “I take it upon myself to light the world.” Then the gods spoke again and said: “Who will be the other?” At once they looked at one another and discussed who the other would be, and none of them dared to offer himself for that office; all were afraid and excused themselves. One of the gods, of whom no account was taken and who was covered with sores, did not speak, but listened to what the other gods were saying. The others spoke to him and said: “You be the one who gives light, little sore-covered one.” And he willingly obeyed what they ordered him and answered: “I receive as a favor what you have ordered me; let it be so.” And then the two began to do penance for four days.

Then they lit a fire in the hearth, which was made in a rock that they now call teutexcalli. The god called Tecuciztecatl offered only precious things: instead of branches he offered rich feathers called quetzalli; instead of balls of hay he offered balls of gold; instead of maguey spines he offered spines made of precious stones; instead of bloodied spines he offered spines made of red coral; and the copal he offered was very good. The sore-covered one, who was called Nanaoatzin, instead of branches offered green reeds tied in threes, nine in all; he offered balls of hay and maguey spines, and he bloodied them with his own blood; and instead of copal he offered the scabs of his sores. For each of these two a tower like a hill was built; on these same hills they did penance for four nights, and these hills are now called tzaqualli; both are near the town of San Juan, which is called Teutioacan.

[. . .] When midnight came, all the gods placed themselves around the hearth called teutexcalli. [. . .] Then they spoke and said to Tecuciztecatl: “Come now, Tecuciztecatl! Go into the fire!” And he at once rushed to throw himself into it; and as the fire was large and burning fiercely, he felt the great heat, was afraid, did not dare to throw himself in, and turned back. Again he turned to throw himself into the blaze, forcing himself, and coming near, he stopped; he did not dare to hurl himself into the blaze. Four times he tried, but he never dared to throw himself in. It had been ordered that no one should try more than four times. When he had tried four times, the gods then spoke to Nanaoatzin and said to him: “Come now, Nanaoatzin! You try!” And when the gods had spoken to him, he summoned his strength and, closing his eyes, rushed forward and threw himself into the fire, and at once he began to crackle and sizzle in the fire, like someone roasting.

When Tecuciztecatl saw that he had thrown himself into the fire and was burning, he rushed forward and threw himself into the blaze. [. . .] After both had thrown themselves into the fire and had been burned, the gods sat down to wait for Nanaoatzin to come out soon. When they had waited a long time, the sky began to turn red, and the light of dawn appeared everywhere.

[. . .] And afterward the moon came out in the same part of the east, alongside the sun: first the sun came out, and after it the moon; in the same order in which they entered the fire they came out, made sun and moon. [. . .]

After both had come out above the earth, the sun and the moon stayed still without moving from one place; and the gods spoke to one another again and said: “How can we live? The sun does not move. Are we to live among base people? Let us all die, and let us make him come to life through our death.” And then the air took charge of killing all the gods, and killed them. [. . .] And they say that although the gods were dead, the sun did not move because of it; and then the wind began to whistle and to blow hard, and it made him move so that he would go on his way. And after the sun began to travel, the moon stayed still in the place where it was. After the sun, the moon began to move; in this way they separated from each other, and so they come out at different times: the sun lasts through the day, and the moon works at night, or gives light in it. From this, it seems, comes what is said, that Tecuciztecatl would have been the sun if he had thrown himself into the fire first, because he was named first and offered precious things in his penance.

[. . .]

Book 10, chapter 29, paragraph 12 (“Of the Mexicans”): Teotihuacan and its lords

[. . .] By this count it cannot be known how long they were in Tamoanchan; it used to be known from the paintings, which were burned in the time of the lord of Mexico called Itzcoatl, in whose time the lords and the principal men who were then living agreed and ordered that they all be burned, so that they would not come into the hands of the common people and be held in contempt.

From Tamoanchan they went to make sacrifices at the town called Teutioacan (today Teotihuacan, six leagues north of Mexico), where they made two hills in honor of the sun and of the moon; and in this town were chosen those who were to rule the others, for which reason it was called Teutioacan, which means Veitioacan, or place where they made señales [signs]. There also the principal men and lords were buried, over whose graves they ordered mounds of earth to be made, which are still seen today and look like little hills made by hand; and one can still see the pits from which they took the said stones or rocks of which the mounds were made. And those that they made to the sun and to the moon are like great hills built by hand, which seem to be natural and are not; and it even seems an incredible thing to assert that they are built by hand, and they certainly are, because those who made them then were giants. [. . .]

And the town was called Teotioacan, the town of Teutl, which is god, because the lords who were buried there were, after their death, canonized as gods, and they said that they did not die but woke from a dream that they had lived; which is why the ancients said that when men died they did not perish, but began to live anew, as if waking from a dream, and turned into spirits or gods; and so they said to them: “Lord, or Lady, wake up, for it is beginning to dawn; it is already daybreak, for the birds with yellow feathers are beginning to sing, the butterflies of many colors are already flying.” And when someone died, they used to say of him that he was now teutl, which means that he had died to be a spirit or god. And the ancients believed, deceiving themselves, that the lords when they died turned into gods, which they said so that those who ruled would be obeyed or feared; they also said that some turned into the sun, others into the moon, and others into various planets.

[. . .]

And since for a long time lordship and command had been held in Tamoanchan, afterward it passed to the town called Xumiltepec, where those who were lords and elders and priests of idols spoke to one another, saying that their god had told them that they were not to stay forever in the town of Xumiltepec, but were to go farther on. And so all of them, boys, old men, old women, women and men, began to travel, and they went little by little until they reached the town of Teutioacan, where those who were to rule and govern the others were chosen; and those who were chosen were the wise men and diviners, and those who knew the secrets of enchantments. When the election of the lords was done, they all set out from there at once, each of these going with the people of his own language, and each band guided by its god.
