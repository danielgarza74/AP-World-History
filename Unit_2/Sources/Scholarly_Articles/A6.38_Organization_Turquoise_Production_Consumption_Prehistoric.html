Selection Overview

Mathien reviews how turquoise was acquired, worked, and used at Chaco Canyon from about 500 to 1150 C.E., including the more than 56,000 pieces buried with two Pueblo Bonito burials. She finds that Chacoans could not have controlled the distant mines, that beads were made in ordinary households, and that turquoise's "primary function was as a ritual item within the regional system," used as kiva offerings and grave goods, while burial differences point to unequal access between lineages.

For example, Peregrine proposes that Chacoan society was transformed into a matrilocal one between A.D. 700-1000 and that women were the primary agricultural producers. Men were then free to carry out activities that took them away from home base, e.g., hunting, trading, and mining. Although a few turquoise objects appear in the A.D. 500s in Chaco, evidence for the production of turquoise items is sparse prior to the A.D. 900s when several sites reveal evidence ofjewelry making. This increase in activity indicates that Peregrine's model of a society in which men travel to turquoise sources in conjunction with other activities is feasible. Evidence indicates that control over acquisition and production of turquoise ornaments is probably not present in the Chacoan database. Renfrew (this issue) proposes that Chaco is a loca- tion of high devotional expression. The two major uses of turquoise, as offerings in kivas and as grave goods, suggest ritual use of these objects. Do the rich burials in Room 33 of Pueblo Bonito represent a spe- cial event or special people within a group rather than elite leaders buried with status symbols (the inter- pretation preferred by Akins and Schelberg 1984)? Peregrine (this issue) suggests this wealth of turquoise represents gifts from individuals through- out the polity to the dead leader(s) who fostered and maintained corporate bonds through a corporate strategy. Craniometric analyses by Akins (1986) and Schillaci et al. (2001) indicate that the burials accom- panied by turquoise in two sections of Pueblo Bonito belong to two distinct groups; the presence of both males and females in each of their samples suggests lineage burials. Thus, the human remains in Room 33 may include special people who were buried together with a wealth of ritual items in that and sur- rounding rooms. Turquoise appears in sites in Chaco Canyon dat- ing from the Basketmaker Ill period (A.D. 500-700). Pepper (1899, 1905, 1906, 1909, 1920) noticed a number of similarities between materials he exca- vated and modern Hopi and Zuni practices. This review of the acquisition of raw materials, the pro- duction of jewelry items, and the consumption of these ornaments will discuss data that show change through time for a longer period than that for which the models were devised and suggest considerable time depth for some historic puebloan practices. The Acquisition of Raw Materials Peregrine and Hagstrum (this issue) suggest that it is the men who leave the home base and travel throughout the region to hunt, procure lithic materi- als (including turquoise mining), and participate in stone crafts. In this section, I will focus on turquoise sources. Because the prehistoric Cerrillos mines are the best documented, and archaeological sites located nearby serve as a window into how these mines were Ltilized, the evidence from the Cerrillos Mining Dis- trict will be reviewed to evaluate the control exer- cised by any prehistoric puebloan group over this source area. Additionally, trace-element analysis per- formed to date indicates Chacoan turquoise may have originated in several mining areas, thus increas- ing the number of people needed and level of social organization required to control extraction at sources and transportation of turquoise to Chaco. Beginning with the earliest excavators, most researchers cited the prehistoric pits in the Cerrillos Hills as the probable source of turquoise found in archaeological sites in Chaco Canyon (Pepper 1909). This assumption was due, in part, to the limited knowledge of turquoise sources at that time. We now know there were numerous prehistoric turquoise sources located throughout the southwestern United States and northern Mexico (Harbottle and Weigand 1992; Mathien 1981, 1999; Weigand and Harbottle 1993). The Cerrillos Mining District, however, is the source closest to Chaco (approximately 200 km to the east); also published information on other turquoise sources is much less detailed. There are approximately ten prehistoric mining pits of various sizes and types in the Cerrillos Hills (Milford and Swick 1995; Warren and Mathien 1985); those most extensively mined were Mount Chalchihuitl in the south, and the Tiffany and Castil- lian mines in the north. To determine who used the mines in the southern area and when, Warren and Mathien (1985) tabulated the number of potsherds found at each mining area by type. The wares in their sample indicate that less than 1.4 percent of the ceramics date prior to the Pueblo El period, 20.9 per- cent date to the Pueblo 171 period, and 71.2 percent are glaze wares of Pueblo IV or later; the remaining 6.5 percent are of historic or unknown origin. These data suggest the heaviest mining took place between A.D. 1350 or 1375 and 1600, or after the collapse of the Chaco Phenomenon. Closer review of the earli- est pottery types indicates some of the people using the turquoise mines may have been Chacoans; but the ceramic types (e.g., Red Mesa Black-on-white) that tie the miners to Chaco are found across large areas, and there is no way to pinpoint a specific loca- tion for their homeland.
