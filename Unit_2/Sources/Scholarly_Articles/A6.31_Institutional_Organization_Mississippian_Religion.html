Selection Overview

Knight argues that the old idea of a single Mississippian "Southern Cult" should be replaced by a model of several coexisting religious institutions, each with its own family of sacred objects: a communal earth and fertility cult tied to the periodic renewal of platform mounds, a chiefly cult that served "to sanctify chiefly authority," and a priestly cult that mediated between them through mortuary ritual and ancestor veneration. Religion, he concludes, provided "the context of Mississippian political power."

THE INSTITUTIONAL ORGANIZATION OF MISSISSIPPIAN RELIGION Vernon James Knight, Jr. Symbolic objects for ceremonial display, or sacra, tend to be systematically related in their representational content to the cult institutions that produce and manipulate them. Cult organization is normally pluralistic among preliterate complex societies. Mississippian sacra suggest a triad of coexisting types of cult institution: (1) a communal cult type emphasizing earth/fertility and purification ritual, (2) a chiefly cult type serving to sanctify chiefly authority, and (3) a priestly cult type mediating between the other two, supervising mortuary ritual and ancestor veneration. The problem taken up here is the formulation of a new and productive mode of conceptualizing the social forms of Mississippian period (ca. A.D. 1000-1550) supernaturalism in the southeastern United States. It is now possible to go well beyond the long-entrenched concept of a Mississippian "Southern Cult" (Waring 1968a; Waring and Holder 1945) by exploring a fuller range of relevant phenomena than was comprehended by that classification, by avoiding certain inherent difficulties attending it, and by invoking a less simplistic frame of reference allowing for pluralism and spe- cialization of religious institutions (Brown 1976). SACRA AND CULT INSTITUTIONS The most fundamental question to be resolved in the study of this aspect of Mississippian social organization is: How can religious institutions be identified from the sacra associated with them? Sacra (Turner 1964) here denotes the totality of representational art, artifacts, and icons that by inference appear to have been charged with conventional supernatural meaning, in the context of ritual activity or display. A second key term of analysis is the cult institution, defined by Wallace (1966:75) as "a set of rituals all having the same general goal, all explicitly rationalized by a set of similar or related beliefs, and all supported by the same social group." Among other classifications of religious phenomena, this concept has the advantage of calling attention to the important religious aspects of social phenomena that are usually thought of as being primarily secular or political in nature. Thus, for example, the religious component of an institution of government is considered a cult institution, alongside other organizations whose purposes are more exclusively oriented to the supernatural. The concept emphasizes the character of a society's religion as reducible to an assemblage of such cults. Religious beliefs and practices in complex societies are always discriminated into more than one, and usually several, analytically discrete and relatively autonomous cult institutions. Even in societies possessing strongly centralized and influential religious bureaucracies, there are always a number of lesser cult institutions, of variable composition, which attend to different sorts of supernaturalism in a more or less complementary fashion (Wallace 1966:75-77, 84-88). 676 AMERICAN ANTIQUITY [Vol. 51, No. 4, 1986 The term "cult institution" as applied here subsumes a variety of organizational types having religious content. They are encountered in ethnographic literature under many rubrics: ancestral cults, medicine societies, ritual fraternities, secret societies, fraternal orders, communal agricultural cults, specialized priesthoods, cults of nobility or divine rulership, lineage or clan linked ritual organizations, and churches. As regards the participants, these phenomena, of course, vary markedly on such criteria as the presence or absence of age grading, sex linkage, relation to descent or residence groups, relative exclusivity, and degree of internal hierarchy (Edmonson 1958:36-43). The miscel- lany of cults that together can be said to constitute the religion of a society can be distinguished on other accounts as well. Each cult claims, and can be defined by, its own exclusive corpus of rites. Each cult institution in turn possesses its own unique history and structural role (Wallace 1966:75, 84). These structural positions are not static. Cult institutions wax and wane in popularity, ascend and descend in the authority they impose, are born out of dissent or revitalization, and may eventually succumb. Cult pluralism commonly is formalized into a more comprehensive structure. Two predominant cults in a society, rather than being merely complementary in function, are sometimes seen to assume a true dialectical relationship. The overall structure therefore is a dyadic one, and the various symbolic oppositions that come into play in marking the relation are profitably analyzed according to canons of structuralist theory in the tradition of Durkheim and Levi-Strauss. The formal artic- ulation of two powerful and complementary cult organizations into a structure of dyadic type deserves close scrutiny because, as will be seen, it may be fundamental to Mississippian religious organization. A dualism of this kind may incorporate elements of an underlying ritual hostility between the paired cults, especially in the case of a popular or communal inclusive cult contraposed against an exclusive cult of nobility. This antagonism may be codified in the form of "rituals of rebellion" (Gluckman 1954; Norbeck 1961, 1963; Wallace 1966), which may involve sham battles or the temporary symbolic overthrow of nobility. The inherent potential for such antagonism underlies the need for intellectual reconciliation of varied sets of cult goals and premises that frequently are at odds. Eister (1974:73-74) maintains that the interpretation of religion as having a mainly inte- grative social function "is hard to accept for any complex society where there is a high degree of specialization and functional autonomy among institutions or where religion itself is organized on a pluralistic or quasi-pluralistic basis." But such dyadic religious structures may serve to channel and codify these mutual antitheses by allowing a calendrical alternation of ritual dominance, or some comparable form of cooperative enterprise. The pairing of a cult of nobility with a ritually antithetical cult institution associated with earth/ fertility symbolism is sufficiently distributed worldwide to be considered a valid general type (Turner 1969:99). Ritual and symbolic symmetry in these dyadic systems importantly contrasts with the political asymmetry of the same systems. Cults of nobility provide supernatural sanction to the political sway of an elite, particularly where such cults are directly connected with paramount political offices. This primacy attached to one member of the dyadic pair is necessarily at the expense of the institutional authority of the other in administrative affairs. ICONIC FAMILIES One further concept is required to establish a theoretical connection between sacra and the cult institutions they serve. This concept is the iconic family, the set of sacra particularly associated with a corresponding cult institution. In the archaeological situation the discovery and definition of iconic families among sacra is central to a project of identifying coexisting cults. Each cult institution, where cult pluralism prevails, is oriented to a unique set of goals or purposes. Each sponsors a distinct rite or group of rites, explicitly distinguished from others. Consequently, means tend to be elaborated for objectifying the symbolic distinctiveness of cults. Mutually exclusive rights to the display and interpretation of particular sacra among coexisting cults is expected, both as an overt manifestation of the symbolic independence of cult ritual and belief from that of other cults, and as a reflection of their structural complementarity. Discrete cult institutions in a society will tend to be associated with equally discrete subsets of ritual sacra.
