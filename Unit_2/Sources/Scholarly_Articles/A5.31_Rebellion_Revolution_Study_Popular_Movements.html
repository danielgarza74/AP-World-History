Selection Overview

Wakeman surveys the scholarship on Chinese popular movements, from bandits and millenarian sects to the Communist mobilization of peasants, and shows how the Maoist picture of history as "perennial class struggle" has been questioned as historians examine the sources for themselves (historiography). He concludes that China's traditional rebellions "may have acquired their greatest historical influence as mythical signposts toward a revolutionary social consciousness"; his evidence comes mainly from the Ming period onward.

VOL. XXXVI, No. 2 JOURNAL OF ASIAN STUDIES FEBRUARY 1977 Rebellion and Revolution: The Study of Popular Movements in Chinese History FREDERICK WAKEMAN, JR. URING the past twenty-five years, hundreds of studies of Chinese peasant rebel- lions have appeared in print. Most of these were published in the People's Republic of China, where they represented an effort to create a new revolutionary history of class struggle, intended to replace the elite history written by Confucian historiographers under the empire. Embodying Mao Tse-tung's belief that "the ruthless economic exploitations and political oppression of the peasantry by the landlord class forced the peasants to rise repeatedly in revolt against its rule" (Mao I96I, Vol. III, p. 75), these studies of popular uprisings describe a bewildering variety of social phenomena. As they evolved from the Han period to the twentieth century, varying from region to region of China, such mass movements ranged in kind from secret society conspiracies to chiliastic crusades, from urban workers' riots to rural tenants' jacquerie, from small garrison mutinies to rebellions of a magnitude "without parallel in world history" (Mao I96I, VOl. III, p. 76). Despite the variation in these individual forms of social disorder, all are now roughly grouped together under the single rubric of class struggle. This indiscrimination reflects the didactic purpose of histories written in China today. As the historical muse serves the revolution, so then does almost any kind of social disorder in the past supposedly provide an inspiration for class struggle in the present. Particular categories of rebellion thus merely illustrate the general ideal of protest. Distinctions between different forms of dissent grow blurred except for the clarity of each specific incident. A number of historians outside the People's Republic have also developed a strong interest in Chinese popular movements. Recognizing the unusual frequency of uprisings in traditional China and curious about social movements as such, Western scholars tried to build upon the strong foundation work completed earlier. However, as they drew upon Chinese scholarship to write histories of their own, they began to question the assump- tions that had inspired the original research. Indeed, after tapping primary sources for themselves, some Western historians came to doubt the conventional Maoist depiction of Chinese history as perennial class struggle. Others reaffirmed the contention that traditional Chinese rebellions represented class warfare in the past. Or would it be more accurate to say that, while the population suffered in times of epidemic or famine, there were long periods of plenty when relatively affluent farmers benefited from rising agricultural prices, and negotiated rental contracts to their own liking with accom- modating landlords? Any single scholar's answer to those questions reveals a particular understanding of the fundamental social and economic relationships between Chinese peasants and their superiors. If one believes, for instance, that Chinese peasants mainly tilled single-family farms leased from other rich peasants, then it is difficult to conceive of rebellions as purely class-conscious movements. On the other hand, it is much easier to speak of "peasant wars" if one subscribes to the view that pre-modern peasants formed a bound class of villeins, personally subordinated to "feudalistic" landlords. The Theory of Peafant Subordination The latter view, that the peasantry was subordinated after the Tang period and remained virtual serfs until the Qing period, is usually identified with the Tokyo School of Japanese sinology (Sudo '954; Niida I956). In its broadest outlines, this view has been brilliantly exposed to Western readers by Mark Elvin in his suggestive work The Pattern ofIthe Chinese Past (I973), where he argues that throughout the later Song, Yuan, and Ming dynasties, poor peasants were indeed bound as serfs to cultivate the Yangtze valley manors of the rich. Toward the end of the sixteenth century, however, serfs and poor tenants began to acquire a more cohesive class identity, due in part to increased social contact between them in the burgeoning market towns. This new consciousness helped provoke serf uprisings in the mid-i6oos, in which poorer peasants banded together to attack the villas of their masters, freeing household slaves and destroying tenant contracts (Fu I96I, pp. 68-I53; Tanaka I96I). The most important effect of the seventeenth-century revolts, according to Elvin, was the rapid decline of the manorial system and the eventual formation of a new economic basis for gentry rule. Frightened by the ferocity of their serfs' attacks, landlords abandoned their manors to invest in safer enterprises like pawnshops, or purchased more profitable urban real estate in place of farmland. By the middle Qing period the economic basis of the gentry had shifted from land to credit. Usury the control of financial resources had become "a more important source of social and economic power in the countryside than ownership of land" (Elvin I973, p. 250; see also Migdal I974, p. 39). The decline of the manors and the freeing of serfs did not necessarily mean an improvement of the peasants' lot. In Elvin's analysis, the peasants of the Qing period eventually did win a more independent social status as farmers, but they were still economically dependent upon the local moneylender. And because the former rural gentry were now either urban moneylenders or absentee rentiers, they "performed no function that the peasants regarded as essential for their way of life" (Moore I966, p. 205). According to Barrington Moore (I966, pp. 206-o8), institutions like the baojia (local control units) and xiangyue (village covenants) supposedly connected the upper classes and the peasantry; but this was only an artificial link between rulers and ruled a link "liable to snap under any severe strain," and thus accounting "in considerable measure for the fact that peasant rebellion was endemic in Chinese society."
