Bishops and Temples in the Eastern Roman Empire A.D. 320–435
Garth Fowden

JOURNAL Journal of Theological Studies 29, no. 1 (1978)
EXCERPT Original journal pages 53–54
JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/23960254
ESSAY PROMPTS 4.4, 4.5
ARGUMENT ROLES 4.4.3 Qualifies; 4.5.3 Supports
Selection Overview

Fowden examines how bishops in the eastern empire worked with, and often pushed ahead of, imperial officials in closing and destroying pagan temples between 320 and 435, when paganism stopped being the empire's dominant religion. He argues that Theodosius's laws gave the Church "by implication carte blanche," that enforcement remained "extremely hit and miss," and that bishops such as Porphyry of Gaza and Marcellus of Apamea drove the campaign, producing mass conversions "through violence rather than conviction."

BISHOPS AND TEMPLES IN THE EASTERN ROMAN EMPIRE A.D. 320-435* τ 'HE penultimate section (16. 10) of the Codex Theodosianus, entitled De paganis, sacrificiis et templis, is our fundamental source for the anti-pagan legislation of the fourth and early fifth centuries; between the years 320 and 435—■the dates of the first and last decrees in this section—paganism ceased to be the dominant re ligion of the empire and became the creed of an isolated minority.1 The relative speed with which the externals of the pagan cults were dis mantled should not however make us forgetful of the lag between the passing of a law and its execution, nor of the problems encountered by a policy which sought to transform the age-old spiritual and aesthetic landscape of the empire. An examination of the manner in which the Church collaborated with the civil authorities in the attack on the pagan temples and their cults can tell us much about the way in which the empire was ruled, and about the extent to which the government succeeded in preserving a formal neutrality until the pagan establish ment had been so far undermined that serious resistance to the final push was out of the question. The Codex Theodosianus tells us virtually nothing about the part played by bishops in the eradication of paganism. They are referred to for the first and only time in this connection in 407/8, when Arcadius, Honorius, and Theodosius issued a law stating that: 'It shall not be permitted at all to hold convivial banquets (convivia) in honour of sacrilegious rites in such funereal places (Junestioribus locis), or to cele brate any solemn ceremony. Of the twenty-five decrees in question, thirteen were addressed to a PraefectusPraefectus Praetorio,' and one to an agens vicem Praefectorum Praetorio.2 Four were addressed to the Praefectus Urbis Romae3 and five to parti cular provincial governors,4 while the remaining two have no addressee.5 In other words, the general supervision of the execution of imperial policy towards the pagans was entrusted to the Praetorian Prefect, and to him the emperor addressed the majority of his instructions, though special local situations could provoke a more direct imperial inter vention: C. Th. 16. 10. 17-18 are two surprisingly moderate decrees addressed to the Proconsul Africae in August 399, clearly in an attempt to conciliate the pagans of Africa, who had reacted violently to the destruction of the Carthaginian temples in March of the same year.6 The extent to which individual Praetorian Prefects involved them selves actively in the attack on paganism varied considerably. Naturally they tended to delegate responsibility; few were as active as Cynegius, who held the office of Praetorian Prefect of the East from 384 to 388, and devoted considerable efforts to the closure of temples throughout the eastern provinces.7 Even a pagan prefect such as Cynegius's successor Tatianus (388-92) could not hope to reverse entirely the trend of imperial policy; if Tatianus secured the exclusion of monks from towns, the law was soon reversed,8 and Theodosius's anti-pagan decrees of 391 also fell within his prefectship.9 Like all other officials, the Praeto rian Prefect's tenure of office was short, and he could not hope to evolve a long-term policy in the same way as an emperor or (as we shall see) a bishop. Nor could he ever be sure of the co-operation of his subordi nates, especially at the local level. The laws give us a certain amount of information about how and by whom (theoretically) they were to be implemented, but also leave us in no doubt that the whole process was extremely hit and miss.
